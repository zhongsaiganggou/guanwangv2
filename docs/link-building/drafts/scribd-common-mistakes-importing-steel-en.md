# 7 Common Mistakes When Importing Steel Structures from China (And How to Avoid Them)

**A practical buyer guide for overseas importers of prefabricated steel buildings**

Importing steel structures from China can be a reliable way to access structured manufacturing capacity and control project cost. Most problems are not caused by the product itself, but by gaps in information, specification, verification, and planning. This guide sets out the seven mistakes buyers make most often, with practical steps to avoid each one.

## Mistake 1: Incomplete Project Information Before Quoting

A short request such as "I need a 50 x 20 m steel warehouse, please quote" leaves too many open questions. Missing information produces vague quotes, repeated clarification rounds, and supplier assumptions that may not match the real project, so quotes cannot be compared on the same basis.

Steel structures are highly customised. The final design depends on drawings, loads, local code, building use, crane requirements, corrosion conditions, fire requirements, and transport constraints. A complete brief should include building dimensions and eave height; building use; country and site location; wind, snow and seismic data if known; crane capacity, span and height if applicable; roof and wall cladding and insulation; doors and windows; coating system and colour; design standard such as GB, AISC, EN, BS or AS/NZS; and delivery terms with destination port.

If drawings or a bill of quantities are available, send them. This is the most effective way to obtain an accurate, comparable quote. Using one standard project information template for every supplier keeps all quotations on the same basis.

## Mistake 2: Choosing a Supplier Based Only on the Lowest Price

In steel fabrication, an unusually low quote can hide thinner steel, a reduced coating system, weak welding, no quality inspection, incomplete scope such as missing fasteners or accessories, hidden extra costs, and delayed delivery from an overloaded production schedule. When material grade, coating thickness or bolt quantities do not match the specification, rework after delivery is far more expensive than the apparent saving.

Buyers should compare quotes on equal scope, request itemised pricing for steel weight, coating, fabrication, accessories and shipping, verify manufacturing capacity and delivery schedule, ask for references from similar projects, and consider total cost of ownership rather than purchase price alone. Warning signs include a quote well below every other offer, refusal to provide factory photos or a video tour, no described inspection process, vague specifications, pressure to decide immediately, and the absence of a written contract or proforma invoice.

## Mistake 3: Skipping Factory Verification and Quality Inspection

Photos alone do not verify a supplier, and defects discovered only after arrival are expensive to correct. Buyers can verify manufacturing bases through a physical visit, a live video tour showing equipment and production, a third-party audit by an inspection company, or references from previous customers in the same region.

Quality controls should include inspection during production and a final pre-shipment inspection, together with container loading supervision where needed. Key checks cover material mill test reports, welding quality and inspection records, coating dry film thickness, dimensional accuracy, completeness against the packing list, surface preparation, and component marking. Even when a site visit is not possible, a pre-shipment inspection is a prudent control compared with the cost of receiving non-conforming goods.

## Mistake 4: Unclear Coating and Surface Treatment Specifications

Coating is one of the main factors in the service life of a steel building, yet buyers sometimes leave it unspecified. Without a clear standard, the supplier may use a basic paint, skip surface preparation, leave thickness unverified, or select a system unsuitable for the environment. Missing RAL or Pantone references can also cause colour mismatch.

The specification should state the surface preparation standard, with Sa 2.5 near-white metal common for many projects; each coat including primer, intermediate and topcoat with type and dry film thickness; the minimum total dry film thickness; RAL or Pantone colour codes; the coating standard such as ISO 12944; and requirements for thickness reports and adhesion tests. The appropriate system depends on the corrosion environment: inland, coastal humid, industrial chemical, or extreme corrosion conditions may require different multi-coat systems, or galvanising with a topcoat. Coating thickness reports and material certificates should always be available.

## Mistake 5: Ignoring Packing, Marking and Container Loading Planning

Poor packing and loading cause component damage, missing parts, confused assembly, wasted container space, pieces that do not fit the container, and packing lists that do not match the cargo. Buyers should specify edge and corner protection, bundle organisation, moisture protection for ocean freight, and a stacking plan.

Marking should identify each piece with drawing number, position and size, label each bundle with contents and weight, and show orientation marks such as "this side up". Container planning should identify the container type, including open-top or flat rack for oversized pieces; provide a loading plan; distribute weight safely; secure cargo with lashing; and sequence loading for practical unloading and erection. Supporting documents include a container-specific packing list, a loading plan, a component location guide, and an assembly sequence guide.

## Mistake 6: Underestimating Import Documentation and Customs Requirements

Missing or incorrect documents can cause customs delays, storage and demurrage charges, penalties, inspection holds, and clearance problems. Typical documents include a commercial invoice with HS codes and terms, a detailed packing list, a bill of lading with correct consignee and notify party, a certificate of origin where applicable, material test reports, inspection certificates, insurance documents where terms require them, and technical drawings for classification and verification.

Conformity requirements vary by country and region, and buyers should confirm the local rules before ordering. Working with an experienced customs broker, verifying documents before shipment, and allowing buffer time in the project schedule reduce the risk of delay. Copies of shipping documents should be reviewed before the vessel arrives so the broker can prepare clearance in advance.

## Mistake 7: No Post-Delivery Technical Support or Installation Guidance

For a steel structure, delivery is the beginning of installation, not the end of the project. Without clear support, buyers may face installation errors, unclear erection drawings, unresolved missing or damaged parts, delays while the erection team learns the system, and safety issues from improper procedures.

Before ordering, buyers should confirm that detailed erection drawings, a step-by-step installation manual, a technical contact during installation, a missing-parts policy, warranty terms, and site supervision options where needed are all included. Useful support covers component location guides, bolt torque specifications, safety guidance, prompt responses for missing or damaged parts, and remote video support for complex assemblies. First-time buyers and complex projects may also consider factory training, an engineer on site for critical phases, or a local erection team experienced with exported steel structures.

## Final Buyer Checklist

Before placing an order, confirm that complete project information, drawings or BOQ, equal-scope quote comparison, factory verification, inspection arrangements, detailed coating specifications, packing and marking requirements, a container loading plan, documentation research, a document review process, technical support, warranty terms, payment and currency considerations, and schedule buffer are all in place.

## Scope and Responsibility

The exporter's scope covers engineering coordination and detailing, fabrication and quality control, marking, packing and container loading, export coordination, and installation technical guidance. The local side remains responsible for permits and registered engineer submissions, foundations and civil works, local labour and physical erection, and authority submissions.
