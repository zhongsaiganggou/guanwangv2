**Direct answer:** a labour camp is not one building. It is a group of small, repeated buildings — sleeping blocks, toilet and ablution blocks, a kitchen and dining hall, a laundry, a clinic, a store and a generator house — laid out around a yard. Steel is used because these blocks repeat, travel in containers and have to be standing before the main plant is finished. What drives the steel scope is the block plan and column grid, whether blocks are single or two-storey, the climate (heat, dust, sand, coastal salt), the fire and egress strategy for sleeping areas, and the order in which the camp will be occupied.

Most camp enquiries arrive as a headcount and a site plan. The headcount sets how many beds are needed; it says nothing about what the steel has to do. Re-planning four-bed rooms to two-bed rooms keeps the same frame and changes plumbing, partitions and egress width. A camp that grows from 400 to 900 beds changes block count, utility loads, walking distances and often the fire access road.

This guide sets out what the steel package covers in a labour camp, which decisions must be fixed before a frame can be priced, and where the boundary sits between the frame supplier and the local team.

## 1. What a labour camp needs from the steel frame

Four properties matter more than anything else in camp construction: repetition, transport, erection speed and the ability to be taken down or extended later. This is the same logic that drives most [steel building solutions](/en/solutions) for industrial sites.

- **Repetition.** One block type used twenty times costs less to detail, fabricate and erect than five bespoke designs. Standardising the grid is worth more than optimising any single block.
- **Transport.** Block length should let columns, rafters, purlins and panels pack into standard containers without a large share of air.
- **Erection speed.** Bolted frames with marked members, factory-fitted base plates and one braced bay can be erected by a small crew with a mobile crane.
- **Change of use.** Camps are extended, split or moved. A bolted, marked, documented frame keeps that option open; a site-welded frame usually does not.

What the frame does not solve matters as much. Steel provides no sanitation, privacy, cooling or egress layout; those come from the block plan, the fit-out and the local code.

**What is a labour camp steel structure made of?** Typically hot-rolled columns and rafters or cold-formed sections for light blocks, purlins at close spacing, bracing in one or two bays per block, and an envelope of sandwich panels, single-skin sheeting with separate insulation, or a hybrid where the first block is clad and the rest follow. The steel is galvanised or coated for the site environment, and the panels carry the thermal and fire performance the block needs. Frames are fabricated, inspected and marked under the same [manufacturing and quality process](/en/manufacturing-quality) as plant buildings.

## 2. Permanent housing and a temporary site camp are different projects

| Option | Suits | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Demountable steel camp, bolted and marked | Projects with a defined construction phase; camps that will move or be resold | Panels and fittings take damage on every move; foundations are abandoned each time |
| Permanent staff housing in steel frame | Camps that will outlive the project; owner-operated facilities | Longer design life expectations, heavier services, more authority involvement |
| Stacked container or modular units | Fast mobilisation, small headcount, tight site | Module width and height are fixed by transport; corridor and stair options are limited |
| Hybrid: steel frame with masonry or block infill | Hot climates where thermal mass is wanted; local labour is available | Slower, heavier, and the steel scope becomes a frame-only supply |

Design life is the distinction buyers miss. A demountable camp is built to be assembled, used and moved; permanent housing is built to last decades. The frames look alike; the connections, coating and documentation do not.

## 3. Block layout and column grid decide how many panels you buy

Camp cost is driven by repetition and envelope area, not by the tonnage of any one block. Three layout families cover most projects:

| Layout | Suits | Limits | What it changes in the steel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single-storey rows with covered walkways | Hot, dusty sites; fast phasing; low-rise preference | Large footprint; long service runs; more roof area per bed | Many identical short-span blocks; walkway roofs add purlins and columns |
| Two-storey blocks with an external stair or a central corridor ([two-storey frame considerations](/en/blog/multi-storey-steel-frame-buildings-guide/)) | Tight sites; camps close to the plant boundary | Stairs, balustrades and floor systems enter the scope; egress and fire separation become decisive | Braced frames, floor beams, stair towers, higher wind uplift on the upper roof |
| Stacked modular units | Very fast mobilisation; short duration | Transport-bound dimensions; limited corridor width; stacking needs a designed connection | Minimal frame; the connection and foundation design carry the risk |

Two rules save money later. Keep block lengths in multiples of the panel module, so cladding needs no cut panels at every end. And fix the grid before the room layout; moving a column to gain one bed costs more than the bed is worth.

**Can a labour camp be moved to the next project?** Yes, if the frame is bolted rather than site-welded, every member is marked to a block and position, and panels are handled as reusable units. What rarely survives is the foundation, floor slab, buried drainage and sanitary fit-out. A move is a new project with a new foundation design; it is not a transport exercise. The [dismantle and re-erect guide](/en/blog/relocatable-steel-building-dismantle-reerect-guide/) covers what survives a move in detail.

## 4. Heat, dust and sand change the envelope, not the frame

In hot, arid and coastal sites the frame is usually the easy part. The envelope and the details decide whether the camp is habitable.

- **Solar gain.** A dark roof over a sleeping block radiates heat into the room below all evening. Roof colour, a ventilated cavity, insulation and shading of the walls matter more than the frame.
- **Dust and sand ingress.** Dust enters at panel joints, at the gap under the wall sheet, at door thresholds and at any opening that was not closed. Closure details are a design item, not a site improvisation.
- **Abrasion.** Wind-blown sand wears coatings on exposed edges and fixings. The coating system and the touch-up regime belong in the specification.
- **Coastal salt.** Camps near a port or a shoreline need a corrosion strategy for the frame, the fixings and the panels; the same frame inland may need nothing beyond a standard system.
- **Condensation.** A camp that is cooled at night and hot at midday can condense on the underside of the roof sheet. A vapour control layer and a drained cavity cost less than replacing fittings every season; the [condensation guide](/en/blog/steel-building-insulation-condensation-guide/) sets out where those layers belong.
- **Rain and drainage.** Flat-looking camp roofs still need falls, gutters and downpipes; a roof that ponds will fail at the first joint it can find.

None of these change the frame sections much, but all of them change the price, the maintenance plan and how long the camp stays usable.

## 5. Utility blocks sit inside the steel line: kitchen, ablution, laundry

The utility blocks are where camp projects usually go wrong, because they are procured as fit-out but they load the structure.

- **Kitchen and dining.** Canopies, grease ducts, cooking equipment and hot water tanks impose point loads and need support at height. A canopy arriving after the frame is erected has to hang from something designed for it.
- **Ablution and toilets.** Wet rooms need falls, trapped drainage and often raised floors; pipework must be planned around the bracing, not through it.
- **Laundry.** Washing machines and dryers vibrate, and a light steel floor transmits it through the block. Isolation and a stiffer local floor are usually the answer.
- **Water storage.** Tanks on the roof add permanent load and wind area; tanks on the ground need a plinth and a pump house. Decide early, because a tank added later is a structural review, not an order.
- **Generator and fuel.** A generator house needs ventilation, exhaust routing and fire separation from sleeping blocks.

**Who is responsible for foundations and utilities in an overseas camp?** Normally the local party: foundations, slab, buried drainage, water and power connections, operating approvals. The steel supplier provides the frame, envelope, connection design and erection guidance. Where this split is not written down, a frame ends up standing on a slab nobody designed for it.

## 6. What the frame carries beyond the roof

Camp frames acquire loads after handover. A realistic load schedule should list:

- roof sheeting, insulation, purlins and any walkway platform;
- water tanks and solar water heaters, filled weight included;
- air-conditioning condensers, brackets and cable trays;
- antennas, signage, floodlighting and future internal storage.

Wind uplift on a light camp roof is often the governing case, not gravity. A block with a large overhang or an open walkway canopy behaves very differently from the same block closed on all four sides.

## 7. Sleeping blocks are a life-safety problem before they are a steel problem

The frame rarely decides whether people get out of a burning block. The panel core, spacing between blocks, exits, stairs and alarm system do.

Points that should be settled with the local authority before fabrication:

- travel distance from the furthest bed to an exit, measured on the real layout;
- two independent exits from every sleeping block larger than the local single-exit limit;
- fire performance of the wall and roof panels, and whether a combustible core is allowed;
- separation between blocks, and whether that gap is kept on site;
- stair and corridor width for the occupancy assumed, not the occupancy at handover;
- detection, alarm and extinguishing provision, and who commissions it.

A steel camp that meets the structural code but fails the fire strategy cannot be licensed, however low its price.

## 8. Delivering a camp in phases

Camps are usually occupied before they are finished, so plan the delivery around that.

1. **Freeze the headcount curve.** Get the client's occupancy plan per month, not a single total. It sets block count, utility capacity and shipping batches.
2. **Freeze one block type and one grid.** Detail the prototype fully, including the wet areas, before repeating it.
3. **Approve the prototype.** Erect and inspect one block; fix the panel joints, the door thresholds and the walkway detail there, not on twenty blocks.
4. **Ship by block, marked.** Bundle each block's members and panels together and mark them to the block and position used on the erection drawing.
5. **Hand over phase by phase.** Complete the utilities for a phase before the next phase ships, so that occupied blocks are not sitting next to open trenches.

## 9. Responsibility boundary in an overseas camp project

| Stage | ZhongSai scope | Local party scope |
|---|---|---|
| Design coordination and detailing | Frame and envelope detailing, connection design, erection drawings, block marking plan | Local structural review, registered engineer approval, site-specific loads |
| Fabrication and quality control | Fabrication, inspection, mill certificates, coating checks | Independent inspection if required by the client |
| Marking, packing, container loading | Block-wise marking, packing lists, loading plans | Site unloading, storage, damage claims |
| Export coordination | Export documents, booking support | Import clearance, duties, inland transport |
| Installation | Technical guidance, erection sequence, tolerances | Local labour, crane, erection, site safety |
| Foundations, slabs, utilities, fit-out | Interface information only | Foundations, civil works, drainage, water and power connections, sanitary and kitchen fit-out, camp operating approvals |

## 10. What to prepare before a quotation

- [ ] Headcount by month, and the room standard (single, two-bed, four-bed)
- [ ] Site plan with block positions, access roads and the fire access route
- [ ] Single-storey or two-storey preference, and the reason
- [ ] Climate data: temperature range, wind, rainfall, dust, distance from the coast
- [ ] Utility loads: water tank size, generator capacity, kitchen equipment list
- [ ] Fire strategy or the local code clause that governs sleeping accommodation
- [ ] Foundation information, or confirmation that the local engineer will provide it
- [ ] Phasing plan and the shipping schedule it implies

**How many workers does one accommodation block hold?** It depends on the room standard the client and the local labour authority accept, the block length that fits the container, and whether rooms are single or shared. The frame can often be re-planned for a different bed count, but the plumbing, partitions, exits and utilities cannot — so the bed count should be fixed before the block is detailed, not after the frame is fabricated.

## Common questions

### How much steel does a labour camp need per worker?

There is no reliable global figure. It depends on block size, span, whether blocks are single or two-storey, the panel system, the wind and seismic loads at the site and how much walkway and canopy is included. A camp with two-storey blocks and heavy walkway roofs carries more steel per bed than a single-storey layout with open walkways.

### Can we mix single-storey and two-storey blocks in one camp?

Yes, and many camps do: single-storey for family or supervisor accommodation, two-storey for single workers. The cost is a second block type to detail, fabricate and mark. Mixing is a layout decision, but it should be taken before the first block is detailed.

### What roof and wall system suits a hot, dusty site?

Usually a light-coloured roof with a ventilated cavity or a insulated panel with a non-combustible core, plus closing details that actually stop dust at joints and thresholds. The right answer depends on the local fire rules for sleeping accommodation, the cooling strategy and the maintenance capability on site.

### Do you supply bathrooms, kitchens and furniture?

No. Sanitary ware, kitchen equipment, furniture and the internal fit-out normally sit outside the steel package and are procured locally or by the client's fit-out contractor. The steel scope provides the frame, the envelope, the openings and the interface information those trades need.

### Can the camp be extended when the headcount grows?

Usually yes, if the original grid, bracing and block type were designed for extension and the foundations can be added alongside. Extension is easiest at the end of a row and hardest in the middle of an occupied block, where the roof and the walkway have to be opened.
