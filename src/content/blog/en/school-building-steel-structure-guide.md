**Direct answer:** a school is rarely one building. It is a set of repeated classroom blocks, one or two larger halls for assembly, dining or sport, and small service buildings. Steel suits this pattern because the blocks repeat, erect quickly and can be handed over in phases around the school calendar. What drives the steel scope is the classroom grid and corridor width, the clear span of the hall, single or two-storey blocks, the roof and envelope the local climate demands, the foundation type the ground allows, and the order in which the campus will open.

Most school enquiries start as a site plan with a number of classrooms, a hall and a boundary fence. That plan fixes the teaching spaces but says nothing about what the frame has to do. Twelve classrooms can be one long block, two blocks of six, or a two-storey block; each choice changes bracing, corridor structure, roof area, service runs and how much can be erected in one holiday window.

This guide explains what the steel package covers in a school project, which decisions must be settled before a frame can be priced, and where the boundary sits between the frame supplier and the local team.

## 1. What a school campus asks of the steel frame

Five properties matter more than anything else in school construction: repetition, clear internal space, speed, durability and room to grow. The same logic drives most [steel building solutions](/en/solutions) for public and institutional projects.

- **Repetition.** One classroom block type used six or twelve times costs less to detail, fabricate and erect than a different layout for every block. Standardising the grid is worth more than optimising one block.
- **Clear space.** Teaching rooms want as few internal columns as possible, and halls want a genuinely column-free activity area.
- **Speed.** Schools are usually needed for a fixed intake date, and the safe working window is often a holiday.
- **Durability.** Envelopes and connections have to survive decades of use by children, with doors, corridors and roofs taking the most punishment.
- **Growth.** Campuses add classrooms, toilets and stores over time; a bolted, marked and documented frame keeps that option open.

What the frame does not solve matters as much. Steel provides no thermal comfort, no acoustics, no daylight strategy and no behaviour of a crowd; those come from the block plan, the envelope build-up and the local design code.

**What steel structure is used for a school building?** Low classroom blocks are normally hot-rolled portal frames with purlins at close spacing and bracing in selected bays; larger halls move to trusses or deep beams to reach a clear span. The envelope is single-skin sheeting with separate insulation, sandwich panels, or sheeting over a masonry infill where local labour and thermal mass are preferred. Members are galvanised or coated for the site environment, and the frames are fabricated, inspected and marked under the same [manufacturing and quality process](/en/manufacturing-quality) as industrial buildings.

## 2. Three building types, three different frames

A campus is not one structural problem. The classroom block, the hall and the service building each fail in a different way, and each is priced separately.

| Building type | Typical steel form | What decides it | Where it goes wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single-storey classroom block | Portal frames, short spans, braced bays, purlins at close centres | Classroom depth, corridor width, roof pitch and the number of bays per block | Blocks detailed one by one instead of as one repeated type |
| Two-storey classroom block | Braced frames, floor beams, stair and landing structure | Floor system, stair position, egress width and the extra wind load on the upper roof | Floor and stair scope left out of the steel quotation |
| Assembly, dining or sports hall | Trusses or deep beams, heavier columns, larger roof area | Clear span, roof weight, ceiling or grid height, and how the roof is braced | Span promised before the roof build-up and loads are known |
| Service and admin block | Light portal frames or cold-formed framing | Openings, equipment loads, kitchen or laboratory services | Services routed after the frame is fixed, forcing site cuts |

The most common commercial error is quoting one frame type for the whole campus. A hall that needs a 24-metre clear span cannot be priced on a classroom rate, and a two-storey block cannot be priced as two single-storey blocks stacked on the same drawings.

## 3. What fixes the classroom grid and the hall span

Span and grid are the two numbers that control tonnage, envelope area and erection method. Both are set by use and code before they are set by steel.

| Decision factor | Why it matters | Information required |
|---|---|---|
| Classroom module and bay spacing | Sets the number of frames, purlins and panels, and how the roof repeats | Room sizes, corridor width, local space standard per pupil |
| Hall clear span | Drives truss depth, column section and crane size on site | Required clear area, ceiling height, roof and services weight |
| Roof build-up | Insulation, ceiling, solar or water loads change the rafter and purlin sizes | Envelope specification, drainage path, wind and rain data |
| Storey count | Two-storey blocks add floor beams, stairs, balustrades and egress structure | Stair and corridor layout, floor finish, upper-floor loads |
| Openings and extensions | Large door and window openings remove bracing; future blocks need planned joints | Door and window schedule, phasing plan, boundary of future stages |

**How wide can a classroom block be before columns appear?** That is a planning question first and a structural one second. Classroom depth is normally set by the local space standard per pupil, desk layout and daylight, while the structural bay follows the room module. A block can usually keep its teaching rooms column-free at the room scale and place columns on the corridor line; whether a whole block can be column-free depends on the depth you choose, the roof loads and the local code, so it has to be checked against drawings rather than assumed from a typical figure.

## 4. Climate, comfort and the envelope

Steel does not make a classroom comfortable. In hot, dry or coastal sites the envelope does most of the work, and the frame only has to carry it.

- **Heat.** A light steel roof heats quickly. Insulation under the sheeting, a ventilated cavity, light colours and shading over walkways change internal conditions more than any frame decision.
- **Dust and sand.** Openings, door thresholds and panel joints are the entry points; sealing details belong to the envelope package, and they have to be designed rather than added on site.
- **Coastal salt.** Coating specification and galvanising follow the exposure, not a default. Members that will be washed by rain need different treatment from sheltered ones.
- **Condensation.** A steel roof with a cold outer skin and a warm interior condenses on the underside unless the build-up includes a vapour path and a controlled detail. The mechanics are set out in our guide to [insulation and condensation in steel buildings](/en/blog/steel-building-insulation-condensation-guide/).
- **Acoustics.** A steel-framed hall with light walls is a poor concert space unless the ceiling and wall build-ups are planned for it; that is a design decision taken with the architect, not a steel detail.

## 5. Foundations, floors and the civil boundary

School sites are often donated land, filled ground or former farmland, and the ground report is frequently the weakest document in the package. Foundation type follows the ground, not the frame.

- Pad footings under isolated columns suit firm ground and low-rise blocks.
- Rafts or ground beams are used where the soil is weak, variable or previously disturbed.
- A suspended slab is needed where the ground floor is a hall or where fill cannot be compacted reliably.
- Slab-on-grade works for classroom blocks once the sub-base and damp proofing are controlled.

Anchor bolt position, base plate level and the interface between the frame and the slab have to be agreed in writing before fabrication, because correcting a misplaced bolt after the concrete has cured is expensive in any country. The foundation, the slab, the drainage and the access road are civil works, normally carried out by the local contractor against drawings and a setting-out schedule issued from the steel side.

## 6. Building around the school calendar

Schools are occupied for most of the year, and work that closes a corridor or a hall is usually only tolerated in a holiday. Phasing is therefore part of the structural brief, not a logistics afterthought.

1. **Fix the term dates and the working windows** before the programme is promised. One long break and two short breaks give different erection sequences.
2. **Freeze one block type and one grid.** Every extra type costs drawings, marking and site confusion.
3. **Order the envelope with the frame.** Panels, insulation and flashings are often the longest lead items, and a frame standing without a roof is a frame exposed to rain.
4. **Fabricate and mark by block.** Members are packed and labelled per block and per erection zone so that nothing has to be sorted on site.
5. **Ship in phases** where the campus is being opened in stages, using the container planning approach described in [export delivery](/en/export-delivery/).
6. **Erect block by block and hand over block by block**, so teaching can start in a finished building while the next stage is still open.

**Can a school building be erected during term breaks?** Often yes, if the blocks are standardised, the foundations are cast in advance and the frame arrives marked per block. A single-storey classroom block with a small crew and a mobile crane can be closed in within a short window; a two-storey block with stairs and a suspended floor usually needs a longer window or an occupied-site plan with segregated work areas. The honest answer depends on the block type, the number of blocks, and whether the envelope is on site before the break starts.

## 7. Where the steel scope stops

International school projects fail most often at the boundary between the supplier and the local team, not inside the frame.

| Stage | ZhongSai scope | Local party scope |
|---|---|---|
| Design | Engineering coordination and detailing to the supplied architectural and structural intent | Registered engineer, permit drawings and authority submissions |
| Ground and floor | Foundation interface drawings, anchor bolt setting-out, base plate details | Soil investigation, excavation, foundations, slab and drainage |
| Fabrication | Fabrication, inspection, marking, packing and container loading | Import clearance, duty, inland transport and site storage |
| Erection | Installation technical guidance, sequence and torque instructions | Local labour, plant, scaffolding, lifting plan and site safety |
| Envelope | Supply of panels, insulation and flashings as specified | Local installation labour, sealants and final weather sealing |
| Completion | As-built marking list and connection records | Building inspection, occupancy approval and handover to the school authority |

This split holds unless the contract says otherwise. It matters because a school cannot open without an occupancy approval, and that approval is a local process.

**Who is responsible for foundations and building approvals on an overseas school project?** The local party. Foundations, slabs, drainage, permits and the registered engineer's sign-off sit with the client's local team; the steel supplier provides the interface drawings, the setting-out information and the fabrication records those parties need. Putting either side's work into the other's quotation is the fastest way to create a dispute.

## 8. What to prepare before a quotation

A school frame can be priced properly only when the block plan is settled. The list below is what a fabricator needs to quote without guessing.

- [ ] Site plan with block positions, boundaries and the phasing sequence
- [ ] Room schedule: teaching rooms, hall, admin, toilets, store, kitchen
- [ ] Corridor and stair layout, with single or two-storey intent
- [ ] Required clear span and height for the hall or sports area
- [ ] Roof and wall build-up, including insulation and ceiling
- [ ] Local design loads: wind, seismic, rain and any snow requirement
- [ ] Ground investigation or the assumed bearing capacity
- [ ] Door and window schedule, plus any future extension boundary
- [ ] Delivery port, site access and the lifting equipment available

Where an item is missing, the quotation has to carry an assumption. Assumptions are fine if they are written down; they become claims when they are not.

## 9. Common questions

### Does a two-storey classroom block need a different frame?

Yes. A two-storey block adds floor beams, a floor system, stairs, landings, balustrades and a heavier upper roof, and it changes the bracing and egress design. It is a separate structural package, not two low frames stacked, and it is quoted separately. The general framing logic is covered in our guide to [multi-storey steel frame buildings](/en/blog/multi-storey-steel-frame-buildings-guide/).

### Can one design be reused on another campus?

Often yes, and that is where the cost advantage sits. A standard classroom block with a fixed grid, fixed envelope and a documented connection set can be reissued for a second site with only the load and foundation inputs updated. Reuse only works when the first project was documented properly, including the marking list and the as-built connection details.

### What roof suits a hot, dusty site?

A light-coloured metal roof with insulation under the sheeting and a ventilated cavity above the ceiling performs better than a bare single-skin roof, and it can be detailed to keep dust out at the joints. The exact build-up depends on the local climate data, the fire requirement and the maintenance capability of the school authority, so it should be confirmed against the project specification rather than fixed from a catalogue.

### How is a school building shipped?

Members are normally packed by block and by erection zone, with panels crated separately and labelled to match the marking list. For a campus of repeated blocks, the container plan follows the erection sequence so that the first blocks can be built while later ones are still in transit. Packing and loading are part of the steel scope; clearance and inland haulage are not.

### Can the frame be extended later for more classrooms?

Usually yes, if the original design left a joint, bracing plan and extendable foundation edge. Adding a bay at the end of a block is straightforward; inserting a bay inside an operating corridor is not. Decide at design stage where the campus is allowed to grow.
