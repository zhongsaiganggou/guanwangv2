---A mill test certificate (MTC) under EN 10204 records what a specific heat of steel actually tested: its standard, grade, heat number, chemical composition and mechanical properties. Accepting it comes down to four checks — the certificate type matches the contract (3.1 or 3.2), the standard and grade match the order and drawings, every heat number traces to the physical members, and the reported values sit inside the standard's limits for that grade. Those limits depend on the grade and the standard, not on one global figure. ZhongSai has built steel structures for 20 years and focused on exports for 15, and coordinates MTC documentation for the structures it fabricates.

This guide covers the fields to read, 3.1 versus 3.2, what each document does and does not prove, when certificates should arrive in an export shipment, and which checks belong to the buyer.


## What is a mill test certificate?

A mill test certificate is a material document issued by the steel mill that reports the actual tested results of a heat — a single production batch of steel. It links the delivered material to a heat number, the applicable standard, the grade, the dimensions, and the tested chemical and mechanical properties. Fabricators typically collect the mill certificates for the steel they buy and pass them on in the project's material documentation pack. At ZhongSai, heat-number traceability and certificate collection are coordinated as part of [our manufacturing and quality process](/en/manufacturing-quality/).

Names vary by standard: mill test report (MTR), mill certificate, inspection certificate. For European projects, EN 10204 defines the types.

## EN 10204 Type 3.1 vs Type 3.2

| Factor | Type 3.1 | Type 3.2 |
|---|---|---|
| Issued by | Manufacturer's independent quality department | Manufacturer + the manufacturer's or an independent third-party authorized inspector |
| Independence | Independent of the production function | Independently witnessed/verified |
| Tests | Performed on the delivered batch per the order | Same, with third-party witness or verification |
| Common use | Most structural steel orders | Projects requiring independent inspection authority |
| Cost/time | Standard | Adds inspection cost and time |

Which type is required depends on the project specification, the local authority and the contract; it is not a choice to make after delivery. The type number is not a quality grade of the steel: both types report tested results for the heat, and the difference is who issues and who witnesses the inspection.

## Which document proves what

Buyers often ask for "the certificate" without separating the documents around it. They prove different things and do not substitute for each other.

| Document | What it proves | What it does not prove | Typical stage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mill certificate, EN 10204 3.1 | Reported chemistry and mechanical results for a heat, tied to grade, standard, dimensions and heat number | That member dimensions, welds or coating are correct | Material receipt |
| Mill certificate, EN 10204 3.2 | The same results, with independent witness or verification of the inspection | Anything outside the tested scope | Where the contract requires independent assurance |
| PMI (XRF or OES) spot check | Chemistry at the point measured, as a screening check | Mechanical properties; conformity of the whole heat | Yard or site verification |
| Re-test on sampled material | Mechanical results of the sampled piece under the standard's sampling rules | The rest of the consignment | Dispute resolution or specified acceptance testing |
| Factory production control records (e.g. EN 1090) | The fabricator's control system and the execution class it is certified for | The grade of one specific heat | Supplier qualification |

**Does a mill certificate prove the welds are sound?** No. Weld quality is shown by procedure and welder qualification, visual inspection and non-destructive testing — covered in our [NDT testing guide](/en/blog/steel-structure-ndt-testing/). The MTC reports the base material only, and buying 3.2 does not add weld inspection to the scope.

## Fields to read on an MTC

- **Standard** — e.g. EN, ASTM, GB/JIS; the values must be assessed against that standard
- **Steel grade** — must match the order and the fabrication drawings
- **Heat number** — the batch traceability key; it appears on the physical steel
- **Dimensions / product form** — plate, section, tube, and size
- **Chemical composition** — carbon and alloy elements, reported against the standard's limits
- **Mechanical properties** — yield strength, tensile strength, elongation, and (where specified) impact test results
- **Delivery / heat treatment condition** — e.g. normalized, thermomechanical rolled, as delivered
- **Signatures and date** — the authorized sign-off and issue date

The exact chemical and mechanical values are not fixed global numbers: they depend on the steel grade and the standard being used, and the MTC reports the tested values for that specific heat.

A large structure can use many heats. Ask for a register mapping each heat number to the member marks or bundles it went into, rather than a loose stack to reconcile yourself.

## How to verify an MTC against the steel

1. Match the heat number on the certificate to the marking or stamping on the physical members
2. Confirm the steel grade matches the purchase order and the drawings
3. Check the reported chemical values fall within the standard's limits for that grade
4. Check yield strength, tensile strength and elongation against the standard
5. Confirm the certificate type (3.1 or 3.2) matches the contract
6. Where required, arrange independent re-testing (e.g. PMI chemical analysis) for disputed material

Member length, camber and bolt-hole positions are a separate check against the [fabrication tolerances](/en/blog/steel-structure-fabrication-tolerances-guide/) your order specifies — the certificate does not report them.

## MTC timing in an export shipment

Certificates that arrive after the steel is on site are hard to act on, so fix the document flow in the order and follow it.

| Stage | What the buyer should expect | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Before loading | Scan copies of the MTCs for the heats allocated to the order, plus a heat-number list | Standard, grade and heat numbers against the approved material list |
| With the shipping documents | The certificate set in the form the contract requires — copies or originals | Completeness against the heat list; consistency with the [packing list](/en/blog/steel-structure-packing-list-container-loading-plan-guide/) |
| After arrival | Access to the register mapping heat numbers to member marks | Physical markings match; disputed heats quarantined before erection |

**Can the certificates be reviewed before shipment?** It depends on the contract, but scan copies plus a heat-number list before loading are a normal request and catch a mismatch while the steel is still in the yard. Originals and payment-linked documents follow the contract and any letter of credit.

## Chemical and mechanical checks

Chemical composition is read element by element against the standard table for the grade — carbon and the main alloying elements must sit within the specified ranges. Mechanical properties are read as tested values: yield strength, tensile strength and elongation at minimum, with impact energy added where the specification requires it.

![Handheld PMI spectrometer checking steel chemistry on a member (illustrative concept image)](/images/blog/steel-structure-mill-test-certificate-guide/spectrometer.jpg)

**Can a PMI check replace the MTC?** No. A handheld XRF or OES unit reads chemistry at one spot; it gives no tensile or impact results and does not certify the whole heat. It screens an unclear marking or a disputed heat, but reconciliation still runs through the certificate.

## MTC verification checklist

- [ ] Required certificate type (3.1 or 3.2) stated in the purchase order
- [ ] Standard and grade on the certificate match the order and drawings
- [ ] Heat numbers trace to the physical member markings
- [ ] Chemical composition within the standard's limits
- [ ] Yield, tensile and elongation values conform; impact values where required
- [ ] Dimensions and product form match
- [ ] Certificate register received and reconciled with the packing list
- [ ] Certificates collected in the project handover pack
- [ ] Disputed material quarantined and re-tested before use

## FAQ

### What is a mill test certificate?

It is the steel mill's document reporting the tested chemical composition and mechanical properties of a specific heat, together with the grade, standard, dimensions and heat number used for traceability.

### What is the difference between EN 10204 3.1 and 3.2?

Type 3.1 is issued by the manufacturer's independent quality department; Type 3.2 adds inspection or verification by an independent authorized representative. Type 3.2 is used when the project requires independent assurance.

### Is a 3.1 certificate enough for a steel structure?

For many structural orders, 3.1 is the standard requirement. Whether 3.1 or 3.2 is needed depends on the project specification, local authority and contract, so it must be fixed before ordering.

### How do I check the heat number?

The heat number is marked or stamped on the steel; match it to the certificate, then confirm the grade and the tested values for that heat.

### When should I receive the certificates?

It depends on the contract. A common arrangement is scan copies and a heat-number list before loading, with the formal certificate set travelling with the shipping documents; originals and payment-linked documents follow the agreed terms.

### Does one certificate cover a whole container?

No. Certificates follow heats, and a container can carry members from many heats, so one shipment may need a large certificate set. That is why a heat-number register is more useful than counting pages.

### Do ASTM or GB grades use EN 10204?

EN 10204 is the European inspection-document standard; ASTM and GB orders use their own mill report forms. The check is the same: grade, heat number, chemistry and mechanical values assessed against the standard named on the document.

### Does an MTC replace NDT or third-party inspection?

No. The MTC is a material document. NDT covers welds and is performed to a separate procedure and acceptance level; third-party inspection is a scope the buyer appoints and pays for.

### What happens if the certificate does not match the steel?

Quarantine the material, do not install it, and ask the supplier to reconcile the records or provide independent re-testing; non-conforming material should be resolved before erection.

### Can chemical composition be checked on site?

A handheld XRF/PMI spectrometer can verify chemistry on site as a check, but it supplements rather than replaces the mill certificate and mechanical test results.
