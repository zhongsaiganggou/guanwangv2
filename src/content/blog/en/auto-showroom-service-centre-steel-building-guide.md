A car showroom and service centre is not one building with one set of requirements. It is a retail space that has to look right from the road and a workshop that has to work right under a vehicle lift, and both sit inside the same steel envelope. Steel framing is a practical choice because it delivers the long clear span the showroom needs and the repetitive bay module the workshop needs, on a schedule that suits a brand launch. The layout itself is driven by the dealer's brand standard, the workshop equipment list, vehicle flow, local fire and building rules, and the site's drainage and access — not by the frame alone.

Getting this wrong is expensive in a specific way: a showroom column that lands in the middle of the display area, a workshop bay too shallow for a lift and a long-wheelbase vehicle, or a floor slab that cannot take the point loads of the equipment. All three are layout decisions made before the frame is detailed.

## Part 1: Two functions inside one envelope

| Requirement | Showroom side | Workshop side |
|---|---|---|
| Primary driver | Brand identity, sightlines, facade | Equipment layout, vehicle circulation |
| Structural priority | Long clear span, minimal interior columns | Repetitive bays, local strengthening |
| Floor | Finished, level, presentable | Hard-wearing, drained, equipment anchors |
| Services | Lighting, HVAC comfort, low noise | Extraction, compressed air, drains, power |
| Fire strategy | Occupancy and egress for the public | Hazard areas, separation, extinguishing |
| Enclosure | Glazing, feature cladding, signage zones | Robust cladding, access doors, ventilation |

The two sides can share a roof and a structural system. They usually should not share a floor specification, a fire compartment, or a circulation route.

## Part 2: What determines the showroom span

Showroom span is a brand and merchandising decision before it is a structural one. What matters:

- **How many vehicles** the display floor must hold, and whether they sit in a line, a fan or a raised feature.
- **Whether the brand standard requires an uninterrupted glass frontage** with no visible mullion grid, which pushes structure to the perimeter.
- **Whether a mezzanine** is used for offices, a lounge or delivery handover, and whether that mezzanine is structural or a fitted platform.
- **Ceiling height and lighting strategy** — a tall volume with high-level lighting reads differently from a low ceiling with pendants.
- **Signage and facade zones** that must stay free of bracing and tie rods.

A frame that gives a long clear span is straightforward. The harder question is where the one or two interior columns that remain will land, because those positions are fixed once the steel is fabricated.

## Part 3: What determines the workshop bay module

The workshop is a different geometry problem:

| Workshop element | What sets the dimension | Planning consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Service bay length | Vehicle type, lift platform, technician working space | Bay depth is usually governed by the longest vehicle, not the average |
| Bay width | Lift size, tool clearance, door positions | Repetitive modules cost less to fabricate than bespoke bays |
| Lift type | Two-post, four-post, scissor, alignment | Point loads and slab thickness change with lift type |
| Alignment bay | Equipment footprint, run-out space | Needs a level, stable floor and a defined approach |
| Paint or body area | Extraction, fire separation, containment | Usually compartmented away from the display floor |
| Parts and tyre storage | Racking heights and access | May justify a separate low-clearance zone |
| Wash bay | Drainage, water supply, separation | Drainage falls and grease separation must be designed early |

Bays can be grouped in a single hall or split between a fast-fit front area and a deeper body workshop. The frame cost difference is small; the operational difference is large.

## Part 4: Structural implications of glazing and canopy

A showroom frontage is often the most structurally awkward part of the building, because the visible elements are the ones that must not exist — no bracing in the glass plane, no tie rods across the display front.

| Element | Design issue | Typical resolution direction |
|---|---|---|
| Full-height glazing | Wind load and racking stability | Stability from roof diaphragm, portal action or rear bracing |
| Entrance canopy | Cantilever loads, drainage, appearance | Cantilever or tied canopy with its own drainage path |
| Feature cladding bands | Tolerance and interface with glazing | Setting-out coordinated with the glazing supplier |
| Signage and light boxes | Fixing loads and waterproofing | Secondary steel designed with the signage vendor |
| Vehicle ramp or display platform | Slab and frame interface | Ramp structure coordinated with civil works |

Canopies deserve early attention: they collect rain, they move in wind, and they are usually the first thing a customer walks under.

## Part 5: Floor slab and equipment interface

The floor is usually built by the local civil contractor, not supplied with the steel. That makes it the most common interface failure on dealership projects.

| Item | Who normally decides | Why it matters to the steel package |
|---|---|---|
| Slab thickness and reinforcement | Local structural engineer | Lift and equipment point loads must be known before bay layout is frozen |
| Flatness and level tolerance | Owner and equipment supplier | Alignment and measuring equipment need a defined tolerance |
| Joint layout | Civil designer | Joints should not run through a lift footprint |
| Drainage falls and gullies | Civil and MEP | Wash bays and service areas need falls designed with the slab |
| Anchor positions for lifts | Equipment supplier | Anchor setting-out must be issued before the slab is poured |
| Edge details at doors | Civil and steel detailer | Door thresholds and apron levels must match |

If the lift supplier's anchor layout arrives after the slab is poured, the project pays for post-installed anchors or a local slab cut-out.

## Part 6: Fire separation and hazard zoning

Fire strategy is set by local code and the authority having jurisdiction; the steel design has to follow it, not the other way round.

| Zone | Typical concern | Design response direction |
|---|---|---|
| Display floor | Public occupancy, egress, smoke | Protected escape routes, smoke control provisions |
| Workshop hall | Vehicle fuel, hot work, stored materials | Compartmentation, ventilation, extinguishing provisions |
| Paint or body repair | Flammable materials, extraction | Separated compartment with dedicated services |
| Battery or EV workshop area | Thermal runaway risk, isolation | Separated area, detection, ventilation, fire service access |
| Parts store | Combustible packaging and racking | Sprinkler and separation strategy |
| Charging area | Electrical hazard, vehicle access | Equipment protection and egress clearance |

Whether a specific separation is one hour or two, and whether sprinklers are required, depends on the local code, the building's occupancy classification and the authority's interpretation. Those questions belong to the local fire engineer and the submission process.

## Part 7: Vehicle and customer flow

- [ ] Customers and vehicles enter through separate routes.
- [ ] Display vehicles never cross the workshop traffic path.
- [ ] Delivery and PDI movements are separated from customer parking.
- [ ] Service reception has a covered, signposted drop-off.
- [ ] Workshop bays are reachable without reversing through another bay.
- [ ] Parts deliveries do not block the service lane.
- [ ] Refuelling or charging areas are outside the main circulation.
- [ ] Waste oil and tyre collection points have their own access.
- [ ] Fire service vehicles can reach the workshop and charging areas.
- [ ] Staff parking, wash bays and customer parking do not share one entrance.

Most operator complaints after handover are flow complaints, not structural ones.

## Part 8: Roof, drainage and tropical rain

In Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and much of the region, a dealership roof has to handle intense short-duration rain without leaking onto a display floor.

| Risk | Design consideration |
|---|---|
| Overflow at valleys and parapets | Overflow scuppers and secondary drainage paths |
| Canopy drip onto customer areas | Canopy gutters and downpipe routing |
| Wind-driven rain at glazing joints | Interface detailing between frame, glazing and cladding |
| Condensation under metal roof | Vapour control and insulation strategy |
| Debris blocking outlets | Outlet protection and maintenance access |
| Roof access for maintenance | Safe access routes and fall protection |

Roof drainage capacity is a local rainfall design decision; the steel package should provide the structural support for the specified system, not assume a default.

## Part 9: EV charging and battery work areas

Dealers adding EV sales and service need space that the original layout may not have.

- Charging positions require electrical capacity, cable routing and vehicle clearance.
- Battery service areas need isolation, detection and a fire strategy agreed with the authority.
- High-voltage work bays may need separation from general service bays.
- Floor loading changes when battery packs are stored or handled.
- Client handover areas may need a dedicated charging bay away from display traffic.

None of these change the steel frame much. All of them change the layout, the slab, the services and the fire submission — which is exactly why they belong in the brief before detailing.

## Part 10: What to send before a steel quotation

- [ ] Site plan with access, levels and boundary conditions.
- [ ] Brand standard or reference layout showing display area and facade intent.
- [ ] Vehicle count and display arrangement.
- [ ] Workshop equipment list with lift types and anchor data.
- [ ] Bay count, bay function and expected vehicle sizes.
- [ ] Mezzanine requirement and intended use.
- [ ] Canopy extent and signage zones.
- [ ] Required fire compartments and any authority comments.
- [ ] Roof and wall cladding preferences, colours and insulation.
- [ ] Local design loads: wind, seismic where applicable, and floor live loads.
- [ ] Programme and expected handover date.
- [ ] Whether the local civil and foundation design is already appointed.

A quotation built on a brand picture alone will be replaced by a redesign. A quotation built on the list above can be fabricated against.

## Part 11: Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it happens | Cost consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Freezing the frame before the lift list | Steel is urgent, equipment is not yet chosen | Bay widths and slab loads change after fabrication |
| Treating the floor as someone else's problem | Civil scope is separate | Anchor and joint conflicts at commissioning |
| Bracing placed across the glass frontage | Stability assumed before facade design | Visible tie rods or a late redesign |
| Canopy added after the roof is detailed | Canopy is seen as an architectural extra | Re-detailing, drainage leaks, delayed handover |
| One fire compartment for display and paint | Fire strategy not yet consulted | Compartmentation retrofit |
| Ignoring EV plans | EV service added after opening | Charging and battery areas squeezed into unsuitable space |

## Part 12: Responsibility boundary

| Task | Export supplier | Local side |
|---|---|---|
| Engineering coordination and detailing | Yes | Confirms design basis, brand standard and loads |
| Fabrication, quality control, marking, packing, container loading | Yes | May witness or audit |
| Export coordination and shipping documents | Yes | Clearance, inland transport |
| Foundation and slab design | Interface information only | Local structural engineer and civil contractor |
| Fire strategy and authority submissions | Supporting information on request | Local fire engineer, architect and owner |
| Local labour and physical erection | Technical guidance only | Local contractor |
| Equipment installation and commissioning | No | Equipment supplier and dealer |
| Glazing, signage and specialist facade | Interface dimensions only | Specialist subcontractors |

## Part 13: FAQ

**Question: Can one steel frame serve both the showroom and the workshop?**
Answer: Yes. A single frame usually works, with different bay modules on each side and local strengthening where the workshop equipment sits. The two areas still need different floor specifications, service routes and often different fire compartments.

**Question: How long a clear span does a showroom need?**
Answer: It depends on the brand standard, the number of display vehicles and whether the frontage must be uninterrupted glazing. There is no universal figure; the span follows the merchandising layout, and the remaining column positions should be agreed before fabrication.

**Question: Who designs the workshop floor slab?**
Answer: Normally the local structural engineer and civil contractor, working from the equipment supplier's load and anchor data. The steel supplier provides interface information such as column base positions and door openings, but the slab is a local design responsibility.

**Question: How is the workshop separated from the showroom in fire terms?**
Answer: The separation is set by local code and the authority's interpretation, based on occupancy, hazard and egress. A paint or battery area is usually compartmented; whether the general workshop needs separation from the display floor depends on the applicable code and the fire engineer's strategy.

**Question: Can a canopy be added later?**
Answer: It can, but it is usually more economical and cleaner to design it with the main frame. A late canopy means re-detailing connections, revisiting drainage and possibly reworking the facade interface.

**Question: Does the building need to be designed for EV charging from the start?**
Answer: If EV sales or service are planned within the building's life, the electrical capacity, vehicle clearances, battery area and fire strategy should be considered at layout stage. Retrofitting charging into a completed showroom is usually constrained by the existing services and slab.

**Question: What is the biggest cause of delay on dealership projects?**
Answer: Late equipment information. Lift types, anchor layouts, paint booth requirements and charging plans all change the slab, the bays and the fire strategy, and steel fabrication cannot wait indefinitely for them.

## Part 14: Start from the layout, not the frame

Steel is the easy part of a dealership building. The expensive part is discovering, after the frame is fabricated, that the display column sits in the wrong place or the lift anchors do not match the slab. Send the brand layout, equipment list and site plan together, and the steel package can be detailed once.
