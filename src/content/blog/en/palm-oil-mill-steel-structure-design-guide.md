A palm oil mill steel structure is designed from the process equipment list backwards, not from a standard warehouse plan. Sterilizer cages, the threshing drum, digester and press stations, the boiler house, kernel silos and the effluent area decide the loads, the vibration, the corrosion class and where maintenance access must be. Mill capacity, equipment vendor drawings, local code, site conditions and transport limits then set the actual dimensions — there is no universal frame for a palm oil mill.

## Part 1: What the steel package actually covers

A mill building looks like one structure on a drawing, but the steel package is a set of structures with different duty. Getting the boundary wrong is the most common source of site rework.

- Mill main building frame: columns, rafters, purlins, roof and wall cladding over the process line.
- Sterilizer and threshing area: heavier framing, local strengthening and usually a monorail or hoist beam for maintenance.
- Boiler house and stack support, where the boiler vendor's scope stops.
- Kernel silo support structure, if the silos sit on steel legs rather than concrete.
- Platforms, walkways, stairs, handrails and roof access for operations and maintenance.
- Equipment support steel that the vendor does not supply: fan bases, pipe supports, chute brackets.
- Lifting beams, hoist monorails and maintenance access openings that must exist before erection.

What is normally outside the steel package: the process equipment itself, piping and insulation, electrical and instrumentation, the boiler pressure parts, and all civil works. Those interfaces have to be named in the quotation, because an unpriced interface becomes a site argument.

## Part 2: Process data needed before detailing

Steel cannot be detailed from a layout sketch. The following inputs are needed before the frame can be sized, and each one has an owner.

| Input | Normally provided by | Why the frame cannot be fixed without it |
|---|---|---|
| Mill capacity and process flow | Owner / process designer | Sets equipment count, station sizes and platform extent |
| Equipment layout with elevations | Process designer | Sets column positions, pit locations and clear heights |
| Weight, centre of gravity and support points of each major item | Equipment vendor | Determines local loads, base frames and support steel |
| Dynamic data: threshing drum, digester, press, vibrating screens | Equipment vendor | Determines vibration isolation and frame stiffening |
| Sterilizer cage travel and maintenance withdrawal space | Equipment vendor / owner | Determines clear bay width and monorail position |
| Boiler and stack interface data | Boiler vendor | Determines the boundary and the loads passed to steel |
| Steam, condensate and hot surface temperatures | Process designer | Determines thermal movement and detailing at interfaces |
| Corrosion environment and washdown practice | Owner | Determines coating system, detailing and material choice |
| Local wind, seismic and code requirements | Local registered engineer | Determines the design basis, not a default value |
| Site boundary, access and lifting constraints | Owner / contractor | Determines module size and erection sequence |

If vibration data arrives after fabrication, the frame is being redesigned on site. Palm oil mills are unusually unforgiving here: a threshing drum or a press that shakes a frame joint loose will also shake loose the equipment alignment.

## Part 3: The loads that are not "roof plus wind"

A mill frame carries several load families at once, and they do not peak at the same time. That is exactly why a plain portal frame copied from a warehouse drawing does not work.

| Load family | Where it comes from | What it does to the steel |
|---|---|---|
| Equipment dead load | Sterilizer, thresher, press, tanks, boiler | Local column loads, base frame sizing, support steel |
| Dynamic and vibration load | Threshing drum, digester, press, screens, fans | Stiffening, joint detailing, isolation, fatigue checks at connections |
| Thermal load | Steam lines, hot equipment, hot surfaces | Expansion allowance, clearance at interfaces, not rigidly boxed-in steel |
| Humidity and condensate | Steam, washing, tropical climate | Corrosion class, detailing to drain, avoid water traps |
| Maintenance load | Cage withdrawal, rotor change, monorail lift | Clear bay width, hoist beam capacity, temporary support provisions |
| Wind on a low, wide, clad building | Local wind data | Bracing, roof fixing, cladding restraint |
| Seismic action, where required | Local code | Frame ductility and connection design |
| Construction load | Module weights, crane positions, temporary bracing | Erection scheme and temporary works |

The mistake is to add these up into one "heavy" number and design everything heavier. The right approach is to place them where they occur: stiffen the threshing bay, isolate the vibrating equipment, and let the warehouse-type bays stay economical.

## Part 4: Which structural form fits which area

There is no single frame type that suits the whole mill. Most mills end up with a mixed structure, and the mix should be decided before tendering.

| Structural form | Suits | Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Portal frame with heavier local members | Mill main building, bagging and storage bays | Fast to detail and erect, economical per tonne | Local dynamic loads need separate stiffening |
| Braced heavy frame with isolated equipment bases | Sterilizer and threshing area | Controls vibration and cage travel clearance | More fabrication hours, tighter tolerances |
| Separate steel tower or support frame | Kernel silos, stack, some tanks | Keeps process loads out of the main building frame | Extra foundations and interfaces |
| Open steel structure with light roof | Boiler house, effluent and utility areas | Ventilation, access, lower cladding cost | Weather protection and corrosion exposure |
| Modular bolted frames | Remote sites, phased expansion | Container-friendly, faster site assembly | More joints to align and seal |

Two rules are worth stating plainly. First, keep vibrating equipment on its own stiffened sub-frame, connected to the building frame only where the design intends. Second, do not let a silo or a stack hang its loads on a roof frame that was sized for wind.

## Part 5: Corrosion is a design input, not a paint choice

A palm oil mill is a wet, warm, steamy building. Condensate forms on cool steel, washdown water sits in detail pockets, and steam lines leak at joints long before anyone repairs them. Coating selection and detailing therefore belong in the design stage.

- Detail for drainage: no flat pockets, no closed boxes that hold water, no unvented cavities.
- Treat the sterilizer, boiler and effluent areas as a more severe exposure than the bagging bay.
- Protect the inside of hollow sections and the back of cladding where condensation is likely.
- Choose the coating system from the actual exposure and the owner's maintenance capability, not from a single default specification.
- Hot surfaces and steam lines need a system rated for the temperature at that location.
- Keep touch-up access in mind: a coating that cannot be inspected or repaired will fail quietly.

Coating type, number of coats and dry film thickness depend on the corrosion category, the local atmosphere and the owner's maintenance plan. A single global specification copied from another project is not a design decision.

## Part 6: Interfaces that decide whether the mill runs

Most mill problems appear at interfaces rather than in the middle of a member.

- Sterilizer cage withdrawal: the clear space must exist in the steel layout, including the monorail and the temporary platform used during maintenance.
- Threshing drum rotor change: access opening, lifting beam and clear height need to be designed, not negotiated during a shutdown.
- Boiler house boundary: the boiler vendor supplies pressure parts; the steel supplies the supporting frame and the stack support. That line must be drawn in the contract.
- Kernel silo discharge: the silo support, the chute brackets and the access platform must line up with the vendor's connection points.
- Effluent and utility areas: open structures with corrosion-resistant detailing, and a clear path for maintenance vehicles.
- Roof drainage: heavy tropical rainfall means the gutter and downpipe layout is part of the structural layout, not an afterthought.

## Part 7: Floors, pits and foundations are not steel

Equipment foundations, pits, slabs and the civil works around them are local scope. Steel sits on them and must be detailed to what actually exists, not to what the drawing assumed.

- Anchor bolt positions and levels must be surveyed before fabrication is released.
- Pit edges, trench locations and drainage falls affect column bases and platform framing.
- Where the steel frame supports vibrating equipment, the foundation mass and the connection detail work together; changing one changes the other.
- Settlement under silos or stacks shows up as misalignment at the discharge point, not as a crack in the frame.

Whoever holds the civil scope must confirm the surveyed as-built before the steel detailing is frozen. Otherwise the mill's first alignment problem appears during commissioning.

## Part 8: Erection in a mill that may already be running

Many palm oil projects are expansions: the new line goes up beside an operating mill. That changes everything about the erection plan.

- Module size is limited by what the crane can reach and lift at that radius, not only by the container.
- Hot work near live steam lines or a running boiler needs a permit regime agreed before mobilization.
- Temporary bracing must stay until the bay is closed and the equipment bases are connected.
- Access routes for the crane and for the equipment vendors compete; sequence them in one plan.
- Deliveries have to match the erection order: frames first, cladding and platforms later.

## Part 9: Packing and shipping for a process plant

Process plant steel is not a warehouse kit, and the packing list has to reflect that.

- Mark every member with a mark that matches the erection drawing, and pack by zone rather than by member type.
- Keep the stiffened equipment sub-frames identifiable; a mixed bundle is a site delay.
- Protect machined and bolted faces, and separate bolted assemblies from loose plate.
- Confirm module lengths against container or break-bulk limits before detailing, not after.
- Ship the anchor bolts, base plates and shims as a controlled set with the column schedule.

## Part 10: Responsibility boundary

| Scope | ZhongSai (steel package) | Local party |
|---|---|---|
| Engineering coordination and detailing | Steel frame, platforms, support steel, connection details | Process design, equipment selection, local code confirmation |
| Fabrication and quality control | Members, welds, coating, dimensional check | Vendor equipment inspection |
| Marking, packing, container loading | Marks, packing list, loading plan | Unloading and site storage |
| Export coordination | Documents, shipping coordination | Import clearance, local transport |
| Erection | Technical guidance, erection drawings | Local labour, crane, supervision, permits |
| Foundations and civil | Interface data and base plate details | Design, construction, survey, as-built confirmation |
| Authority submissions | Design information as required | Registered engineer, permits, approvals |

Unless the contract says otherwise, foundation design, erection labour and authority approvals sit on the local side. Stating that early prevents the assumption that a steel supplier also supplies a mill.

## Part 11: Checklist before the frame is detailed

- [ ] Mill capacity and process flow confirmed in writing.
- [ ] Equipment layout with plan and elevations issued.
- [ ] Weight, centre of gravity and support points for each major item received.
- [ ] Dynamic data for threshing, digestion, pressing and screening received.
- [ ] Sterilizer cage travel and maintenance withdrawal envelope agreed.
- [ ] Boiler and stack interface scope written into the contract.
- [ ] Steam and condensate temperatures confirmed.
- [ ] Corrosion category and washdown practice agreed with the owner.
- [ ] Local wind, seismic and code basis confirmed by the local engineer.
- [ ] Anchor bolt positions surveyed against the drawing.
- [ ] Pit, trench and drainage layout reflected in the steel detailing.
- [ ] Module sizes checked against transport and crane capacity.
- [ ] Monorails, lifting beams and maintenance openings listed.
- [ ] Packing by erection zone and mark sequence agreed.

## Part 12: Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Better approach |
|---|---|---|
| Treating the mill as a large warehouse | Vibration shakes joints loose, equipment misaligns | Identify equipment zones and stiffen them locally |
| Detailing before vibration data arrives | Redesign and site rework | Freeze the frame only when dynamic data is in |
| Silo or stack loads carried by the roof frame | Overloaded members, blocked access | Separate support structure with its own foundation |
| One coating spec for the whole mill | Early failure in wet areas, over-spec elsewhere | Exposure-based coating schedule |
| Anchor bolts assumed, not surveyed | Columns cannot be set | Survey and confirm before fabrication release |
| Ignoring maintenance withdrawal space | Equipment cannot be serviced | Design the envelope into the layout |
| Modules sized only by container | Crane cannot lift at that radius | Size against both transport and lifting |

## FAQ

**Question: Is a palm oil mill steel structure different from a normal industrial building?**
Answer: Yes. A normal industrial building is usually governed by roof, wind and occasional crane loads. A mill frame carries vibrating process equipment, hot surfaces, steam and condensate, and maintenance operations such as cage withdrawal, so the stiffening, the detailing and the coating class change.

**Question: What information does a steel fabricator need before quoting a mill?**
Answer: Mill capacity and process flow, equipment layout with elevations, weights and support points of major items, vibration data, sterilizer cage travel, boiler interface scope, steam temperatures, corrosion environment, local design basis and transport limits. Without these, any price is only an allowance.

**Question: Who designs the equipment foundations?**
Answer: Normally the local civil or structural engineer, working from the equipment vendor's data. The steel package provides base plate details and interface loads. Foundations and steel must be detailed against the same surveyed data, or the mill will not align at commissioning.

**Question: Can the steel frame be split between several suppliers?**
Answer: It can, but the interfaces become the risk. Platforms, monorails, support steel and cladding all attach to the main frame; splitting them across suppliers usually produces tolerance conflicts at exactly the points that matter.

**Question: How is corrosion handled in the sterilizer and boiler areas?**
Answer: With a more severe exposure category than the dry bays, detailing that drains water instead of trapping it, protection of concealed surfaces, and a coating system rated for the surface temperature. The exact system depends on the local atmosphere and the owner's maintenance capability.

**Question: Is a bolted modular frame suitable for a remote mill site?**
Answer: Often, yes, because container shipment and site assembly are simpler. The trade-off is more joints to align and seal, and a need for tighter fabrication control. Module size should be set against both the container and the crane available at site.
