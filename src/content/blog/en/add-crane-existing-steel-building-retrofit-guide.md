Adding an overhead crane to an existing steel workshop is usually possible, but not by ordering a crane and hanging it on the frame. The crane introduces vertical wheel loads, longitudinal and lateral surge forces, and a moving load that the original frame, columns, base plates, anchor bolts and foundations were never designed for. The first decision is whether the crane load goes into the existing structure or into a separate crane structure. That decision drives cost, downtime and risk far more than the crane brand.

## Part 1: Two ways to carry the crane

| Approach | What it means | When it makes sense | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Load the existing frame | New runway beams tied into existing columns | Frame has spare capacity and drawings exist | Hidden overstress in columns or foundations |
| Independent crane structure | New columns and runway beams carry the crane only | Existing frame is light, unknown or must not be altered | More steel and a separate foundation |
| New crane bay alongside | A new bay built for the crane | The crane needs more span or height than exists | Largest scope and longest programme |

An independent structure is often the most economical safe answer, because it removes the crane load from a frame that cannot be verified.

## Part 2: Data needed before anyone can answer

- Original structural drawings, or a measured survey if they are missing.
- Column section, spacing, base plate and anchor bolt details.
- Roof structure, purlin layout and existing bracing.
- Crane data: capacity, span, hook height, wheel loads, duty class, crane weight.
- Clearance between the proposed runway level and the roof structure.
- Foundation information: existing pad size, slab, soil report if available.
- Site constraints: access for a crane, working height, production downtime allowed.

Without the first four items, any answer is a guess. This is the most common reason retrofit quotations stall.

## Part 3: What actually gets checked

| Element | Check | Typical failure mode |
|---|---|---|
| Rafter / roof beam | Additional bending from runway loads | Local overstress, deflection |
| Column | Axial plus bending from crane surge | Buckling or weld distress |
| Base plate and anchors | Uplift and shear from surge | Plate bending, anchor pull-out |
| Foundation pad | Eccentric load and bearing pressure | Settlement, cracking |
| Bracing | New lateral forces from crane travel | Sway, roof racking |
| Rail and runway beam | Wheel load, rail alignment | Rail wear, crane skew |
| Connections | Weld or bolt capacity at the joint | Cracking at existing welds |

The check must be done by a local engineer who can inspect the building, not from a photo.

## Part 4: Wheel loads, surge and duty class

Crane loads are not static:

- **Wheel loads** concentrate on the rail and runway beam, not spread over the roof.
- **Surge** along and across the runway adds horizontal force to the columns and bracing.
- **Duty class** decides how often the structure sees those loads; a frequently used crane is a different case from occasional maintenance lifting.
- **Impact** from load handling adds a dynamic factor that depends on the crane type and the local code.
- **Fatigue** matters at connections when the crane runs many cycles.

These parameters come from the crane supplier and the local design code. A capacity figure alone is not enough.

## Part 5: Clearance inside an existing building

Retrofit projects usually fail on geometry before they fail on strength:

- Hook height above the highest obstruction.
- Runway elevation versus roof tie and purlin level.
- Crane end carriage clearance to columns.
- Space for rails, buffers and end stops.
- Maintenance access to the crane and the roof above it.

If the crane needs more height than the roof allows, the answer is usually a new bay or an external gantry, not a taller building.

## Part 6: Strengthening details

Where the existing frame is kept, strengthening usually means:

- Adding cover plates or new members to columns at the runway level.
- New runway beams with bolted or welded connections to the column.
- Local stiffening at the bracket to spread wheel loads.
- Additional bracing to resist surge.

Site welding on existing steel is a quality question: surface preparation, access, qualified welders, inspection. Bolted solutions are often preferred in an operating plant because they avoid hot work and reduce fire risk.

![A bolted collar plate and crane bracket being fitted to an existing steel column (illustrative concept image)](/images/blog/add-crane-existing-steel-building-retrofit-guide/bracket.jpg)

## Part 7: Foundations in an operating plant

- Existing slabs are usually not designed for crane column loads.
- Isolated pads beside or under the column line are common; they must be checked against the existing foundation.
- Underpinning or enlarged pads may be needed where the existing base is small.
- Water and drainage around new pads must not undermine the old foundation.
- Vibration and excavation near live machinery need a method statement.

Foundation work is local scope and is usually the item that stretches the programme.

## Part 8: Electrical, rails and safety

- Crane power supply, festoon or conductor rails, and isolation points.
- Rail installation, alignment and grouting to the runway beam.
- End stops, buffers and limit switches.
- Access platforms, ladders and fall protection for maintenance.
- Warning signage and operating zones agreed with the plant.

These are small in steel tonnage but decide whether the crane can be commissioned on schedule.

## Part 9: Doing the work without stopping production

| Stage | Typical activity | Production impact |
|---|---|---|
| Survey and check | Measure, inspect, verify drawings | None |
| Design and approval | Local engineer, crane supplier, permits | None |
| Foundation | Pads, anchors, curing | Local, planned shutdown |
| Steel erection | New columns or runway beams | Partial, by zone |
| Crane installation | Rails, hoisting, commissioning | Shutdown window |
| Load test | Test lift per local requirement | Shutdown window |

Phasing by bay or by zone normally loses less output than one long shutdown.

## Part 10: Responsibility boundary

| Task | Export supplier | Local side |
|---|---|---|
| Feasibility check of existing frame | Review of supplied drawings, comment | Local engineer inspects and confirms |
| New crane structure or runway beams | Detailing, fabrication, QC | — |
| Connections to existing steel | Details and fabrication of new parts | Site fit-up and welding by local crew |
| Foundations | Interface loads and base plate details | Local civil design and construction |
| Crane supply and commissioning | Not included unless agreed | Crane supplier and local electrician |
| Permits and load testing | Supporting documents on request | Owner and local authority |

## Part 11: Checklist before ordering steel

- [ ] Crane capacity, span, hook height and wheel loads confirmed.
- [ ] Duty class and operating hours known.
- [ ] Original drawings or measured survey obtained.
- [ ] Column, rafter and base plate details verified on site.
- [ ] Foundation condition inspected.
- [ ] Clearance to roof and purlins measured.
- [ ] Local engineer appointed for the capacity check.
- [ ] Option chosen: existing frame, independent structure, or new bay.
- [ ] Surge and bracing effects reviewed.
- [ ] Connection method decided: bolted or welded.
- [ ] Rail, buffer and end stop details agreed.
- [ ] Staging plan and shutdown windows agreed with the plant.
- [ ] Permits, load test and inspection requirements checked.
- [ ] Access for erection crane confirmed.

## Part 12: Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Choosing a crane before checking the frame | Crane arrives, building cannot take it |
| Checking only the runway beam | Columns and foundations overload |
| Ignoring surge forces | Sway and connection cracking |
| Using a photo instead of a survey | Wrong assumptions, site rework |
| Welding heavily in a live plant | Fire risk and production shutdown |
| Reusing the existing slab as a foundation | Settlement under crane columns |
| No maintenance access | Crane unusable or unsafe to service |
| Skipping the load test | Commissioning refused by authority |

## Part 13: FAQ

**Question: Can any steel workshop be fitted with an overhead crane?**
Answer: Not any. A light frame with unknown foundations may not be able to take crane loads at all. In that case an independent crane structure beside or inside the building is the safer route.

**Question: What is the most economical way to add a crane?**
Answer: It depends on the existing frame. If the frame has capacity, tied-in runway beams use the least steel. If capacity is unknown or too low, an independent structure avoids reinforcing a frame nobody can verify.

**Question: Do I need a structural engineer?**
Answer: Yes. A local engineer has to inspect the building and check the frame, columns, anchors and foundations against the crane loads. Export fabrication cannot substitute for that.

**Question: Can the crane be installed without stopping production?**
Answer: Often in stages. Survey, design and off-site fabrication happen while the plant runs; foundation, erection, commissioning and load test need planned shutdown windows. The sequence decides how much output is lost.

**Question: How much height does an overhead crane need?**
Answer: It depends on hook height, crane height, runway elevation and the roof structure above. If the existing roof cannot provide it, a new bay or external gantry is usually the answer.

**Question: Do the existing columns need strengthening?**
Answer: If the crane load goes into the frame, often yes — cover plates, new brackets or additional bracing. Whether it is needed comes from the local engineer's check, not from a rule of thumb.

**Question: What does a retrofit quotation need?**
Answer: Drawings or a survey of the existing building, crane data, site location and intended operating pattern. With those, the options can be priced and compared.

## Part 14: Start with the building, not the crane

Send the existing drawings (or a measured survey), the crane data, and the intended lifting duty. The retrofit options — tied-in runway beams, independent crane structure, or a new bay — can then be compared before any steel is ordered.
