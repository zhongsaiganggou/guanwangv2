A cleanroom steel structure is not a factory shed with a better wall panel. The frame has to carry concentrated tool loads and a raised floor, hold a ceiling grid and air-handling plant, limit vibration at the tool base, and keep an envelope tight enough for the cleanroom contractor to pass its own tests. The building footprint says almost nothing about member sizes; the tool list, the cleanliness class, the air-change strategy, the vibration criterion and the local code say almost everything. Until those inputs are agreed, shop drawings cannot be frozen, because each one of them changes the column grid, the roof framing or a connection detail.

## Part 1: What the steel shell is actually responsible for

A cleanroom project is two buildings in one envelope. The steel shell is the base building: columns, roof framing, purlins, cladding, roof plant supports and the structural interface to the raised floor and partitions. The cleanroom is the fit-out: wall and ceiling panels, fan filter units, ducts, raised floor, tool hook-ups and validation.

Problems start when the two are designed separately. A shell designed as a warehouse gives the cleanroom contractor a roof that deflects too much for a ceiling grid, a slab that cannot take a raised floor pedestal grid, and penetrations that were never sealed in the cladding detail. Fixing that after erection costs far more than sizing the frame correctly at tender stage. The shell only needs to be predictable: loads known, limits stated, details drawn, interfaces owned.

## Part 2: The load list the steel frame must be sized for

Cleanroom loads are rarely uniform. They arrive as a tool schedule with point loads, a raised floor on a pedestal grid, a ceiling hung from the roof and a plant deck. The structural team needs numbers and locations, not a description.

| Load source | What the steel team needs | Who normally defines it |
|---|---|---|
| Process tools and benches | Weight, footprint, point-load positions, anchoring method, any dynamic component | Tool vendor through the owner or cleanroom contractor |
| Raised access floor | Pedestal grid spacing, dead load, live load, height above slab | Cleanroom fit-out designer |
| Ceiling grid, panels and FFUs | Suspended load per square metre and where it hangs | Cleanroom fit-out designer |
| Roof-mounted AHU, MAU, exhaust and chillers | Equipment weight, curb positions, maintenance access, pipe and duct routes | MEP designer with vendor data |
| Ducts, cable trays, busways and process piping | Route, support spacing, loaded weight per metre | MEP designer |
| Fire-rated separation and doors | Compartment lines, rating, door and damper positions | Local fire engineer |
| Future expansion | Where the next tool bay or cleanroom module goes | Owner's master plan |

Two items are almost always late: the tool schedule and final MEP route. Both change member sizes, and a roof beam sized before the air-handling units are selected is usually re-sized after the MEP tender — a re-design that lands mid-fabrication unless the sequence is planned.

### What loads does a cleanroom steel structure carry?

It carries three groups: concentrated process and utility loads from tools and raised floors, distributed loads from ceiling grids and ducts, and roof plant loads from air-handling and exhaust equipment. Vibration and deflection limits sit on top of those loads as design constraints, so the same weight can require a much heavier frame when a sensitive tool sits above it.

## Part 3: Vibration and deflection are the criteria that set member sizes

In a warehouse the governing criterion is usually strength; in a cleanroom it is often stiffness. Sensitive tools — lithography, metrology, bonding, inspection — publish their own vibration criterion, usually a velocity or acceleration limit against a frequency band. The engineer converts that into a floor response limit at the tool base, and that limit drives beam depth far more than tonnage does.

| Criterion | What drives it | Structural response |
|---|---|---|
| Tool vibration limit | Tool vendor's published criterion, measured at the tool base | Stiffer floor framing, shorter spans, isolated slabs or separate support |
| Floor flatness and level | Raised floor levelling, tool installation tolerance | Slab and framing tolerances agreed before pour |
| Roof deflection above ceiling grid | Ceiling panel alignment, FFU sealing | Tighter deflection limit than a warehouse roof |
| Differential movement between shell and fit-out | Thermal movement, settlement, slab-on-grade vs steel frame | Movement joints and slip details at interfaces |
| Walking and trolley vibration | Operations traffic near sensitive tools | Layout separation, not only heavier steel |

None of these limits has a single global value: a packaging cleanroom and a semiconductor cleanroom in the same city produce different numbers because the tools differ. That is why the criterion must come from the tool vendor, and why a fabricator quoting without it may be quoting a shell that does not fit the process.

### Who decides the vibration limit for a cleanroom?

The tool vendor publishes it, the owner's engineer adopts it for the project, and the structural engineer converts it into a floor response limit. The steel fabricator does not invent the number. What the fabricator needs is the resulting limit, the tool positions and the floor framing layout, because those decide whether the frame is a normal portal or a stiffened, separately supported structure.

## Part 4: Air tightness and envelope continuity

A cleanroom envelope holds pressure through cladding, penetration details and joints — not the frame alone. But the frame decides whether those details are buildable. Three things matter structurally: a cladding support grid whose purlin spacing matches the panel module so joints land on framing; a framed, sealed opening for every duct, pipe, tray and exhaust route crossing the envelope; and continuous junctions where vapour and air barriers run past the steel. A food plant fights washdown water, a cleanroom fights air leakage and particle ingress, so a cladding specification written for one should not be copied to the other without review.

## Part 5: Roof plant, interstitial space and service routes

High-tech plants carry far more roof plant than a warehouse: make-up air units, exhaust fans, chillers, scrubbers and sometimes a full air-handling deck. It needs a structural deck or curb system, maintenance access that avoids the cleanroom, and routes that reach the ceiling grid without cutting a classification boundary.

Interstitial space — the volume between ceiling and roof — is the pressure valve of the design. Too little leaves no room for ducts, valves, sprinklers or maintenance; too much pays for height the building does not use. Workable height is set by duct depth, service clearances, ceiling grid depth and access strategy, fixed by the MEP designer with the steel team checking framing depth.

### How much interstitial space does a cleanroom need?

No universal figure exists. It is calculated from duct depth, sprinkler and service clearances, ceiling grid construction, access strategy and local code, then rounded up for future tool changes. The steel team's input is the structural depth available and the cost of carrying that height across the whole roof.

## Part 6: Fire strategy and compartment interfaces

Cleanroom fire strategy is usually built around compartmentation: the shell provides the compartment walls and rated structure, the fit-out provides rated panels, doors and dampers. That split has to be drawn early, because a compartment line through a cleanroom bay changes both the steel layout and the panel system. Rated columns and beams need their protection system agreed before fabrication, since coating thickness depends on the section factor and the required period; wall openings need framed, sealed details for ducts and trays; and escape routes must not depend on doors locked for pressure control. Fire ratings and damper requirements depend on local code and insurer requirements, confirmed by the local fire engineer before coating.

## Part 7: Corrosion and chemical exposure

A cleanroom is controlled, but not benign. Cleaning agents, solvents, acids, process chemicals and condensate from cooling coils all reach surfaces the steel team built, so the corrosion system has to be selected against the actual chemical list rather than a general "indoor" assumption. The practical consequences sit at the interfaces: coated steel behind wall panels, roof plant curbs, exhaust duct supports and floor-level plinths where spills collect. Coating selection, dry film thickness and surface preparation depend on chemical exposure, cleaning regime and local environment, so they are specified per project with the owner's process data.

## Part 8: Phasing — base build and tool fit-out are different projects

Most cleanroom buildings are built under two contracts: base shell, then fit-out and tool install. The steel design must anticipate that gap, because tool positions move between contracts more often than any other input.

| Phase | What is fixed | What the steel design must leave open |
|---|---|---|
| Concept | Building envelope, roof plant strategy, expansion direction | Column grid that can accept a later tool bay |
| Base build tender | Shell loads, roof plant allowances, compartment lines | Allowance for future point loads and ceiling changes |
| Fit-out tender | Tool schedule, raised floor, ceiling grid, duct routes | Penetrations and reinforcement planned before pour |
| Tool install | Final anchoring, hook-up, commissioning | Access paths and temporary openings in the envelope |

### Can a cleanroom steel building be expanded without stopping production?

Usually yes, if the expansion was designed in advance: a column grid that continues, a roof and wall detail that can be extended, and a service corridor that lets the new bay be connected during a planned shutdown. Expanding a building that was designed as a closed box is much harder, because the new bay has to tie into an envelope that has no designed joint.

## Part 9: Erection sequence and keeping the envelope clean

Cleanroom construction starts with a steel frame, a slab and dust, so the sequence must protect surfaces that will later sit inside the classified area. Erect and align the frame before partitions arrive, complete the envelope and roof drainage before fit-out, seal ducts and openings temporarily, and set out the raised floor grid from a surveyed datum rather than the steel line. Trial assembly at the manufacturing base reduces site cutting and painting, a major source of contamination and damage.

## Part 11: Responsibility boundary on an overseas cleanroom project

| Stage | ZhongSai scope | Local party scope |
|---|---|---|
| Design development | Engineering coordination, detailing support, buildability review | Registered engineer, local code compliance, permits |
| Shop drawings | Detailing, connection design coordination, drawing issue for approval | Approval of interfaces with fit-out and MEP |
| Fabrication | Fabrication, quality control, trial assembly, marking | — |
| Packing and shipping | Packing, container loading plan, export coordination | Import clearance, inland transport |
| Site works | Installation technical guidance | Foundations, civil works, local labour, physical erection |
| Handover | As-built steel documentation | Cleanroom validation, authority submissions, commissioning |

This split is the default and changes only where the contract says so. Cleanroom vendor, MEP and shell scopes belong in one interface matrix: failures here are ownership failures, not steel failures.

### Where does the steel fabricator's scope end on a cleanroom project?

It ends at the structural and envelope interface: the frame, the cladding support, the framed penetrations and the roof plant supports. Wall and ceiling panels, fan filter units, raised floor, tool hook-ups, validation and classification testing belong to the cleanroom specialist. The fabricator supplies the interface geometry those systems need, and the contract should say who seals each penetration.

## Part 12: Common mistakes buyers make

1. Quoting the shell before the tool list exists, then treating the frame as fixed.
2. Copying a warehouse cladding specification into a cleanroom.
3. Leaving the raised floor grid out of the structural set-out, so pedestals land off-grid.
4. Sizing roof beams before the air-handling units are selected.
5. Assuming the steel contractor will seal cleanroom penetrations.
6. Fixing the fire strategy after the steel is coated.
7. Designing expansion as an afterthought, then finding the envelope has no joint.

## Part 13: FAQ

**Is a cleanroom building structurally different from a normal factory shell?**
The principles are the same, but the governing criteria change. Stiffness, floor flatness, envelope continuity and penetration control usually matter more than strength, and loads are concentrated rather than uniform.

**Can an existing steel workshop be converted into a cleanroom?**
Sometimes, but it depends on existing frame stiffness, the slab, the envelope and space for interstitial services. A conversion needs a survey and vibration assessment before committing to a cleanliness class.

**Who provides the raised floor, partitions and ceiling?**
Normally the cleanroom fit-out contractor. The steel scope provides the structural support, the set-out datum and the framed openings those systems connect to.

**How is vibration handled for sensitive tools?**
The tool vendor's criterion is converted into a floor response limit, and the framing is then stiffened, shortened or separated to meet it. In some cases the tool sits on an isolated slab rather than on the building frame.

**What fire protection does the steel need?**
It depends on the local code, the compartment strategy and the insurer's requirements. The protection system and the section factor have to be agreed before fabrication, not after erection.

**Can we add tools or a new bay later?**
Yes, if the original design left a continuation grid, a designed expansion joint and a service route. Retrofitting those into a closed building is significantly more expensive.

**How long does fabrication and delivery take?**
It depends on drawing approval, material availability, tonnage, coating system, trial assembly requirements and shipping schedule. It cannot be reduced to one number before the drawings are approved.

## Part 14: Send the inputs, not just the footprint

A cleanroom shell can be fabricated quickly once the inputs are stable: tool loads, vibration criterion, roof plant, penetrations, compartment lines and the expansion plan. Without them, every week of fabrication is a week of potential re-work. Send the tool schedule, cleanroom layout, roof plant list and local code requirements with your enquiry. If the drawings are not complete, the process description, cleanliness class and target tool list are enough to start the structural conversation — far less costly than reworking a frame sized for the wrong building.
