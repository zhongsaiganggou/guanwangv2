A box beam is not an alternative to a portal frame. A portal frame is a structural system — columns and rafters in a single bay, with stability from the frame action and bracing. A box beam is a member section — four plates welded into a hollow rectangle. A portal frame can be built from hot-rolled I sections or from welded box sections, and the choice changes fabrication, connections, fire protection and cost. Buyers who ask "box beam or portal frame" are usually comparing a section against a system, and the real decision is which section the system should use.

## Part 1: The two terms sit at different levels

A portal frame is defined by its geometry and its behaviour: two columns, two rafters, a moment connection at the knee, and a pinned or fixed base. Its stability comes from frame action plus roof and wall bracing, not from the section shape. That is why a portal frame can be designed with hot-rolled I sections, welded I sections or box sections, and why the same building can be a portal frame in one design and a braced frame in another.

A box section is defined by its build-up: two webs and two flanges welded into a closed hollow. It can be a column, a beam, a truss chord or a crane girder. It is a component decision, not a system decision. Confusing the two leads to the wrong comparison: a buyer may reject "box beams" because a box-column quote was expensive, when the actual comparison was between two fabrication routes for the same frame.

| Term | What it is | What it decides | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Portal frame | A structural system of columns and rafters with frame action | Overall stability, bay spacing, roof geometry, foundation reactions | Single-storey warehouses, workshops, sheds |
| Hot-rolled I or H section | A rolled member with open profile | Member capacity, connection detailing, lead time | Portal frames, braced frames, secondary framing |
| Welded box section | Four plates welded into a closed hollow | Torsion capacity, stiffness in two directions, architectural expression | Long-span frames, heavy crane buildings, exposed structures |
| Welded I section | Three plates welded into an open I | Capacity beyond rolled sizes, custom depth | Long spans, heavy loads, restricted depth |

## Part 2: Why the confusion exists

The confusion is partly linguistic. In some markets, a "box beam" means a hollow structural section bought to a standard, while in others it means a welded built-up member made to a drawing. In a portal-frame building, the rafter is usually a hot-rolled I section tapered or uniform; when the same building needs a deeper section than the rolling programme offers, the fabricator switches to a welded I section, not necessarily to a box. A box appears when the design needs stiffness in both directions, torsion resistance, or a clean exposed profile.

There is also a commercial reason: a box column quote is often higher per tonne than a rolled I quote, so a buyer concludes that box beams are expensive. That compares a fabricated item with a rolled item. The fair comparison is the whole frame.

## Part 3: When a portal frame with I sections is the right answer

The portal frame with hot-rolled I sections is the default for single-storey industrial buildings because it is the most economical way to span a regular bay with a simple roof. The sections are rolled to standard dimensions, available from stock or a mill programme, and the connections are familiar to any fabricator and erector. Detailing is repetitive, coating is straightforward, and the frames ship nested.

It stops being the obvious answer when one of four conditions appears:

- The required section is deeper than the rolling programme can supply at a reasonable lead time.
- The building carries a heavy overhead crane, where the column must resist repeated lateral and longitudinal forces and the section needs a stiff, closed profile.
- The architecture exposes the frame, and a closed box with a clean weld and a smooth coating is the intended finish.
- The frame is long-span and shallow, where a box or a welded I section gives the stiffness that a rolled section cannot.

None is automatic: a light-roof portal frame is usually a rolled-section job, a frame with a heavy crane is not.

## Part 4: What a welded box section actually costs

A box section is four plates, four longitudinal welds, and usually internal stiffeners or diaphragms. The fabrication sequence is: cut and edge-prep the plates, fit them into a box, weld the longitudinal seams, weld the stiffeners, straighten after welding distortion, then blast, coat and mark. Each step has a cost that a rolled section does not carry.

The welds are the main driver. A box column with full-penetration longitudinal seams needs a welding procedure, qualified welders, and normally ultrasonic or radiographic inspection on the critical seams. Welding distortion is real: a long box can bow or twist, and straightening after welding adds hours. Internal stiffeners must be fitted before the box is closed, which means the fabrication drawing must define them, not the site.

![Welded box column assembled from four plates in a fabrication workshop, with stiffeners and submerged arc welding (illustrative concept image)](/images/blog/box-beam-vs-portal-frame-steel-buildings/box-column-fabrication.jpg)

Coating is another difference. A box has an external surface and, if it is sealed, no accessible internal surface. If the box is not sealed, the internal faces need protection and access, which is difficult and sometimes impossible after assembly. The detailing must state whether the cell is sealed, vented or accessible, because that decision affects corrosion protection, fire protection and inspection for the life of the building.

## Part 5: Connections decide whether the box is practical

A box column is a good member and a difficult connection. Bolting a beam to a box column means the bolts must be placed from outside, or the column must have access holes that are later closed. Welding on site is possible but slower and harder to inspect. The common solution is to weld the connection plates and the beam stubs in the workshop, then bolt the beam to the stub on site, so the site connection is a flange-to-flange bolted joint rather than a weld against a closed cell.

![Bolted moment connection between a box column and a box beam, with workshop-welded stubs and site bolts (illustrative concept image)](/images/blog/box-beam-vs-portal-frame-steel-buildings/box-beam-connection.jpg)

This changes what the buyer must freeze. The connection type, the stub length, the bolt grade and the access route all have to be decided before fabrication, because a box column cannot be drilled from inside on site. A hot-rolled I column is more forgiving: a plate can be welded to the flange and bolts placed from the open side.

## Part 6: Fire and corrosion behave differently in a closed cell

Fire protection for a box section is usually external: intumescent coating, boards or a sprayed system. The closed cell needs no internal protection if it is sealed, but that condition must be maintained, because a cell that fills with water becomes an uninspectable corrosion problem. Box columns therefore often carry a vent or drain detail, and the coating specification should state how the cell is treated.

Corrosion protection is a surface-area question. A box has less external surface than an open I of similar capacity, which can reduce coating volume, but the welds and the closed corners are harder to prepare and inspect. In a coastal or chemical environment, the detailing should avoid traps where water sits, and should define whether the cell is sealed, vented or filled.

**What is the difference between a box beam and a portal frame?** A portal frame is a structural system; a box beam is a member section. A portal frame can use box sections, hot-rolled I sections or welded I sections. Comparing the two is comparing a system with a component, so the useful question is which section the frame should use.

## Part 7: The decision table a buyer can actually use

| Building condition | Portal frame with rolled I sections | Frame with welded box sections | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regular single-storey warehouse, light roof | Usually the lowest total cost | Rarely justified | Rolled sections and repetitive detailing |
| Long span with shallow structural depth | May need a welded I section | Often the better fit | Closed profile gives stiffness in both directions |
| Heavy overhead crane | Possible but heavier | Common | Box resists lateral and torsional forces with a stiff closed profile |
| Exposed architectural frame | Weld and finish become visible | Common when a clean line is required | Closed profile hides the web and gives a smooth face |
| Tight site with limited crane capacity | Lighter individual members | Needs a splice strategy | Box members are heavier per metre and may need site splices |
| Aggressive corrosion environment | Simple to inspect and recoat | Needs a sealed-cell strategy | Internal surfaces are not accessible after closure |

**When should box columns be used instead of hot-rolled I sections?** When the design needs stiffness or torsion resistance that a rolled section cannot provide, when the required section is beyond the rolling programme, when a heavy crane loads the column in two directions, or when the frame is exposed and a closed profile is the intended finish. Otherwise the rolled section is usually the lower-cost route.

## Part 8: Fabrication and transport limits that change the design

A box section is heavier per metre than an open section of similar depth, and it is wider. That affects transport: a long box beam may exceed a container or a truck width, and the splice becomes a design item rather than a site detail. The splice needs a designed joint, bolted or welded, with the plates and access defined in the workshop.

Erection is also different. A box column is stiffer and heavier, so the crane lift plan changes; a box beam may need temporary bracing until the connections are complete. The erection sequence must be designed with the connections, not after them, because a box frame with welded stubs cannot be adjusted as easily as a rolled frame with slotted plates.

## Part 9: What the buyer must freeze before pricing

- [ ] Building use and the loads that come with it, including crane capacity and duty class
- [ ] Span, bay spacing, eave height and the maximum structural depth available
- [ ] Section strategy: rolled I, welded I or welded box, and who decides
- [ ] Connection strategy: workshop-welded stubs, site welding or bolted plates
- [ ] Whether the box cell is sealed, vented or accessible
- [ ] Fire protection period and the proposed system
- [ ] Corrosion category and coating system, including internal surfaces
- [ ] Transport limits: container or truck width, maximum piece weight, splice locations
- [ ] Erection method and crane capacity at site
- [ ] Inspection and NDT requirements for the longitudinal and connection welds

## Part 10: Common mistakes

The first mistake is comparing a box quote with a rolled quote per tonne. The second is specifying a box column without deciding how the beam connects to it. The third is assuming a box is automatically stronger; capacity comes from dimensions and plate thickness. The fourth is forgetting that a closed cell must be sealed or protected for its whole life. The fifth is leaving the splice to the site, when a box splice is a designed connection.

**What does a welded box section cost in fabrication time?** It depends on size, plate thickness, weld length, stiffener count and inspection requirements. A box section normally takes more fabrication hours than a rolled section of similar capacity because of the longitudinal welds, distortion correction and internal detailing, but the difference varies by design and cannot be reduced to a single percentage.

## Part 12: How to specify it so the quotes are comparable

Ask each fabricator to price the same scope: section and dimensions, plate thickness, weld procedure, NDT extent, coating, connections, splices, packing and transport. A rolled-section quote and a box quote are not comparable unless the cell treatment, fire period and corrosion category are also stated.

A box frame is a legitimate choice, not a premium upgrade. It earns its cost when the building needs stiffness in two directions, torsion resistance, a heavy crane, a long shallow span or an exposed finish. When none of those conditions applies, a portal frame with rolled I sections is usually the better commercial answer, and the buyer should ask for that comparison explicitly.

## Part 13: FAQ

**Is a box beam stronger than an I-beam?** Not by shape. Capacity comes from the section dimensions, plate thickness and steel grade. A box resists torsion better because the closed profile carries shear around the perimeter, but a deeper or thicker I-beam can carry more bending moment than a small box.

**Can a portal frame use box columns?** Yes. Frame action and bracing define the system; the columns and rafters can be rolled I, welded I or welded box sections. The connections and fabrication drawings change, not the structural concept.

**Why are box sections welded instead of rolled?** Rolling programmes cover a limited range of sizes. A box is built from plates when the required dimensions, plate thickness or length fall outside that range, or when the design needs a closed cell.

**Do box columns need internal access?** Only if the cell is not sealed. A sealed cell is closed for its service life and needs a vent or drain detail; an unsealed cell needs internal protection and access, which is difficult after assembly. State the choice on the drawings.

**Are box sections better for fire protection?** Not automatically. The closed cell reduces the protected surface, but external fire protection still has to be applied and maintained, and a sealed cell must stay sealed. The fire strategy decides, not the section shape.

**Are box beams more expensive than I-beams?** Usually more expensive per tonne, because of the longitudinal welds, distortion correction and internal stiffeners. The frame comparison should include connections, coating, fire protection and erection, not the member price alone.

**What information should I send to price a box frame?** Building use, span, bay spacing, eave height, crane capacity and duty, maximum structural depth, section preference, connection preference, transport limits, fire period and corrosion category.
