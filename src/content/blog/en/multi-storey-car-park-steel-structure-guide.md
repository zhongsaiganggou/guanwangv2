A multi-storey car park is not a warehouse with cars inside it. The frame is sized by the bay module, ramp geometry, deck system, clear height and the fire and ventilation strategy. Floor area tells you almost nothing. A layout that gains one bay per column line can change the whole grid; a ramp moved a few metres can change beam depths across half the building. Those decisions belong to the architect, the traffic consultant and the local code, but they land on the steel frame, so they must be fixed before shop drawings are issued.

## Part 1: What the steel frame is responsible for

The shell is the base building: columns, beams, secondary framing, deck supports, edge protection and the interface to ramps, stairs, lifts and façade. The deck may be composite steel deck with concrete topping, precast planks on steel beams, or a steel deck used only for plant and maintenance areas.

What the shell is not responsible for is the parking operation. Bay marking, signage, barriers, lighting, drainage falls and the wearing surface sit with the architect, traffic consultant and local contractor. Mixing those up is the most common reason a car park is priced twice: the supplier allows for a structural deck, the client expects a finished drivable surface, and nobody has defined the topping.

One difference from an industrial shed matters: almost every square metre is occupied by a vehicle or by someone walking to one, so edge protection, parapets, stairs and lift cores are structural items rather than add-ons.

## Part 2: The layout decisions that size the frame

Four decisions reach the frame before any calculation:

- **Bay module**: bay width and stall depth come from the local parking standard and the traffic consultant. The grid normally lands on one or two bays between column lines, because a column mid-bay removes a space.
- **Aisle and circulation**: aisle width and turning radii set how close columns may stand to the driving line. A column inside a turning envelope gets moved on site at the worst moment.
- **Ramp type and position**: straight, split or helical, internal or external, defines headroom, slope transitions, beam depth beneath it and often the joint position.
- **Storey height**: deck-to-deck height is clear height plus deck and topping, services zone and structural depth. Fix it before the services zone is known and a beam ends up eating the headroom.

Draw one typical bay with column, beam, deck, services and car, and check every clearance. If that bay works, the rest is repetition.

## Part 3: Deck systems and what each one commits you to

The deck is where frame, concrete trade and parking operation meet. Three options are common and not interchangeable; the detailing logic for the first one is covered in the [steel deck and composite floor guide](/en/blog/steel-deck-composite-floor-guide/).

![Steel deck with shear studs before topping (illustrative concept image)](/images/blog/multi-storey-car-park-steel-structure-guide/deck-construction.jpg)

| Deck option | Suitable for | Advantages | What it commits you to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Composite deck with concrete topping | Most car parks with reliable local concrete | Diaphragm action, familiar to site crews, tolerates later openings and EV works | Wet trade, curing time, falls to outlets, topping coordinated with drainage |
| Precast planks on steel beams | Reliable local precast and lifting capacity | No site concrete, fast, clean finish | Careful tying to frame, crane time, joint detailing, factory-cut openings |
| Steel deck without concrete | Plant decks, maintenance floors, temporary access | Light, quick, removable | Not a parking surface and not a diaphragm; vibration, fire and drainage checked separately |

Two points decide more projects than the table suggests. First, whether concrete can be delivered and poured on a city site with restricted hours. Second, how the deck ties to the frame: the diaphragm exists only if deck, studs, edge details and connections are designed as one system. A deck fixed to beams detailed for a warehouse roof will not give the lateral behaviour a car park assumes.

## Part 4: Vehicle loads, ramp loads and the cases that get missed

**What loads does a car park deck carry?** The imposed load for parking areas is set by the local code, usually as a uniform load plus a concentrated wheel load for local effects. Neither number should be invented by the steel supplier: the code and the traffic consultant set them and the structural engineer applies them. What the supplier must confirm is that deck, beams and connections were checked for the same case, including the concentrated load position between beams.

Three cases are regularly missed:

1. **Ramp loads.** A ramp carries braking and turning vehicles on a slope, so the beam beneath may be deeper and more heavily loaded than a deck beam.
2. **Queuing areas.** Vehicles standing at barriers create a denser pattern than parked cars, so entry and exit need their own case.
3. **Maintenance and plant.** Cleaning machines, planters and EV equipment add loads outside the parking pattern; roof plant must be supported, not hung from the deck.

The rule: every deck area is assigned to a load case, and every case appears on the fabricator's drawing.

## Part 5: Clear height, the number that gets cut last

**How much clear height should be allowed?** Enough for the tallest permitted vehicle, signage and lighting below the beams, drainage and services, plus construction tolerance. The exact figure comes from the local code and the operator's vehicle mix, not from a rule of thumb copied from another country.

![Internal ramp between parking levels with a car descending (illustrative concept image)](/images/blog/multi-storey-car-park-steel-structure-guide/ramp.jpg)

Clear height is the first number reduced under pressure and the most expensive to restore. Once fabricated, gaining headroom means raising the building or lowering the deck. Fix the storey height bottom-up: clear height, deck and topping, services zone, then structural depth.

Two zones deserve early review: under the ramp, where beam and slab compete, and the entry throat, where headroom, slope and barrier meet. A drawing review catches what a site visit cannot.

## Part 6: Ventilation, drainage and the services above the deck

A car park is full of exhaust emissions, and ventilation is both a load and a spatial constraint. Whether the building uses natural cross-ventilation through open decks or mechanical ducts and jet fans, the services zone must be reserved before the frame is sized; the planning logic is set out in the [ventilation and daylight guide](/en/blog/steel-structure-ventilation-daylighting-guide/). Ducts hung below a deck sized without them cut the clear height promised to the operator.

Drainage reaches the steel too: outlets, falls and downpipes must match the deck. A composite deck takes falls in the topping, precast needs them built into the planks, and a steel-only deck needs separate waterproofing.

In cold climates, de-icing salt drives corrosion of deck, edge beams and drainage; it belongs in the protection specification before fabrication.

## Part 7: Fire strategy — open deck or enclosed?

Car park fire strategy varies widely between jurisdictions, and the difference changes the frame. An open-sided deck may be treated differently from an enclosed storey, and a sprinklered building differently again. Fire resistance of the steel frame, the protection system, compartmentation and smoke ventilation all follow from that classification.

The consequences are real: protected beams cost more to coat, a compartment wall may need frame support, and smoke control may need shafts that compete with the services zone. A strategy developed after fabrication either over-protects at extra cost or fails at approval.

Obtain the strategy in writing before pricing, and put protection in the steel scope only if the contract says so; it is often a local supply-and-apply item.

## Part 8: Corrosion, coating and the exposed edge

Steel car parks are exposed buildings. Open decks receive rain, salt-laden air and de-icing salt, and edges and drainage paths show it first. Coating, surface preparation and film thickness follow the exposure category, environment and maintenance regime, agreed before fabrication.

Three details matter more than the brand:

- **Edge beams and parapets** collect water and salt and need the same system as the deck, not a site touch-up.
- **Outlets and scuppers** create cut edges that must be protected; a hole cut after coating is a corrosion start point.
- **Galvanizing versus paint** depends on environment, appearance and maintenance access, not on a default.

## Part 9: Movement, joints and long decks

A long deck moves with temperature and the frame must allow it. Joints at ramp interfaces, structural discontinuities and between independently founded sections are normal; discovering the need after the deck is poured is not.

Joint positions belong with the architect and engineer at layout stage: a joint line decides where the deck stops, where beams end and how the ramp connects. On split-level or helical layouts, draw the pattern rather than noting it.

## Part 10: The interfaces that decide the programme

The frame is one of several packages competing for space and crane time. The interfaces that most often delay a car park are:

- **Lift and stair cores**, usually built by others, tied to the frame without unexpected loading.
- **Façade and cladding**, especially on enclosed storeys where cladding must close before the next deck.
- **EV and payment systems**, needing routes, containment and sometimes openings absent from the layout.
- **Signage and lighting**, hung below the deck and consuming clear height.

## Part 11: Erection sequence and the traffic interface

Erection follows multi-storey logic: braced bay first, then the primary frame in sequence, then secondary framing and deck. What differs is that the building may be partly open to the public while later phases are built, on a city plot with restricted hours and no storage.

That makes three items structural rather than logistical:

1. **Marking and delivery** follow the erection plan, not the drawing order, so the crane picks what is needed next.
2. **Temporary bracing** is designed for the actual sequence, including partial decks carrying wind before the diaphragm is complete.
3. **Storage** may be impossible on a tight site, pushing the project to just-in-time delivery.

## Part 12: Inputs to freeze before shop drawings

| Input | Who sets it | Why it changes the frame |
|---|---|---|
| Parking layout and bay module | Architect and traffic consultant within local standards | Sets the column grid and whether a column falls in a bay |
| Ramp type, slope and position | Architect with the structural engineer | Sets headroom, beam depth and joint positions |
| Deck system and topping | Structural engineer with the client | Sets dead load, diaphragm behaviour and drainage coordination |
| Imposed and concentrated vehicle loads | Local code, applied by the structural engineer | Sizes beams, deck and connections |
| Clear height and storey height | Operator, architect and code | Sets the structural depth available |
| Fire strategy and protection | Fire engineer and authority | Sets protection, compartmentation and shaft space |
| Ventilation strategy | MEP engineer | Reserves services zone and shaft space |
| Corrosion category and coating | Client, environment, maintenance regime | Sets surface preparation and protection |
| Expansion joint positions | Structural engineer with architect | Decides where decks and beams stop |

## Part 13: Responsibility boundary

| Stage | ZhongSai scope | Local party scope |
|---|---|---|
| Design coordination | Engineering coordination, detailing, connection design within the agreed scope | Registered engineer, permits, authority submissions, local code interpretation |
| Foundations and civil | Anchor bolt setting information and base plate details | Foundations, civil works, slab and drainage construction |
| Fabrication and quality | Fabrication, QC, trial assembly where required, marking | Third-party inspection if the contract requires it |
| Packing and shipping | Packing, container loading plan, export coordination | Import clearance, port handling, inland transport |
| Erection | Installation technical guidance, erection drawings | Local labour, crane, physical erection, site safety |
| Finishes and systems | Steel frame and deck steel as scoped | Topping, waterproofing, marking, ventilation, fire protection application, EV systems |

This split changes only where the contract says so. The common failure is the deck: the supplier provides deck and studs, and everyone assumes someone else pours the topping.

## Part 14: Common mistakes and a ten-step checklist

The recurring mistakes:

- Sizing the frame from a warehouse load case because the building looks like a shed.
- Fixing the grid before the bay module is confirmed.
- Treating the ramp as just another deck area.
- Leaving the services zone out of the storey height.
- Developing the fire strategy after fabrication.
- Assuming the topping is in the steel scope.
- Forgetting falls, joints or marking by erection sequence.

Ten steps to a fabricable frame:

- [ ] Confirm parking layout and bay module with the traffic consultant.
- [ ] Fix ramp type, slope and position.
- [ ] Choose the deck system and check local supply.
- [ ] Obtain imposed and concentrated vehicle loads from the code.
- [ ] Reserve clear height, services zone and structural depth.
- [ ] Obtain the fire strategy in writing.
- [ ] Fix the ventilation strategy and shaft space.
- [ ] State corrosion category and protection system.
- [ ] Set expansion joint positions.
- [ ] Agree the scope split, including topping and fire protection.

## FAQ

**Is a steel car park lower cost than a concrete one?**
It depends on the local market, deck system, foundation and programme. Steel is fast and light on constrained sites; concrete may cost less where local precast is strong.

**Can a warehouse frame be converted into a car park?**
Sometimes, but load pattern, clear height, ramp, ventilation and fire strategy change, and the deck usually has to be replaced.

**Who designs the ramp?**
Ramp geometry is an architectural and traffic decision; the structural engineer designs the frame and the local engineer confirms compliance.

**Does the steel supplier provide the deck?**
Deck and studs are usually steel scope; topping, reinforcement and falls are local, and precast planks are a separate supply.

**How is fire protection handled?**
The period and system come from the local fire strategy and authority, and the coating route is compared in the [fire protection guide](/en/blog/steel-structure-fire-protection-guide/). Application is often local, and the steel scope should state whether protection is included.

**Can EV charging be added later?**
Usually yes, but containment, routes and openings should be allowed for at layout stage.

**What is the biggest cause of delay?**
The deck interface: steel arrives and the frame goes up while topping, falls and drainage remain unresolved.

## Send the layout, not just the floor area

A car park frame can be fabricated quickly once the layout is stable: bay module, ramp, deck system, loads, clear height, fire strategy and joints. Without them, every week of fabrication is a week of potential rework. Send the parking layout, ramp arrangement, deck preference and local code requirements with your enquiry, or [send project drawings](/en/contact/#quote-form).
