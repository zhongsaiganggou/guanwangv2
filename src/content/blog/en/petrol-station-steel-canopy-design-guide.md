A petrol station canopy is not a roof over some pumps. It is a steel structure that has to sit clear of the tanker envelope, protect dispensers from rain and sun, carry lighting and signage, drain water away from the islands, resist wind uplift on a large open roof, and stay outside the hazardous area rules that apply to fuel. Those constraints fix the geometry and the steel long before appearance does.

## Part 1: What the canopy actually has to do

- Keep rain off the dispenser island and the customer standing at it.
- Carry luminaires, signage and sometimes cameras or price displays.
- Route conduits and drainage inside the structure, not across the soffit.
- Survive wind uplift on a roof with no walls under it.
- Stay clear of vehicle paths and protect columns from impact.
- Respect the hazardous area around vents, dispensers and tanker connections.
- Read as a brand element from the road.

Every one of these is a design input, not a finish choice.

## Part 2: The four common layouts

| Layout | Description | Suits | Constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single island, single canopy | One roof over one dispenser row | Small sites, low throughput | Limited signage area |
| Multi-island, one continuous canopy | One roof spanning several islands | Busy urban sites | Long span, drainage runs |
| Dual-sided, canopy split by shop | Two roofs either side of the building | Sites with a central shop | Two drainage systems |
| Canopy tied to the shop | Roof cantilevers from the building | Tight sites | Loads into the shop frame |

The layout decides span, column positions and where water goes. It should be fixed before any steel is detailed.

## Part 3: What fixes the geometry

| Input | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Dispenser positions and island size | Sets column-free area and clearance |
| Tanker size and turning path | Prevents columns in the swept path |
| Required clearance above vehicles | Depends on local code and vehicle mix |
| Signage size and position | Adds wind area and weight |
| Luminaire layout | Fixes purlin spacing and service routes |
| Drainage outlet locations | Fixes roof falls and gutter depth |
| Fire separation to shop and tank vents | May move a column or shorten the roof |
| Local wind and seismic loads | Govern bracing and foundations |

Clearance heights, span limits and separation distances are set by local code and the fuel system design. They cannot be copied from another country's project.

## Part 4: Hazardous area and electrical

Fuel vapours change what is allowed inside the canopy:

- The area around dispensers, vents and tanker connections is classified by the fuel system designer and the local code (for example IEC 60079 or NFPA 497 as adopted locally).
- Luminaires, conduits, junction boxes and cameras inside a classified area must suit that classification.
- Cables should run in the structure, not clipped to the soffit where they can be damaged.
- Isolation points must be accessible without climbing on the roof.
- Signage and price displays need their own supply and protection.

The canopy fabricator needs the classification drawing, not a verbal description.

## Part 5: Drainage and spill

- Water must fall away from the islands, not onto customers or dispensers.
- Gutters and downpipes run inside or behind the structure, not across the soffit.
- Downpipe outlets must not discharge into the fuelling area or the tanker path.
- Roof falls and gutter capacity depend on local rainfall intensity.
- The apron drainage and the canopy drainage are separate systems with separate responsibilities.
- Spill containment around fill points is a fuel-system item, not a canopy item, but the two must not clash.

A canopy that drips on the island is a design failure, even if the steel is correct.

## Part 6: Impact protection

- Columns should sit outside the vehicle swept path wherever the layout allows.
- Where a column must stay close to the apron, bollards or a protective kerb are part of the design.
- Protection must be visible and maintainable, not buried in the landscaping.
- Column base plates and anchor bolts should be detailed so a damaged bollard can be replaced without touching the column.
- Height barriers and sign supports need the same impact review as the columns.

## Part 7: Fire separation and interfaces

- Separation between the canopy, the shop, the compressor area and tank vents follows the local fire code.
- A canopy tied to the shop transfers fire exposure and structural loads into the shop frame; a free-standing canopy does not.
- Vent pipe positions from underground tanks can force a column move late in the design.
- Emergency shut-off and fire alarm devices must remain accessible and visible under the canopy.

These interfaces are where late changes come from, and late changes are expensive once steel is cut.

## Part 8: Structural form

| Form | Behaviour | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Portal frame canopy | Two columns per frame, roof spanning between | Simple, predictable, good for wide sites |
| Cantilever canopy | Columns on one side only | Clear apron, higher moment at base |
| Tied or braced canopy | Columns linked by bracing | Stiffer, but bracing must clear vehicles |
| Tied to building | Roof supported by the shop frame | Saves columns, loads the building |

Wind uplift usually governs a canopy more than gravity load, because there is no building under it to hold it down. Foundations must be designed for that uplift, not only for compression.

## Part 9: Corrosion and finish

- Coastal and urban sites see salt and pollution; the exposure category decides the coating system.
- Hot-dip galvanizing suits members that are hard to repaint; painted systems suit visible surfaces.
- The soffit is the most visible surface and the hardest to maintain.
- Site touch-up of welds and damaged areas is part of the coating system, not an extra.
- Stainless or aluminium elements may be needed where fuel or cleaning chemicals attack the finish.

Coating thickness and system selection depend on the corrosion category and the local environment; they are not a single global specification.

## Part 10: Cladding and soffit

- Single-skin steel soffit is common and economical; an insulated soffit gives a cleaner look and reduces condensation.
- Panel joints should follow the roof falls so water cannot stand.
- Open soffits expose purlins and services; closed soffits need access for maintenance.
- Colour and finish are brand decisions, but they also affect how visible dirt and condensation become.

## Part 11: Prefabrication and shipping

- Shop-welded frames or modules reduce site work and improve weld quality.
- Module size is limited by container or flat-rack dimensions and by what the site crane can lift.
- Splice points should fall where the site can align and bolt them, not mid-cantilever.
- Marking must match the erection sequence, or the canopy sits on the ground waiting.
- Canopies are light in tonnage but bulky; packing and sea freight are a real cost line.

## Part 12: Responsibility boundary

| Item | Export supplier | Local side |
|---|---|---|
| Canopy structural design and detailing | Yes, with supplied layout and loads | Local engineer reviews and stamps |
| Fabrication, coating, marking, packing | Yes | — |
| Hazardous area classification | Inputs required | Fuel system designer and local authority |
| Foundations and apron | Base plate loads and details | Local civil contractor |
| Electrical installation and certification | Conduit and luminaire provisions | Licensed local electrician |
| Erection | Technical guidance | Local crew and crane |
| Permits and commissioning | Supporting documents | Owner and local authority |

## Part 13: Checklist before design freeze

- [ ] Dispenser layout and island dimensions confirmed.
- [ ] Tanker size and turning path checked against column positions.
- [ ] Required clearance height confirmed with local code.
- [ ] Hazardous area classification drawing received.
- [ ] Luminaire and signage layout agreed.
- [ ] Roof falls and outlet positions fixed.
- [ ] Downpipes kept clear of the fuelling area and traffic path.
- [ ] Impact protection positions agreed.
- [ ] Fire separation to shop and tank vents checked.
- [ ] Wind and seismic loads confirmed for the site.
- [ ] Foundation uplift design coordinated.
- [ ] Corrosion category and coating system selected.
- [ ] Module sizes checked against shipping and crane capacity.
- [ ] Erection sequence and marking scheme agreed.

## Part 14: Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Designing the roof before the dispenser layout | Columns land in the vehicle path |
| Ignoring tanker swept path | Canopy struck on the first delivery |
| Standard clearance copied from another country | Non-compliant or unusable height |
| No hazardous area drawing | Luminaires and cables rejected at inspection |
| Drainage outlets over the island | Water on customers and dispensers |
| No bollards where columns are close | Column damage and closure |
| Uplift ignored in foundation design | Roof movement in high wind |
| Splices placed mid-cantilever | Site alignment impossible |
| Signage added after fabrication | Unplanned load and wind area |

## Part 15: FAQ

**Question: What decides the height of a petrol station canopy?**
Answer: The required clearance above the tallest vehicle, the tanker envelope, the dispenser height and the local code. It is not a standard figure and should be confirmed with the local authority and the fuel system designer.

**Question: Can one canopy cover several dispenser islands?**
Answer: Yes, and it is common on busy sites. The trade-off is a longer span, more drainage runs and larger wind uplift, which usually means deeper members and heavier foundations.

**Question: Does the canopy need explosion-proof lighting?**
Answer: It depends on the hazardous area classification around the dispensers, vents and tanker connections. The fuel system designer and the local code define the zone; the luminaires and wiring must suit it.

**Question: Where should canopy water drain?**
Answer: Away from the islands, the fuelling area and the tanker path, through gutters and downpipes that run inside or behind the structure. The capacity depends on local rainfall intensity.

**Question: Do canopy columns need bollards?**
Answer: Where a column is close to the apron or the vehicle path, yes. Impact protection costs far less than a damaged column and a closed station.

**Question: Is a free-standing canopy better than one tied to the shop?**
Answer: A free-standing canopy avoids loading the shop frame and simplifies fire separation. A tied canopy saves columns on tight sites but transfers load and fire exposure into the building.

**Question: How is a canopy shipped?**
Answer: Usually as shop-welded frames or modules sized to containers, flat racks and the site crane. Splice points are set where the site can align and bolt them, and marking follows the erection sequence.
