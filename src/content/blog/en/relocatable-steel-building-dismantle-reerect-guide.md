A bolted steel building can often be dismantled and re-erected at another site, but “relocatable” does not mean “reusable in full”. What travels well is the primary frame — columns, rafters, bracing, purlins — provided it was designed with bolted connections and a clear marking system. What usually does not travel is the foundation, the cast-in anchors, the site-specific cladding, and anything damaged in handling. Whether a move is worth it depends on connection type, documentation, transport limits, a new foundation design and the labour available at both ends.

This guide is written for owners and contractors who already have a steel building and are asking whether to move it, sell it, or build new.

## Part 1: What actually survives a move

| Element | Usually reusable? | Condition that decides it |
|---|---|---|
| Primary columns and rafters | Yes | Bolted connections, no site welding, no damage at joints |
| End frames and bracing | Yes | Bracing members identifiable and undamaged |
| Purlins and girts | Yes | Screw holes not torn, sections not bent |
| Roof and wall cladding | Partly | Panel condition, cut lengths, whether the new building has the same geometry |
| Insulation | Rarely | Usually compressed, wet or damaged after removal |
| High-strength bolts | No | Single-use; replace with new bolts of the specified grade |
| Cast-in anchor bolts | No | Embedded in the old foundation |
| Base plates | Yes | Reusable if not distorted and if new anchors match |
| Flashing, trims, sealants | No | Cut to the old building, sealed to the old details |
| Roller doors and windows | Partly | Frame condition and whether the new opening sizes match |
| Mezzanine and stairs | Partly | Depends on whether they were bolted as a separate structure |

The pattern is simple: the more the original building was assembled with bolts and the better it was marked, the more of it survives.

## Part 2: Bolted versus welded — the decision that controls everything

| Frame type | Dismantling feasibility | Practical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Fully bolted portal frame | Good | Members can be unbolted and re-used at a new site |
| Bolted frame with site-welded bracing | Moderate | Welded bracing must be cut; replacements fabricated |
| Site-welded rafter splices | Poor | Splices cannot be undone cleanly; frame becomes scrap or a fixed asset |
| Mixed bolted and welded connections | Case by case | Survey every connection before committing |

If the building was welded on site, relocation usually stops being economical. That is worth knowing before a client buys a “relocatable” building, not after.

## Part 3: Marking and documentation decide the schedule

A dismantled frame without identification is a pile of steel. What the move needs:

- **Member marks** on every column, rafter, bracing and purlin, matching the erection drawings.
- **Erection drawings** for the original building, including the connection details.
- **Bolt schedule**, so replacements can be ordered to the correct grade and size.
- **Packing list** from the original shipment, which shows how the members were bundled.
- **Photos of connections** before dismantling, especially where the detail is unusual.
- **A survey** of member condition: bent flanges, torn screw holes, cracked welds, corrosion at the base.

Buildings assembled with a consistent match-mark system come down and go back up in the right order. Buildings without one cost weeks of measuring and guessing.

## Part 4: Dismantling sequence

1. **Survey and document** every member and connection before touching anything.
2. **Clear the roof**: remove cladding, insulation and flashings in a planned sequence so the frame is not left unbraced.
3. **Remove secondary steel** — purlins, girts, canopy, stairs — in a sequence that keeps the frame stable.
4. **Keep temporary bracing** in place until the frame is reduced to stable units.
5. **Dismantle in reverse erection order**, releasing bracing last.
6. **Cut only where cutting is unavoidable**, and record what was cut.
7. **Inspect and sort** members into reusable, repairable and scrap.
8. **Bundle and mark** for transport, matching the original packing logic.
9. **Record the as-dismantled list**, because it becomes the new bill of materials.

The most common mistake is removing bracing early to speed up the roof strip. That is when frames move.

## Part 5: Transport limits

| Constraint | What it affects | Planning action |
|---|---|---|
| Member length | Whether sections ship in containers or break bulk | Confirm route permits and trailer type before cutting |
| Container internal dimensions | Long rafters may need splices or open-top shipping | Check against the original member lengths |
| Road restrictions on the route | Escorts, permits, night movement | Confirm before promise dates |
| Handling at both ports | Damage risk on long members | Packing and lifting points agreed in advance |
| Storage at the new site | Corrosion and damage before erection | Dunnage, drainage and cover plan |

A frame that was originally shipped in containers can usually go back into containers, but only if the members were not cut during dismantling.

## Part 6: The new site is a new project

Relocation does not carry the old design basis with it. At the new site:

- **Foundations are new.** Column base positions must be surveyed and set out against the dismantled frame, not the old drawings alone.
- **Anchor bolts are new.** Base plate hole patterns must be verified against the actual plates.
- **Design loads may differ.** Wind, seismic, snow or crane requirements at the new location must be checked by the local engineer.
- **Permits are new.** The building may need a fresh submission even if it is the same steel.
- **Fire and egress requirements may differ** with the new occupancy and layout.
- **The foundation design must suit the reused frame**, including any splices or member substitutions made during the move.

This is why relocation is usually a design project with a dismantling task inside it, not a moving job.

## Part 7: What almost always has to be replaced

- **High-strength bolts** — do not re-use.
- **Anchor bolts and base grout** — new foundation, new anchors.
- **Roof and wall insulation** — usually damaged beyond re-use.
- **Flashings, trims, gutters and downpipes** — cut for the old geometry.
- **Sealants and gaskets** — age out during removal.
- **Damaged cladding panels** — order replacements before erection, not after.
- **Doors and windows** if the new openings differ.
- **Touch-up paint and galvanising repair** for handling damage.

Budgeting for these is the difference between a relocation that works and one that stalls at 80% erected.

## Part 8: Is relocation actually worth it?

| Situation | Relocation outlook | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Bolted frame, full drawings, short move | Usually worthwhile | Most of the frame and documentation survive |
| Bolted frame, no drawings, long move | Marginal | Survey and re-detailing costs eat the saving |
| Site-welded frame | Usually not | Cutting and re-fabrication approach new-build cost |
| Building needed at the new site within weeks | Usually not | Dismantling, transport and new foundations take longer than expected |
| Frame needed only partially (one bay, one wall) | Case by case | Partial reuse may still justify the move |
| Cladding and insulation in poor condition | Usually not for the enclosure | Replacement cost approaches a new envelope |

The honest comparison is not “old steel versus new steel”. It is “old steel plus survey, transport, new foundations, replacements and re-erection” versus “new building delivered and erected”.

## Part 9: Checklist before committing to a move

- [ ] Original erection drawings and connection details are available.
- [ ] Every member carries a legible mark matching those drawings.
- [ ] Connection type has been surveyed: bolted, welded or mixed.
- [ ] Member condition has been inspected, including base plates and flanges.
- [ ] Dismantling sequence and temporary bracing plan are written.
- [ ] Transport route, permits and trailer types are confirmed.
- [ ] New site survey and foundation design are appointed.
- [ ] New site design loads have been checked by the local engineer.
- [ ] Replacement bolts, anchors, insulation, flashings and panels are budgeted.
- [ ] New permits and fire strategy are understood.
- [ ] Labour and crane capacity exist at both sites.
- [ ] A realistic programme includes dismantling, shipping and re-erection.

## Part 10: Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Dismantling without photographs or marks | Re-erection becomes a measuring exercise |
| Re-using high-strength bolts | Connection capacity is no longer verifiable |
| Assuming old anchor positions can be reproduced | Base plates do not fit the new foundation |
| Cutting members to fit transport | New splices change the structural design |
| Ordering replacement cladding after erection starts | Building stays open and weather-exposed |
| Skipping the new-site load check | Frame may not suit the new wind, seismic or crane requirement |
| Promising a date based on the dismantling alone | Foundation and permit time is ignored |

## Part 12: FAQ

**Question: Can any steel building be relocated?**
Answer: No. A fully bolted frame with drawings and marks usually can. A site-welded frame usually cannot be dismantled economically, because the welded connections have to be cut and re-fabricated.

**Question: Can the foundation be moved too?**
Answer: No. Foundations and cast-in anchors stay at the old site. The new site needs a new foundation design, new anchor bolts and a fresh setting-out survey against the actual base plates.

**Question: Can the same bolts be re-used?**
Answer: High-strength structural bolts are normally single-use. Re-using them means the preload and connection capacity can no longer be verified, so replacements should be ordered to the specified grade and size.

**Question: What is the biggest cost driver in a relocation?**
Answer: Usually the combination of condition survey, re-detailing, transport and replacement materials — not the steel itself. Buildings with complete drawings and clear marks cost far less to move than buildings without them.

**Question: How long does a relocation take?**
Answer: It depends on the survey, the dismantling sequence, transport distance, new foundation works, permitting and re-erection labour. Treat it as a small project with its own programme rather than as a shipping task.

**Question: Can part of a building be moved?**
Answer: Sometimes. A single bay or one wall can be reused if the members are identifiable and the new layout suits the geometry. Partial moves usually need design verification for the reduced or altered frame.

**Question: Does the moved building need new approvals?**
Answer: In most jurisdictions, yes. The building is at a new location with a new foundation and possibly a different occupancy, so permits, structural verification and fire strategy usually have to be resubmitted locally.

## Part 13: Plan the move like a project

Relocation works when the frame is bolted, marked and documented, and when the new site is treated as a new design. It fails when it is treated as a haulage job. If a move is on the table, send the original drawings, member marks and site information for both locations, and the feasibility question can be answered before anything is cut.
