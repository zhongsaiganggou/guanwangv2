---Anchor bolt installation for a steel building runs through three checkpoints: the bolt cage and setting template go into the formwork before the concrete pour; position, level and thread protrusion are verified while the concrete is still workable; and the column base plate is levelled and grouted after the column is plumb. The tolerances that decide whether the frame lines up come from the approved drawings and the project specification, not from a universal number. Foundation design, the pour and the setting are normally the local team's scope; the steel supplier provides the bolt layout, the bolts when they are in scope, and installation technical guidance.

This guide covers the fixing options, the setting sequence, the tolerance checks, grouting, and how bolt groups are marked and packed for an export shipment.

## Part 1: Why anchor bolt setting decides the erection

Anchor bolts are the only connection between the steel structure and the foundation, and once concrete is poured they are hard to move:

- **Position errors** mean the base plate will not fit, or the column sits off the grid line.
- **Level errors** mean the column base is uneven, which shows up later as out-of-plumb columns.
- **Wrong protrusion** means nuts cannot be fitted or threads are damaged during lifting.
- **Poor grouting** means the base plate load never reaches the concrete properly.

Correcting a misplaced bolt group after the pour costs a design review, slotting or reaming of base plates, or post-installed anchors — all of which are decisions for the designer, not site shortcuts. The [foundation design guide](/en/blog/steel-structure-foundation-design-guide/) explains how bolt loads and footing sizes are set before any of this reaches the formwork.

## Part 2: Anchor bolt types and components

| Item | Typical use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Straight anchor bolt** | Standard column bases | Threaded top, hooked or embedded end; size per design |
| **L-shaped / J-bolt** | Where pull-out resistance is needed | Hook improves embedment; per approved drawings |
| **Anchor bolt cage** | Group of bolts tied together | Keeps bolts aligned and level during the pour |
| **Setting template** | Positioning the bolt group | Steel or plywood template at the top of the cage |
| **Leveling shims / nuts** | Adjusting base plate level | Temporary support under the base plate before grouting |
| **Grout** | Filling under the base plate | Non-shrink grout; thickness per spec |

Bolt diameter, embedment depth and spacing come from the approved drawings and depend on the loads. The [column base and anchor bolt design guide](/en/blog/steel-column-base-anchor-bolt-design-guide/) covers how those values are derived from the column reactions.

## Part 3: Cast-in bolts vs post-installed anchors

| Fixing method | When it suits | Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cast-in bolts with setting template** | New foundations and a normal bolt schedule | Bolt and concrete act together; position is fixed before the pour | Needs an accurate template and bracing; correction after the pour is limited |
| **Post-installed chemical anchors** | Missing or misplaced bolts, retrofit work | Can be installed after the concrete hardens | Capacity and approval depend on the designer and the anchor system; hole preparation and curing matter |
| **Sleeved or slotted base plate** | Where some later adjustment is expected | Allows movement at the base plate itself | Plate and hole geometry change; not a substitute for correct setting |

Cast-in is the default for new steel buildings because the bolt and the concrete work together from the start. Post-installed anchors are a design decision, taken when the geometry cannot be corrected any other way — not a routine site fix for a bolt that was set 20 mm off.

**Can anchor bolts be installed after the concrete is poured?** Yes, post-installed chemical or mechanical anchors can be used where bolts are missing or outside tolerance, but only with the designer's approval, because anchor capacity, edge distance and concrete condition all change the result. The steel supplier cannot relocate a column base on site; if the bolt group and the base plate no longer agree, the correction has to be reviewed against the foundation design before anything is drilled.

## Part 4: Installation sequence — before, during and after the pour

1. **Check the drawings** — confirm bolt diameter, embedment, protrusion and group spacing against the grid.
2. **Build the bolt cage** — fix the bolts to the cage frame at the correct spacing and level.
3. **Set the template** — position the cage in the formwork with the setting template; check plan position and level against the grid lines.
4. **Hold in place** — brace the cage so concrete placement cannot push it sideways or lift it.
5. **Pour concrete** — avoid direct impact on the bolts and the cage; vibrate around, not onto, the bolt group.
6. **Verify after the pour** — check position, level, protrusion and thread condition while the concrete is still workable.
7. **Correct while possible** — small adjustments are far easier before the concrete hardens.
8. **Protect the threads** — cap or wrap exposed threads before the next trade starts work.

## Part 5: Tolerance checks and what to do when bolts are out

| Check | What is verified | Where the limit comes from | If it is exceeded |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Plan position** | Distance of each bolt and the group to the grid lines | Approved foundation drawings and project specification; AISC 303, GB 50205 and EN 1090-2 each define default tolerances | Confirm with the designer before slotting, reaming or relocating |
| **Level and elevation** | Height of bolt tops and level across the group | Same drawings and specification | Correct with leveling nuts or shims only within the grout thickness allowed |
| **Protrusion and threads** | Nut height available, thread damage | Bolt size, nut and washer stack shown on drawings | Do not cut or weld without design approval; repair threads per the specification |
| **Base plate gap** | Clearance left for grout | Grout specification | Adjust shims; never bridge a large gap with grout beyond the permitted thickness |
| **Column plumb after grouting** | Verticality of the erected column | Erection tolerance in the project specification | Stop and review before further erection proceeds |

![Engineer checking anchor bolt protrusion with a tape measure after the pour (illustrative concept image)](/images/blog/steel-structure-anchor-bolt-installation-guide/check.jpg)

**What tolerance applies to anchor bolt placement?** There is no single worldwide figure. The limit that governs your project is stated on the approved foundation drawings and in the project specification. AISC 303, GB 50205 and EN 1090-2 each publish default tolerances, but the contract decides which one applies, and buyers should ask for the value in millimetres before the pour rather than discover it afterwards.

## Part 6: Grouting the base plate

1. Clean the base plate underside and the concrete surface of dust, oil and loose material.
2. Build a grout form around the base plate edge so grout cannot run away.
3. Mix non-shrink grout to the product data sheet, not by eye.
4. Pour or pump from one side so air escapes ahead of the grout.
5. Fill completely and finish level with the base plate edge.
6. Keep the grout damp and protected during curing, per the product instructions.

Grout thickness, strength and curing are specified by the designer. The same sequence applies to [crane rail installation and grouting](/en/blog/crane-rail-installation-grouting-guide/), where rail level is even less forgiving than base plate level.

## Part 7: Marking and packing bolt groups for export

Anchor bolts are small, heavy and easy to mix up, so they travel as identified sets rather than loose boxes:

- Bolt groups are bundled per column or grid line and tagged to match the column mark on the erection drawings.
- Nuts, washers and base plates for the same column travel with their group, so the site never has to match a bolt to a plate by trial.
- Threads are capped or wrapped for sea freight; boxes are marked with group code, quantity and weight.
- The packing list is cross-referenced to the erection mark schedule, so the receiving check is done by mark, not by counting pieces.
- Where the bolts are outside the steel scope, the same mark logic still has to be agreed with the foundation contractor.

The [erection marks guide](/en/blog/steel-structure-erection-marks-guide/) explains how those marks link fabrication, packing and site placement.

## FAQ

### How are anchor bolts installed for a steel building?

In three stages: set the bolt cage and setting template in the formwork before the pour, verify position, level and protrusion after the pour, then place the column base and grout the base plate once the column is plumb. Sizes, spacing and tolerances come from the approved drawings.

### Who supplies anchor bolts — the steel supplier or the local contractor?

Either, depending on the contract. Anchor bolts can be fabricated and packed with the steel scope, or sourced locally against the same layout. Foundation concrete and grout are normally local. What matters is that the layout and the mark logic are shared, so the two packages match.

### Why is a setting template used?

The template holds the bolt group at the correct spacing and level while concrete is placed. Without it, the cage moves under the pour and the base plate no longer fits the bolts.

### What happens if anchor bolts are out of position after the pour?

Small deviations are checked against the drawing, because base plates with standard holes allow limited adjustment. If the error exceeds the tolerance on the drawing, stop and get the designer's correction — slotting, reaming or post-installed anchors — before doing anything to the bolt.

### How much should anchor bolts protrude above the concrete?

Enough for the nut and washer stack with full thread engagement, as shown on the approved drawings. Too little and the nut will not seat; too much leaves thread exposed to damage and can foul the base plate.

### What is grouting under the base plate?

Non-shrink grout fills the gap between the base plate and the concrete so that the column load transfers across the full plate area. Thickness and mix follow the specification, and grout is poured from one side to avoid voids.

### Can post-installed anchors replace cast-in bolts?

Only with the designer's approval. Post-installed anchors change the capacity, edge distance and concrete-condition assumptions behind the foundation design, so they are a reviewed alternative, not a site substitution.

### Do anchor bolts need galvanizing?

It depends on the specification and the corrosion environment. Hot-dip galvanizing, zinc-rich coating or plain black bolts are all used; the drawing and the project's corrosion requirement decide which, and mixed coatings on one base plate should be avoided.
