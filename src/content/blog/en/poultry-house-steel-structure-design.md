A poultry house steel frame is structurally a simple portal frame. The difficulty is not the steel — it is the conditions the steel has to live with: a low clear height set by the ventilation plenum and ceiling, bay spacing aligned to feed and drinker lines, an envelope that must stay airtight and dry, and an interior atmosphere of ammonia and high humidity that shortens the life of any detail that traps moisture. Get those four right and the frame is ordinary work. Get them wrong and the house fails in its first cycle, not in its tenth year.

This guide is written for owners and contractors specifying poultry housing — broiler, layer or breeder — and for fabricators quoting a steel package against a farm system that someone else is designing.

## Part 1: Why a poultry house is not a scaled-down warehouse

| Decision | Warehouse | Poultry house |
|---|---|---|
| Clear height | Set by racking and forklift clearance | Set by ceiling, fan plenum and equipment access |
| Bay spacing | Set by racking modules and doors | Set by feed lines, drinker lines, partitions and cleanout access |
| Envelope | Weather-tight and ventilated | Weather-tight, airtight, insulated, washable |
| Interior atmosphere | Ambient, occasional humidity | Ammonia, litter dust, high humidity, wash-down cycles |
| Corrosion | External climate | Internal ammonia plus condensation |
| Loading | Doors, dock, floor slab | Feed delivery, bird movement, litter removal, service access |
| Failure cost | Lost storage time | Lost flock, disease risk, welfare compliance |

The structural consequence: the frame is standard, but the envelope, openings and details are not. A warehouse detail that leaves a ledge, a trapped cavity or an unsealed penetration is acceptable in a warehouse and a problem in a poultry house.

## Part 2: The four decisions that must be settled before steel is ordered

1. **Production system.** Floor-reared broiler, cage or aviary layer, breeder housing — each has a different ceiling height, access pattern and equipment layout.
2. **Ventilation strategy.** Tunnel ventilation, cross ventilation, or a combination, with or without evaporative cooling. This fixes the fan wall, inlet positions and the plenum depth.
3. **Climate and site.** Design temperature, humidity, wind and snow govern the loads and the insulation; ammonia and humidity govern the coatings.
4. **House count and site layout.** One house or a cluster; service roads, feed silos, biosecurity separation and future expansion.

None of these are steel decisions, but all of them change the steel. A fabricator quoting before they are settled is quoting a shape, not a building.

## Part 3: Clear height and eave height

Clear height in a poultry house is rarely driven by the birds. It is driven by what has to fit above and around them.

| Driver | What it does to the frame | What the buyer must confirm |
|---|---|---|
| Ceiling or insulated roof lining | Determines whether the frame carries a separate ceiling system | Ceiling type, weight per area, fixing method |
| Fan plenum and tunnel fan wall | Adds depth at the end wall; may need a separate frame | Fan diameter, count, wall arrangement |
| Evaporative cooling pads | Adds a wall zone with water and higher humidity | Pad height and location |
| Feeding and drinking lines | Determines the height of the service zone and hopper clearance | Line layout and hopper type |
| Litter and cleanout access | Determines door height and internal clearance at the ends | Machine type used for litter removal |
| Local code and welfare rules | May set minimum clear height or maximum stocking density | Local authority requirements |

The eave height follows from the tallest of these, not from a standard portal-frame module. If the plenum is deep and the ceiling is insulated, the frame may need a deeper end section than a warehouse of the same span.

## Part 4: Bay spacing and the column grid

Bay spacing in poultry housing is an equipment decision before it is a structural one.

- **Feed lines and drinker lines** run the length of the house; column positions must not interrupt the service walkway or the line supports.
- **Partitions and end walls** between houses or between sections should land on a frame line, not between frames.
- **Doors** for feed delivery, bird movement, litter removal and service access each need a frame line and a detail.
- **Roof drainage** spacing follows the bay, and the gutter must handle the local rainfall intensity.
- **Future extension** along the length costs less if the bay module repeats cleanly.

A grid chosen purely for steel economy — the fewest frames per metre — often produces a house where every door, partition and line support needs a secondary frame. The lower-cost steel package can be the more expensive building.

## Part 5: Roof, ceiling and condensation

A poultry house has two envelopes: the roof and the ceiling. Both matter.

| Element | Function | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|
| Roof sheeting | Weather protection and drainage | Profile chosen without regard to walk-on access and wind uplift |
| Insulation | Thermal control and condensation prevention | Insulation compressed at purlins, creating cold bridges |
| Vapour control | Keeps moisture out of the insulation | No vapour barrier, or one that is punctured at every fixing |
| Ceiling | Separates the bird zone from the roof void | Ceiling fixed directly to purlins without a service zone |
| Air seals | Keeps the ventilation system controllable | Unsealed penetrations at fans, inlets, feed lines and cables |

Condensation is the quiet failure mode. Warm, humid air rising into a cold roof void condenses on the underside of the sheeting, drips onto litter and into insulation, and starts corrosion on purlins that nobody sees until the roof is opened. A vapour barrier and a continuous insulated envelope cost little at construction and are almost impossible to add later.

## Part 6: Ventilation openings are structural openings

Every fan, inlet and cooling pad is a hole in the envelope, and each hole needs a frame.

- **Tunnel fan wall:** the fans are heavy and vibrate; the wall needs its own framing rather than cladding alone.
- **Inlets:** positioned to the system design, with frames that can be sealed when the house is washed.
- **Cooling pads:** water, higher humidity and a wetted surface; the frame and coatings around them must tolerate both.
- **Ridge and eave details:** must not let wind-driven rain into the void while still allowing the ventilation strategy to work.
- **Service doors and hatches:** every one needs a frame line and a closable, washable detail.

The ventilation design belongs to the farm system supplier or the local poultry engineer. The fabricator's job is to make the openings structurally sound, sealed and corrosion-protected, and to flag when the proposed opening conflicts with the frame.

## Part 7: Corrosion is a service-life decision

A poultry house interior combines ammonia, humidity, litter dust and periodic wash-down. That is a more aggressive environment than most outdoor industrial sites.

| Measure | Why it matters in poultry housing |
|---|---|
| Hot-dip galvanized primary frame | Ammonia and condensation attack bare steel first at joints and trapped areas |
| Coating system suited to the local environment | Interior and exterior exposure differ; one system may not cover both |
| Avoid trapped cavities and unsealed hollow sections | Condensation collects where it cannot drain or dry |
| Stainless or coated fixings | Dissimilar-metal contact and coating damage start corrosion at every screw |
| Washable, smooth surfaces | Litter dust accumulates on ledges; rough surfaces cannot be cleaned properly |
| Drainage at every low point | Water that cannot leave becomes the start of the next corrosion cell |

The right specification depends on the local climate, the wash-down regime, the expected service life and the coatings available in the market — not on a single global standard. What can be stated generally: details that trap water will fail earlier in a poultry house than in a warehouse, and the repair cost is higher because the birds have to be moved out.

## Part 8: Floor, foundation and the load path

Poultry houses usually sit on a concrete slab with the steel frame anchored to it. Three things then matter:

1. **The slab is the floor of the production space**, not just a foundation. Its levelness, drainage slope and joints affect litter condition and cleaning.
2. **The frame loads are concentrated at column bases**; the slab must be designed for them, and for the equipment and feed loads that arrive later.
3. **The envelope must connect to the slab without a gap.** A gap at the base is an air leak, a water path and a place where rodents and litter collect.

Foundation design, slab detailing and civil work belong to the local engineer. The fabricator supplies base plates, anchor bolts and the interface details, and needs the slab and anchor setting-out confirmed before production.

## Part 9: Biosecurity, washing and cleanability

Between flocks, the house is washed and disinfected. Details that cannot be cleaned become a disease risk.

| Detail | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Column and frame surfaces | Smooth, coated, no ledges that hold litter |
| Ceiling and insulation joints | Sealed and washable, not absorbent |
| Doors and hatches | Closable, sealed, with washable frames |
| Cable and pipe penetrations | Sealed and accessible for inspection |
| Floor joints and corners | Coved or sealed, cleanable |
| Drainage | Sized for wash-down water, with traps where required |

This is where a poultry-house steel package differs most from a warehouse package: the same frame, but every detail judged by whether it can be cleaned.

## Part 10: Multi-house sites and layout

A single house is a building project; a cluster is a site plan.

- **Separation between houses** for biosecurity, service access and fire spread.
- **Feed silo positions** and delivery vehicle access, including turning and unloading.
- **Service roads** that stay usable in wet weather.
- **Utilities** — power for fans, water for cooling and washing, backup generation.
- **Future expansion** — leaving the length extension and a second house line possible without moving infrastructure.

The steel scope usually stops at the building envelope. The site plan is the owner's and the local engineer's, but it should be fixed before the first house is built, because retrofitting services around operating houses is expensive and disruptive.

## Part 11: What the steel package includes and excludes

| Item | Usually in the steel package | Usually not |
|---|---|---|
| Primary frame, secondary framing, bracing | Yes | — |
| Roof and wall cladding, insulation, vapour barrier | Yes, if specified | — |
| Base plates, anchor bolts, embedment details | Yes | — |
| Openings framed for fans, inlets, doors | Yes, to the ventilation layout issued | Fan and inlet equipment |
| Gutters and downpipes | Yes, if specified | Site drainage |
| Ceiling system | Only if specified | Poultry-specific ceiling and equipment |
| Ventilation design, fan selection, controls | No | Farm system supplier / local engineer |
| Foundation and slab | Interface only | Local civil contractor |
| Erection, commissioning, biosecurity validation | Technical guidance | Local team and farm |

The gap between "steel package" and "working poultry house" is where most disputes sit. It should be written into the enquiry, not discovered on site.

- [ ] Production system confirmed: broiler, layer, breeder; floor, cage or aviary.
- [ ] Ventilation strategy confirmed, including fan wall, inlets and cooling.
- [ ] Clear height and eave height derived from ceiling, plenum and equipment.
- [ ] Bay spacing aligned with feed lines, drinker lines, partitions and doors.

## Part 14: FAQ

**Question: Can a standard steel warehouse frame be used for a poultry house?**
Structurally often yes, but the envelope is different. Clear height, airtightness, insulation, ventilation openings and corrosion protection have to be specified for poultry, not inherited from a warehouse.

**Question: What clear height does a poultry house need?**
It depends on the production system, the ceiling, the fan plenum, cooling pads and local rules. There is no single global figure; the height should be derived from the system design.

**Question: Why does corrosion matter more in a poultry house?**
The interior combines ammonia, high humidity and wash-down. Details that trap water corrode earlier than in a warehouse, and repairs are disruptive because the flock has to be removed.

**Question: Who designs the ventilation system?**
The farm system supplier or the local poultry engineer. The steel fabricator frames and seals the openings and flags conflicts with the structure.

**Question: Is a ceiling necessary, or can the roof be the ceiling?**
Both approaches exist. A separate ceiling with a service zone gives better control of ventilation and condensation, but adds structure and cost. The decision belongs with the system design.

**Question: Can the house be extended later?**
Usually yes, along the length, if the bay module repeats and the end frame is designed as a permanent frame. Extension during an operating cycle is a biosecurity problem, so plan it between flocks.

**Question: What should be confirmed before the steel is ordered?**
Production system, ventilation layout, clear height, bay spacing, slab and anchor setting-out, and the corrosion specification. Without these, the frame is a guess.
