**Direct answer:** a water or wastewater treatment plant rarely needs a large steel building. It needs steel in the places concrete tanks cannot serve — covers over process tanks, walkways and pipe bridges between tanks, and small buildings for blowers, chemical dosing and control equipment. Whether that steel is still serviceable ten years later is decided by three things: the corrosion zone the steel sits in, the access operators actually need, and how the steel connects to the concrete tank rim. Section sizes, coating systems and panel details all follow from those three.

This guide is written for the buyer or EPC engineer who has to specify, quote and install those steel items on a plant that is often already running.

## 1. Where steel earns its place on a water plant

Concrete is the default material for the tanks themselves. Steel appears where the job is span, access, speed of installation or future removal — not where the job is mass.

| Structure item | What it does | What drives the design |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed tank cover | Keeps rain, debris and birds out; controls odour and algae in hot climates | Clear span between tank walls, gas exposure under the cover, access hatches |
| Removable cover panels | Give maintenance access to mixers, aerators and sensors | Panel weight and lifting method, joint sealing, replaceability |
| Pipe bridge or access walkway | Connects tanks; carries pipes, valves and cable trays | Pipe and cable loads, live load, deflection limits, expansion between structures |
| Blower room, dosing room, control room | Houses equipment and chemicals | Ventilation, chemical vapour, fire separation, door and removable panel sizes |
| Sludge dewatering platform, stairs, guardrails | Operator access and equipment support | Grating specification, fall protection, wet and chemical exposure |
| Roof or enclosure over an open tank | Algae and odour control in hot climates | Wind uplift over a large span, condensation, maintenance access to the underside |

Most enquiries we receive for this type of work are not for one big building. They are for a package of covers, walkways, stairs and two or three small equipment buildings that have to fit an existing concrete layout.

## 2. The corrosion zone decides the coating, not the country

Two plants in the same country can need very different steel protection. What matters is where the steel sits, not the flag on the drawing title block.

| Zone | Typical exposure | What it changes in the detail |
|---|---|---|
| Permanently or intermittently immersed | Continuous wetting, silt, biological activity | Avoid crevices and unsealed pockets; prefer details that can be taken out and replaced |
| Splash zone at the tank rim | Wet–dry cycling, chlorides where relevant | Edge protection and a drip detail so water does not run back onto the steel face |
| Headspace above sewage | Hydrogen sulphide plus high humidity | Sealed joints, accessible repainting points, higher-build systems or stainless where the specification requires |
| Chemical dosing and storage room | Acid or alkali vapours, spills | Ventilation, coating selected against the actual chemical list, no trapped moisture at floor level |
| General outdoor, inland | UV, rain, temperature cycling | Standard exterior system, joint details that drain |
| Coastal or marine site | Salt-laden air | Higher environment category; fasteners and edge details matter as much as the coating |

The system itself is set by the project's coating specification and the durability the client asks for — environment classification such as ISO 12944 is the usual language for this. A supplier should not pick the coating class on the client's behalf, and a buyer should not accept a quotation that names a colour but not a system, a surface preparation standard and a dry film thickness.

## 3. Tank covers: the details buyers most often miss

A steel cover looks like a lid. In service it is an operating platform, a gas boundary and a maintenance access route at the same time.

**Span and support.** Cover framing normally spans between tank walls. When the span is long, a light frame with an internal beam, or supports bearing on the tank wall, keeps the section sizes practical. On existing tanks, the wall may not be designed for a new horizontal or vertical reaction — that has to be checked by the local engineer before the cover is designed.

**Removal strategy.** The question to answer first is which equipment must come out of the tank, how heavy it is, and what lifting path it needs. Panels sized so two people can lift them are simple to make, but costly in use if a mixer needs a crane every time.

**Venting.** A closed cover traps gas. Vent area and position normally come from the process designer, not the steel fabricator. Covers installed without a vent plan are a common cause of corrosion on the underside and of safety incidents during maintenance.

**Walking surface and fall protection.** If operators walk on the cover, the live load has to be stated, the walking surface has to be a proper grating or plate with slip resistance, and guardrails have to meet the local height and load rules. Where access is only for occasional inspection, the requirement may be different — but it should be written down, not assumed.

**Connection to concrete.** Three options exist in practice: cast-in or embedded plates, post-installed anchors, and a bearing detail on a levelling layer. Each has a different tolerance and a different future. Embedded plates give a clean bolted connection but must be placed before the concrete pour. Post-installed anchors suit retrofit work but depend on the concrete condition and the anchor system's approval for the exposure. Neither is "better" without the survey.

**The rim is a water detail, not only a steel detail.** Rain running behind the steel edge, or water standing on a bearing, will find the joint. The cover needs a defined drainage path, a flashing or upstand, and a sealant that the site can actually apply correctly.

### Adding a cover to a tank that is already running

1. **Survey the rim.** Measure elevations around the tank, not just the nominal dimension; concrete tolerances are usually wider than steel fabrication tolerances.
2. **Fix the interface.** Choose embedded plates, anchors or a bearing detail, and design a levelling provision.
3. **Split the cover into transportable panels.** Size against container limits and the crane available on site.
4. **Fabricate and coat.** Coat before assembly; site touch-up should be the exception, not the plan.
5. **Install in a low-flow window.** Sequence the work around the plant's operating cycle and keep the tank vented while panels are placed.
6. **Test the seal and hand over the walkway.** Water test the rim detail before the plant returns to normal level, then check guardrails and access points.

## 4. The small buildings do most of the equipment work

Blower rooms, dosing rooms and control rooms are small in footprint and dense in requirement.

- **Equipment loads and vibration.** Blowers and pumps need bases that suit the supplier's data, and isolation where the supplier requires it.
- **Replacement routes.** A door that clears the largest removable part costs less than dismantling a wall later.
- **Ventilation and heat.** A blower room is a heat source in a hot climate; air change and louvre positions belong in the brief given to the steel supplier.
- **Chemical separation.** Storage of acid or chlorine-related chemicals next to a control room is normally a fire and separation question under local code, not a layout preference.
- **Cable routing.** Cable trays above floor level, with pull points and clearances, avoid later cutting through the structure.
- **Floor and plinth details.** Pumps sit on plinths; the plinth and the steel floor or slab interface should be drawn once, not improvised on site.

## 5. The interface is where these projects go wrong

Most of the cost and delay in water plant steelwork sits in the interface with concrete and with process equipment — not in the fabrication itself.

- **Rim level tolerance.** Concrete tanks are rarely level to steel tolerances; shims, a levelling layer or an adjustable bearing are normal, not a defect.
- **Late or misplaced embedded plates.** Once concrete is poured, the options narrow to anchors or a redesign.
- **Differential movement.** A steel frame bearing partly on a tank wall and partly on a separate foundation moves differently; the detail has to allow it.
- **Drawing responsibility.** Process drawings show equipment; they rarely show the steel that supports or covers it. Someone has to own that interface drawing — usually the steel detailer working from both sets.
- **Site lifting.** Panel weights that suit a factory crane may not suit the crane the contractor has on site.

## 6. What to prepare before asking for a quotation

- [ ] Tank layout plan with wall thickness and rim elevations
- [ ] Cover scope: fixed, removable, or a mix, and which hatches are needed
- [ ] Equipment list inside the tank that must be lifted out, with weights
- [ ] Vent size and position from the process designer
- [ ] Live loads on covers and walkways, including maintenance equipment
- [ ] Chemical exposure list for each room or zone
- [ ] Coating specification, environment classification and durability target
- [ ] Connection method permitted at the rim (embedded plate, anchor, bearing)
- [ ] Local rules on guardrail height and load, fall protection and fire separation
- [ ] Transport and site lifting limits: panel size, panel weight, crane capacity

A quotation built on this list can be compared line by line. A quotation without it will look low-cost on paper and expensive on site.

## 8. Quick answers for procurement teams

**What steel structures does a wastewater treatment plant actually need?**
Covers over process tanks, walkways and pipe bridges between structures, stairs and guardrails, and small buildings for blowers, dosing and control equipment. Large steel buildings are uncommon on a water plant; the steel scope is usually a package of smaller items tied to the concrete layout.

**Can a steel cover be added to an existing tank without stopping the plant?**
Usually yes, if the work is split into transportable panels, the rim is surveyed first and installation is sequenced into a low-flow window. The tank stays vented during installation, and the rim seal is tested before normal level is restored.

**Which coating should be used on steel above sewage?**
It is set by the project specification, not by the supplier. The headspace above sewage combines hydrogen sulphide with high humidity, which is a different exposure from general outdoor steel, and the durability class the client asks for determines the surface preparation and dry film thickness.

**What drawings are needed to quote tank covers and walkways?**
A tank plan with rim elevations, the equipment list that needs access, vent requirements, the coating specification and the permitted connection method at the rim. Without rim elevations the quotation is an estimate, not a price.

## 9. FAQ

**Do water plants need structural steel at all?**
Small open tanks are often all concrete. Steel becomes relevant where a cover is needed, where access has to cross between structures, or where equipment needs a small building that can be erected quickly and extended later.

**Can people walk on a steel tank cover?**
Yes, if it is designed for a stated live load and fitted with a proper walking surface and guardrails to the local rule. A cover designed only for its own weight and wind should not be treated as a walkway.

**Which steel grade is normal for this work?**
It depends on the specification and the zone. Carbon structural steel with a defined coating system is common for covers and walkways; stainless or duplex grades appear where the client's specification calls for them in aggressive headspace or chemical areas. The grade and the coating should be decided together.

**How are large covers delivered to site?**
As bolted panels split to suit container dimensions and the site crane. Panels are marked to a layout drawing, sealed at joints on site, and coated before dispatch so that site work is assembly rather than painting.

**What causes the biggest delay on these projects?**
Interface information: rim elevations that arrive after fabrication starts, equipment lists that change, and coating specifications approved late. Steel fabrication is predictable; the concrete and process data around it usually are not.
