Steel structure for mining equipment is not a building with a machine dropped inside it. The screening station, crusher station, conveyor gallery and bin support are all designed backwards from the equipment: the vendor's static and dynamic loads, the anchor bolt pattern, the isolation system and the maintenance envelope set the frame geometry, the member sizes and the connection type. A fabricator can detail and build that frame accurately only when those data arrive before the shop drawings are frozen. What follows is the interface list, the load items that most often go missing, and how such a package is split for shipping and erection.

The steel package is usually priced before the equipment is fully engineered, so it is quoted on an assumed load set. Every assumption that is not written down becomes a change order later; the point of this guide is to name them early enough that they cost nothing.

## What the steel package actually contains

"Mining equipment steel" is a category, not one structure. The table below separates the structures that are usually bundled into one purchase package, and shows which load source drives each one.

| Structure | What it carries | Load source that drives the steel | Interface that usually goes wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| Screening station / screen house | multi-deck screens, chutes, walkways, maintenance crane | dead load plus out-of-balance dynamic force | isolation mounts fixed to a frame that is too flexible |
| Crusher station | crusher, feed hopper, discharge conveyor, liner access | high impact from dumped material and torque reaction at start-up | anchor pattern and the clearance needed to lift the rotor |
| Conveyor gallery and transfer tower | belt, drive unit, material on the belt, walkway | belt tension reactions, material load, wind on the gallery | gallery width versus walkway and head/tail pulley access |
| Bin or silo support structure | bin shell plus stored material | large vertical load with eccentric discharge | discharge clearance and alignment of the bin support ring |
| Chute and hopper supports | transfer points between belts and screens | impact and abrasion | access to replace wear liners |
| Equipment platform or skid frame | pumps, drives, transformers, compressors | equipment weight plus running vibration | handrail, stair and cable routing interfaces |

A single order often mixes four or five of these. Each has a different governing load, so each is confirmed separately rather than covered by one general note on the drawing.

## The equipment data that fixes the steel

The steel cannot be optimised before the equipment is known. The table below is the data set that a fabricator needs from the equipment vendor or the process engineer, in the form it is actually used.

| Data from the equipment side | Where it lands in the steel | What happens when it arrives late |
|---|---|---|
| Static load at each support point | column and beam sizing, base plate size | connections and base plates redone, or plates field-drilled |
| Dynamic force, frequency and direction | frame stiffness, bracing, isolation detail | frame resonance, cracked welds at the support brackets |
| Start-up and braking torque reaction | tie-downs, bracing, anchor design | skid movement, loosened anchors, belt misalignment |
| Anchor bolt pattern, diameter and grade | base plate hole layout, embedment plates | anchors relocated on site, plates torch-cut |
| Maintenance envelope: rotor removal, liner change, crane hook height | platform layout, openings, local strengthening | equipment cannot be serviced without cutting the steel |
| Discharge geometry and chute angles | chute supports, openings in the platform | material build-up, blocked transfer points |
| Operating temperature and duty cycle | steel grade selection, coating system | distortion, coating breakdown, or over-specified steel |
| Wear liner thickness and replacement method | clearances at chutes and bins | liners cannot be inserted or removed |

The practical rule is simple: if a number is not written down by the party that owns it, it does not belong in a quotation as a fixed allowance. It belongs in the quotation as an assumption to be confirmed, with the steel detailed only after confirmation.

## Why screens and crushers are the hard part

A vibrating screen does not behave like a static load. The screen body is mounted on springs or rubber isolators and driven by an eccentric shaft, so it produces a cyclic force at the operating frequency. The support frame below it is a dynamic system: its stiffness and mass determine whether that force is transmitted into the structure or absorbed by the isolation.

Two things follow. First, the isolation mounts are supplied and warranted by the screen manufacturer, and their required static deflection and dynamic characteristics belong to the equipment package, not to the steel package. Second, the steel frame under the isolators has to be stiff enough that the frame's own natural frequency stays away from the screen's operating frequency. A frame that is flexible enough to "feel" the screen will amplify movement instead of isolating it, and the symptom appears as loosened bolts, cracked brackets or a screen that will not hold its stroke.

A crusher has a different problem: impact. Dumped material produces a shock load outside the steady-state case, and the anchor and tie-down detail has to survive start-up torque as well as running load. Neither the shock factor nor the torque reaction can be assumed by the fabricator; both come from the vendor load schedule.

## Bolted or welded on site

Connection choice on a mining project is driven less by structural preference than by where the work can be done.

| Connection approach | Suitable when | Advantage | Penalty to plan for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shop welded, site bolted with match marks | modules are pre-assembled and shipped | bolting speed, shop weld quality, limited hot work on site | tighter fabrication tolerance, disciplined match marking and trial assembly |
| Site welded, stick-built | access is good and hot work is permitted | geometry can be adjusted to as-built foundations | hot work permits, weld inspection, coating repair at every joint |
| Bolted skid-to-frame connection | equipment may be replaced or removed | equipment can be swapped without cutting steel | bolt loosening under vibration, so locking devices and a re-torque interval are needed |

On a live plant, or in a country where hot work permits are slow and inspection is expensive, the shop-welded and site-bolted route is normally the lower-risk one — provided the modules were trial-assembled in the shop. If trial assembly is skipped, the match marks are only a promise, and the bolted joint becomes the place where accumulated error is discovered.

## Modular limits: what can actually ship

The shipping method decides the module size long before the crane is discussed. A general-purpose container has roughly 12.0 m of internal length, 2.35 m of internal width and about 2.7 m of internal height, with a door opening slightly smaller than the internal section. Anything beyond that is breakbulk, flat rack or a custom transport frame. Exact limits depend on carrier, route and port handling, so they are confirmed per project rather than assumed.

| Module | Likely shipping route | Design decision it forces |
|---|---|---|
| Conveyor gallery section | container if sectional, otherwise breakbulk | split points at bolted flanges, and a joint that can be aligned on site |
| Screen house frame | containerised in columns and beams, or pre-assembled frame | whether to trial-assemble, and how to mark every member |
| Bin support structure | usually breakbulk or flat rack | lifting points and transport bracing designed into the frame |
| Chute and hopper | container, nested | wear liner fitted in the shop or on site |
| Equipment skid | container if it fits, otherwise flat rack | protection of the equipment mounting faces during transport |

Two details decide whether a module arrives usable: designed lifting points, and transport bracing calculated for the road or sea leg. Both belong in the fabrication drawings and cost less than repairing a distorted module on site.

## Corrosion and abrasion are two different problems

Mining environments combine dust, moisture, washdown and abrasive material, but abrasion is not solved by paint. A coating system selected by corrosivity category — ISO 12944 C4 or C5 is common for industrial and coastal sites, and the category has to be agreed with the owner — protects the steel surface from corrosion. It does nothing for a chute that is being hit by ore at speed.

Abrasion is handled by sacrificing material: wear plates, replaceable liners, thicker sections at impact points, and geometry that lets material build a protective dead bed instead of sliding over the plate. The steel design has to leave room for those liners and for the handling equipment that will replace them. A chute that is fully welded with no liner access is a maintenance problem built into the structure.

## Erection on a remote or live site

The erection sequence is where the equipment interface becomes physical: the anchor bolts in the foundations are surveyed, the steel is set, and the equipment is aligned on top of it. If the steel is fabricated before the foundation survey is available, the base plates become the adjustment mechanism, and adjustment has limits.

Four points decide the site phase:

1. Survey the foundations and the equipment anchor positions before shop drawings are released, not after the steel arrives.
2. Fix the erection sequence around the equipment delivery dates — the frame that receives the screen usually has to be complete before the screen is lifted.
3. Design the temporary bracing for the erection stage, then remove it only when the permanent bracing and the equipment are in place.
4. Keep alignment records for the equipment support faces as the reference for commissioning.

## Common mistakes

- Quoting a screen house from a general building frame, with no dynamic load case and no isolation detail.
- Treating the equipment vendor's anchor pattern as a detail to be confirmed later, then field-drilling base plates.
- Designing the platform without the maintenance envelope, so the rotor or the liner cannot be removed.
- Shipping modules without trial assembly, so the match marks do not survive the site bolting stage.
- Using a coating system as abrasion protection, then repairing the same chute every year.
- Fabricating before the foundation survey, and treating base plate shims as the answer to every tolerance problem.

| Site works | installation technical guidance, bolting sequence advice | foundations, civil works, local labour, cranes, permits |
| Authority | export documentation support | local permits, inspections and authority submissions |

The equipment data sits between three parties: the vendor owns the loads, the local engineer owns the approval, and the fabricator owns whether the steel as built can receive the equipment. The fabricator should never quietly fill a missing load with a guess.

## FAQ

**Does the steel fabricator design the equipment support structure?**
Normally no. The structural design and its approval belong to a registered engineer in the project country. The fabricator converts that design into detailing and shop drawings, and comments on buildability, transport and erection. On request, ZhongSai coordinates the interface between the equipment data and the steel drawings, but the design approval stays with the local engineer.

**What equipment data is needed before fabrication starts?**
At minimum: static load at each support point, dynamic force with frequency and direction, anchor bolt pattern and grade, maintenance envelope, discharge geometry, and the isolation system data. If any of these is missing, the steel can still be quoted, but it should be quoted as an assumption and detailed only after confirmation.

**Can a vibrating screen be mounted on a normal steel building frame?**
It can be mounted on steel, but not on a frame that was designed as a building. The frame under the screen has to be stiff enough to keep its natural frequency away from the screen's operating frequency, and it has to accept the isolation mounts at the positions the screen manufacturer specifies. A general-purpose platform frame usually fails on both counts.

**How large a module can be shipped?**
Container shipping limits a module to roughly the internal dimensions of the container, and the door opening is smaller than the internal section. Larger modules go breakbulk, on a flat rack, or on a transport frame. The exact limits depend on the carrier, route and port equipment, so the split points should be agreed before fabrication, not after.

**Who supplies anchor bolts and embedded plates?**
This is a contract decision, not a technical one. In many projects the equipment vendor supplies the equipment anchors and the civil contractor installs them; steel embedments such as base plates and support brackets are supplied with the steel package. What matters is that one party is named as responsible for the setting-out, and that the steel drawings show the same pattern as the equipment drawing.

**How is wear protection handled on chutes and bins?**
By replaceable wear liners, wear plates or thicker sacrificial sections, with access designed into the structure. Coating protects against corrosion, not against sliding ore. The liner thickness and replacement method have to be known before the steel is detailed, because they change clearances and platform openings.
