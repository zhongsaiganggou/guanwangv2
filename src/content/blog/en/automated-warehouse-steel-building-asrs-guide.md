---An automated warehouse is not a taller version of a normal warehouse. Once stacker cranes and shuttles are in the aisles, the building must meet tolerances and deflection limits a general shed never sees, the column grid must follow the rack bay rather than the other way round, and the fire strategy must be agreed before the frame is detailed. The costliest mistake is ordering steel before racking supplier, automation vendor and steel designer have exchanged interface data.

This guide sets out what actually changes, which decisions belong to whom, and the inputs a buyer must assemble before an AS/RS building can be engineered and priced.


## First decision: does the racking carry the building, or does the building carry the racking?

Two structural models exist, and they are not interchangeable. Choosing between them decides who designs what, who carries which load, and how the project is sequenced.

| Model | What it means | Who designs the structure | Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rack-supported (racking is the structure) | The racking uprights form the primary frame; cladding, roof and sometimes the crane rails are carried by the rack | Racking / automation supplier, usually to its own proprietary system | Less steel duplication, tight integration with the cranes, common for very high bay | Building envelope and access details depend on the rack supplier; a steel fabricator's scope shrinks to cladding supports and secondary framing; local approval of a proprietary system can be slow |
| Building-supported (independent steel frame, racking stands inside) | A conventional steel frame carries roof and walls; racking is a separate structure standing on the floor slab | Steel frame designer and racking supplier separately, with an interface check | Clear scope split, familiar to local approving engineers, easier to phase and to change racking later | Two structures to buy; the frame must be laid out around the rack bay; the slab carries the rack loads |
| Hybrid (frame plus rack stiffening) | Steel frame plus selected rack connections for stability | Both parties, with a written interface agreement | Adds stiffness in a very tall bay | Responsibility blurs easily; connections must be jointly specified and inspected |

For most export projects the building-supported model is the safer starting point, because the steel scope, the approval path and the foundation design stay conventional. The rack-supported model is usually chosen when the bay is very high and the automation supplier insists on controlling the whole structure. Either way, the buyer must state which model applies in the enquiry — a fabricator quoting a conventional frame for a rack-supported project is quoting the wrong building.

## What automation actually imposes on the frame

The loads from an automated warehouse are not dramatically heavier than a normal warehouse. What changes is precision.

- **Clear height and the height-to-width ratio.** A tall, narrow building behaves differently in wind and seismic events, and the taller the bay, the more the frame relies on a properly designed lateral system rather than on the portal action that works for a low shed. Height also multiplies wall and roof area, which is a cost consequence, not just a structural one.
- **Deflection limits at the stacker crane.** Crane rails and guide systems tolerate far less movement than a general structural beam. The limit comes from the automation supplier, not from a generic steel code, and it is usually expressed at the rail, under specified operating loads. A frame that is strong enough can still be too flexible.
- **Tolerance stack-up.** The frame has a fabrication and erection tolerance, the slab has a flatness tolerance, the rack has its own tolerance, and the crane has an installation tolerance. Each is acceptable on its own; added together at the top of a tall bay they can exceed what the crane needs. Tolerances must be allocated as a chain, agreed before fabrication.
- **Floor flatness and levelness under the rack.** Very narrow aisle equipment needs a floor that meets the system supplier's specification over the whole travel length, not on average. Floor flatness is a concrete scope item, but it is the steel designer who must align column base levels and slab joints with it.
- **Column grid driven by the rack bay.** If the steel grid and the rack bay disagree, one of them has to absorb the difference — usually with extra framing or a non-standard rack. Fix the rack bay first, then the column grid, then the bracing positions.
- **Fire strategy for a high bay.** High clear height, dense storage and limited access change sprinkler design, and in-rack sprinklers or ESFR may be required depending on the local code and the authority. The frame's fire protection cannot be finalised until that strategy is agreed.
- **Envelope performance at height.** A tall envelope has more area for condensation and air leakage, and maintenance access to roof plant and rooflights becomes a design requirement rather than an afterthought.
- **Erection sequence.** Racking is installed after the slab has cured and after the frame has been surveyed; installing racking against an unverified frame simply transfers the error into the crane rails.

## The interface data that must be exchanged before quotation

An AS/RS project fails at the interfaces long before it fails in the steel. The buyer should collect the following and give the same set to every party.

1. **Rack layout and bay dimensions**, including the aisle width and the number of levels.
2. **Rack reactions**, both downward and uplift, and where they act — on the slab or on the frame.
3. **Crane and shuttle data**: rail loads, speeds, and the deflection limit at the rail.
4. **Tolerance specification** from the automation supplier, including how it is measured.
5. **Floor flatness and levelness requirement**, with the measurement method and the extent it covers.
6. **Clear height** required above the top level, plus the roof structure zone.
7. **Fire strategy**: sprinkler type, in-rack provision, compartmentation, and the authority's requirements.
8. **Structural design basis**: local code, wind and seismic parameters for the site, and the approving engineer.
9. **Foundation and slab information**: what the local civil designer proposes, and the anchor interface.
10. **Phasing and access**: whether racking is installed before or after the envelope is complete, and what lifting equipment will be on site.

Items 3, 4 and 5 are the ones that most often arrive late, and each of them can invalidate a quotation built on assumptions.

## Floor, frame and rack: three tolerances, one chain

The most common argument on an AS/RS site is about where the error came from. The answer is usually that nobody allocated it in advance.

- The **steel frame** has a plumb and level tolerance, measured on the columns and beams.
- The **slab** has a flatness and levelness tolerance, measured over the aisle length.
- The **racking** has a verticality and pitch tolerance, measured on the uprights.
- The **crane** has an installation tolerance, measured at the rail.

If each party works only to its own standard, the crane can end up outside its allowance while every individual inspection report passes. The fix is a written tolerance budget: the automation supplier states what the crane needs, the steel and slab specifications are set tighter than that total, and the survey method and the point of measurement are agreed before work starts.

This is also where a fabricator's process matters. Tighter frame tolerances are produced by controlled cutting, fit-up and dimensional inspection, not by asking erectors to "pull it straight" on site. More on how that is specified is in the [fabrication tolerance guide](/en/blog/steel-structure-fabrication-tolerances-guide/).

## Height, wind and stability

A high-bay building is structurally different from a low shed even at the same footprint: wall area exposed to wind grows with height, the roof is more sensitive to uplift, and lateral stiffness — not vertical capacity — usually decides the column size.

Two practical consequences:

- **Bracing positions must be fixed early**: they compete with rack bays, aisle openings and dock positions, and moving a braced bay after the rack layout is frozen is expensive. Background: [clear height planning](/en/blog/steel-structure-eave-height-clear-height-planning/) and [wind load design](/en/blog/steel-structure-wind-load-design-guide/).
- **The roof zone above the top beam** must carry roof, purlins and any sprinkler mains without eating the promised clear height. Clear height is measured to the lowest obstruction, not to the eave.

## Fire, access and the authority interface

High-bay automated warehouses concentrate value and limit human access, and local fire authorities treat them accordingly. Close early: the sprinkler concept (including whether in-rack sprinklers are required), compartment size, the fire resistance required of frame and rack, and firefighter access.

The steel frame's fire protection depends on that outcome: a specification chosen before the sprinkler concept is agreed will often be revised. The general logic of intumescent versus board protection is covered in the [fire protection guide](/en/blog/steel-structure-fire-protection-guide/); the point here is sequencing, not product selection.

Access also affects the envelope design: roof access, rooflight positions, and door and hatch locations all need to be fixed while the cladding is being detailed, not after the building is up.

## Erection and sequencing

A workable sequence for a building-supported AS/RS project looks like this:

1. Foundations and slab base completed to the agreed levels, with anchor positions verified against the approved drawings.
2. Steel frame erected, plumbed and bolted to final tension, with the lateral system complete before loading the frame.
3. Frame surveyed against the tolerance budget, and results recorded — not just "checked".
4. Slab finished, cured and measured for flatness over the aisle length.
5. Racking installed against the verified floor and frame.
6. Cranes, shuttle systems and conveyors installed and aligned to the rack.
7. Envelope, roof access and fire protection completed around the installed system.

The sequence matters because steps 3 and 4 produce measurements that step 5 depends on. Cutting them short to save programme time is the usual source of crane-rail rework.

## Where the cost actually moves

An automated warehouse does not cost more per square metre only because it is taller. The money moves to:

- **Tighter fabrication and inspection** — more dimensional checks, more documentation, more rework risk.
- **Lateral system** — bracing or frame stiffness sized by deflection rather than strength.
- **Envelope area** — wall and roof area grows with height, including insulation, cladding and flashings.
- **Fire protection** — driven by the sprinkler strategy and the authority's requirements.
- **Floor slab** — flatness over long aisles is a concrete cost, but it is triggered by the automation decision.
- **Survey and interface management** — coordination is a real cost item, and the cheapest to fund properly.

Steel tonnage is one line in that list, which is why an AS/RS project should be quoted from rack layout, crane data and fire strategy — not floor area. Ordinary warehouse cost structure: [warehouse cost guide](/en/blog/steel-warehouse-cost-guide/).

| Envelope | Roof and wall panels, insulation and flashings within the agreed scope | Local installation, sealants, final weathertightness |
| Fire protection | Primed or coated steel to the agreed specification | Intumescent or board application where local, authority approval |
| Erection | Marking, packing, container loading plan, installation technical guidance | Local labour, crane hire, physical erection, temporary works, site safety |
| Documentation | Shop drawings, packing lists, inspection reports, shipping documents | Import clearance, local inspections, as-built records |

## Buyer checklist

- [ ] Structural model chosen: rack-supported, building-supported or hybrid
- [ ] Rack layout, bay dimensions and aisle widths issued
- [ ] Rack reactions, including uplift, with the acting point identified
- [ ] Crane and shuttle rail loads plus the supplier's deflection limit
- [ ] Tolerance specification from the automation supplier, with the measurement method
- [ ] Floor flatness and levelness requirement, and the extent it covers
- [ ] Clear height defined to the lowest obstruction, with the roof zone allowed
- [ ] Fire strategy and sprinkler concept agreed with the authority
- [ ] Column grid aligned to the rack bay, bracing positions reserved
- [ ] Local design basis, approving engineer and foundation proposal confirmed

## FAQ

**Is an automated warehouse just a taller steel warehouse?**
No. The frame may look similar, but design is governed by deflection at the crane rail, a tolerance chain across frame, floor and rack, floor flatness, and a fire strategy built around high-bay storage. A taller standard shed usually fails on precision, not strength.

**Who designs the racking — the steel supplier or the automation vendor?**
The racking vendor designs the racking: it is a proprietary system with its own load tables and tolerances. The steel supplier designs the frame and coordinates the interface. In a rack-supported building the rack supplier becomes the structural designer and the steel scope shrinks to envelope supports.

**Can the steel frame be designed before the racking supplier is appointed?**
It can be started, but it should not be finalised. The rack bay drives the column grid, the rack reactions load the slab or the frame, and the crane data sets the deflection limit. Finalising the frame first usually produces change orders later.

**What does floor flatness have to do with the steel supplier?**
Directly, two things: column base levels and slab joint positions have to align with the floor specification, and the flatness tolerance has to be reconciled with the frame's own tolerance so the crane stays within its allowance. The concrete itself is a local scope item.

**Does a high-bay building need more fire protection than a normal warehouse?**
It depends on the local code, the sprinkler concept and the authority's requirements, and it is not a question the steel supplier can answer alone. What is certain is that the fire protection specification must follow the agreed fire strategy, not precede it.

**What is the most common cause of delay on AS/RS projects?**
Late interface data: rack reactions, crane deflection limits and the tolerance specification. Each arrives after pricing and can change member sizes, bracing or the floor specification.

**Standalone answers for citation**

An automated warehouse steel building is designed around the racking and crane interface, not around floor area: the column grid follows the rack bay, deflection at the stacker crane rail governs the lateral system, and a written tolerance budget links the frame, the floor and the rack.

Two structural models are used for automated warehouses: rack-supported, where the racking is the primary structure and the steel scope is mainly envelope support, and building-supported, where an independent steel frame carries roof and walls and the racking stands on the floor slab.

Before an AS/RS building can be quoted, a buyer must provide the rack layout and reactions, crane rail loads and the supplier's deflection limit, the tolerance and floor flatness specifications, the clear height to the lowest obstruction, the fire strategy, and the local design basis with the approving engineer.

If the rack layout is still moving, a staged approach works better than a fixed price: send the [project requirements](/en/contact/#quote-form) and the current layout, and the frame can be developed in stages as the automation design freezes.
