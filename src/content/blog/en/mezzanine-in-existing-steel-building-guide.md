A mezzanine is the fastest way to add floor area inside a steel building that already stands, and the moment a structure is asked to carry something it was never designed for. The steel is the easy part; deciding what carries the floor is not.

## Direct Answer

A mezzanine can be added inside an existing steel building, but the first decision is a load path, not a floor area. The new floor either transfers its loads into the existing frame — which then has to be checked for the added dead, live and point loads — or it is carried by its own columns and foundations, independent of the building. Most industrial retrofits end up hybrid: independent columns in the open bay, with a few beams tied back for stability only. Which one applies depends on the as-built drawings, the frame's verifiable spare capacity, the local code and how long the plant can stop.

## Part 1: Why the load path comes first

The existing frame was designed for a known set of loads: roof, wind, maybe snow. A new floor introduces a different family — its own dead weight, live load from people and goods, point loads where equipment sits, and horizontal force wherever the floor is tied back to the building. Every one of them lands on a member that already carries something. So the first decision is the route: load through the existing columns, or down new ones. Once that is fixed, connections, foundations, bracing and sequence follow.

One boundary has to be stated early: a fabricator can detail, check and fabricate steel, but it cannot decide whether an existing building may take more load. That belongs to the registered engineer for the project jurisdiction, working from the as-built condition.

## Part 2: Four framing options compared

| Option | How the load reaches the ground | Suits when | What has to be verified | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Suspended from the existing frame** — secondary beams span between existing columns, mezzanine beams sit on column brackets | Through existing columns and foundations | Spare capacity verified, floor is light | Column axial capacity, connections, foundation pressure | Loads every column in that line; hard to reverse later |
| **Independent columns inside the building** — new posts on their own footings | Directly down new posts to new foundations | Existing capacity unknown or insufficient, floor is heavy | New footing capacity, slab condition, headroom | Takes ground-floor space, interrupts layout |
| **Hybrid** — independent columns plus tie beams | Mainly through new posts; ties carry stability | Tall posts, slender frames | Magnitude and direction of tie forces | Ties transfer horizontal load, so they must be designed |
| **Side platform along a wall** — brackets on wall columns | Existing wall columns | Narrow access or maintenance platform, light loads | Local column capacity, wall panel fixings | Cantilever needs bracing; unsuitable for heavy storage |

The lowest quotation is not automatically the lowest total cost: hanging the floor on the existing frame saves foundations, but it loads connections that were never detailed for it.

## Part 3: What to survey before design

Most retrofit delays happen in one place — the as-built information does not exist. Collect this before design starts.

- [ ] **Existing drawings**, plus every later change: new openings, relocated equipment, earlier platforms.
- [ ] **Measured column grid** in both directions; frames are rarely exactly to drawing, and the difference decides whether details must be site-adjustable.
- [ ] **Column sections and steel grade**, from drawings or markings, or sampling if the engineer requires it.
- [ ] **Beam sections and spans**, and what already hangs from them: ducts, conveyors, monorails.
- [ ] **Roof build-up and framing**, which decides whether new members can tie in at roof level.
- [ ] **Floor slab**: thickness, reinforcement, joints, earlier penetrations, whether new posts may land on it.
- [ ] **Clear height** above the new floor and the headroom the process below still needs.
- [ ] **Bracing**: cross bracing, roof bracing, moment frames, and what a new floor does to them.
- [ ] **Services and access**: sprinkler mains, ducts, cable trays, crane runway; door widths, lifting reach, hot-work permits.
- [ ] **Operating window**: which shifts can stop, and which equipment cannot stop at all.

Decking, stairs and edge protection are a separate decision, covered in the [steel platforms and mezzanines guide](/en/blog/steel-platforms-mezzanines-equipment-support-guide/).

## Part 4: What the engineer checks, and how connections are made

![A bolted bracket connecting a new mezzanine beam to an existing column, with the lowest bolt being tightened by torque wrench (schematic illustration)](/images/blog/mezzanine-in-existing-steel-building-guide/column-bracket.jpg)

The engineer checks column axial capacity and buckling (a new floor can shorten the effective length or brace a column in a direction it never was), beam bending and deflection (floor limits are usually stricter than roof limits), connection capacity (bolted connections often govern before the members do), foundation pressure, overall stability, fire resistance of the floor and its supports, and seismic mass where the jurisdiction requires it. Existing corrosion, previous repairs and undocumented welding have to be checked too.

If a member fails, reinforcing the existing frame is usually the more expensive road. Adding independent columns and taking the load past the frame is often faster and lower cost.

Three rules for connections:

- **The bolted bracket is the default.** A plate is fixed to the column flange or web and the new beam is bolted to it; holes either exist or are drilled with the engineer's approval, away from the worst part of the tension zone.
- **Site welding to a loaded column is a design decision, not a field convenience.** It needs the engineer's approval, a statement of what the column carries while the weld is made, a qualified welder, inspection, and repair of the coating and fire protection.
- **Design for tolerance.** Existing frames are rarely plumb, square or exactly to drawing; slotted holes and shims absorb the difference, and forcing members into place preloads connections. Do not open holes in a flange or cut a column casually either.

## Part 5: Foundations and the installation sequence

![A mezzanine carried by its own steel columns on new concrete pads beside an existing workshop frame (schematic illustration)](/images/blog/mezzanine-in-existing-steel-building-guide/independent-columns.jpg)

New pads under independent columns mean local breakout, excavation, a poured pad and reinstated floor: the most reliable route and the most disruptive. Bearing directly on the existing slab is only possible once the engineer has verified it for that point load, which depends on thickness, reinforcement and load spread; anchors fix base plates, they do not create capacity. The details that get missed are slab joints and edges, drains and buried services, and forklift traffic around the new posts.

The sequence runs in six steps:

1. **Freeze survey and design**, with details that can be adjusted on site rather than a design that assumes the frame is exact.
2. **Clear the approvals**: local registered engineer, plus the authority where required.
3. **Fabricate and trial-assemble off site**, marking members to the erection sequence rather than only to drawing numbers.
4. **Enabling works**: relocate services, break out and cast pads, allow curing, protect equipment still running alongside.
5. **Erect inside a shut window**: lift plan first, exclusion zone below, decking laid progressively, temporary bracing kept until connections are checked.
6. **Close out**: bolt tightening, coating repair, stairs and edge protection, inspection records and as-built drawings.

Sequence matters more than duration: a mezzanine erected in a shut window with a clear lift plan disturbs production less than one erected whenever the crew is free.

## Part 6: Scope split on an overseas project

Inside an existing plant overseas, the work divides into what travels with the steel package and what can only happen locally. Getting this line wrong is the most common source of site delay.

| Stage | ZhongSai scope | Local party scope |
|---|---|---|
| Design and detailing | Engineering coordination, connection details, shop and erection drawings | Registered engineer of record, code compliance, permits |
| Fabrication | Members, base plates, brackets, bolts, marked to the sequence | — |
| Packing and shipping | Packing, container loading, export documents | Clearance and inland transport |
| Foundations and civil | Details coordinated so foundations can be built locally | Excavation, pads, slab reinstatement |
| Erection | Installation technical guidance, marked members, sequence information | Local labour, lifting plant, site welding (if any), safety |
| Inspection and handover | Mill certificates, dimensional checks, NDT to the agreed specification | Witnessing, third-party inspection, acceptance |

Measurement of the existing frame has to be done locally in every case: nobody can detail a bracket for a column whose real position has not been measured.

## Quick answers

### Does a mezzanine inside an existing building need its own columns?

Not always. Where the frame has verifiable spare capacity and the floor is light, secondary beams can span between existing columns. In practice most retrofits still use independent columns, because the frame has no documented spare capacity or the floor carries storage and equipment.

### Can a bracket be welded to an existing column on site?

Only as an engineered decision: engineer's approval, an assessment of what the column carries during the work, a qualified welder, inspection, and coating and fire protection repair. Where those conditions are not met, a bolted bracket on approved hole positions is the usual answer.

### What is the first thing to check before adding a mezzanine?

The as-built condition: measured grid, member sizes and the loads already on the frame. Without them, neither capacity nor connection details can be established.

## FAQ

### Can I add a mezzanine without touching the main frame?

Yes, if the floor is fully self-supporting: its own columns, its own foundations, no ties into the existing structure. That removes the need to prove the frame's capacity, but it needs ground-floor space for the posts and usually a slab breakout for the pads.

### What floor load should I put in the enquiry?

Put the use, not a guessed number — maintenance access, palletised storage, racking, a machine with its own dynamic action, or vehicles — and let the engineer apply the governing code. A floor designed for foot traffic and later stacked with pallets is the classic retrofit failure.

### Does a mezzanine need building approval?

It depends on the jurisdiction and on what the floor changes. Where it counts as a storey, alters floor area or affects escape, it normally needs approval and a registered engineer's design.

### Can the new floor use the existing roof bracing?

Not without a check. Roof bracing was designed for roof and wind loads in a specific direction, and a floor introduces another load path; sharing it can work, but it is a design decision with a calculation behind it.

### What decking is normal for an industrial mezzanine?

Grating, steel deck with concrete topping and panel decks are all used, depending on whether the floor is walked on, rolled over, washed down or kept clean; the differences are set out in the [platform and mezzanine guide](/en/blog/steel-platforms-mezzanines-equipment-support-guide/).
