**Direct answer:** a textile mill is rarely one building. It is normally a group of halls — spinning or knitting on several levels, weaving and finishing in high single-storey sheds, plus storage, utilities and a boiler or chiller house. Steel is used where a structure has to span over machines, carry line-mounted equipment and ductwork at height, or arrive in pieces for a site with a short construction window. What decides the steel scope is the machine layout and load schedule, the humidity and dust regime in each hall, the fire strategy for lint and cotton dust, the floor and foundation interfaces, and the local code that governs wind, seismic and fire.

Most enquiries arrive as a floor plan with machine counts and a request to price "the factory building". In a spinning hall the frame is a machine support and a humidity enclosure at once. An envelope that meets span and wind but lets the roof stay cold will drip condensate onto sliver and yarn, and the mill will blame the building, not the process.

This guide sets out which parts of a textile mill are steel, which loads and environments drive the sections and details, and where the steel scope ends. Machine foundations, floors and pits are normally concrete and belong to the civil package; the steel frame supports them and coordinates with them.

## 1. Which buildings in a textile mill are normally steel-framed

| Building | What it contains | Typical steel scope | Deciding interface |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spinning hall | Ring frames, roving and draw frames, overhead cleaners, humidification ducts | Multi-storey or high single-storey frame, floor beams, stair and service cores | Machine module, floor vibration, humidity control |
| Weaving shed | Looms, warp beams, cloth roll storage, overhead travelling cleaners | Long-span portal or truss roof, columns on a machine grid, crane or monorail where used | Loom spacing, roof height, cleaning access |
| Dyeing and finishing | Dye vessels, stenters, wash ranges, chemical storage | High shed with process platforms, pipe bridges and exhaust duct supports | Wet environment, corrosion, drainage, heat |
| Yarn and fibre store | Bale storage, high stacking, dust | Clear-span frame, sprinkler and smoke vent coordination | Storage height, fire load, floor loading |
| Utilities: boiler, chiller, compressor, ETP | Boilers, chillers, compressors, effluent treatment | Boiler house frame, plant platforms, pipe rack, pump house | Equipment vendor loads, local boiler rules |
| Warehouse and dispatch | Finished goods, packing, loading bays | Frame with loading canopy and dock levellers | Truck access, canopy loads |
| Office, canteen, laboratory | Occupancy, testing equipment | Small frames, roofs and walls, partitions | Fit-out coordination |

Steel suits speed, span and later re-layout. Reinforced concrete is still common for multi-storey spinning buildings in some markets, and the choice is usually a cost and schedule decision made with the local contractor, not a supplier preference.

**Is a textile mill built from steel or concrete?** Both are used. Steel dominates where speed, span or later re-layout matters; concrete remains common for multi-storey spinning halls in markets with local precast capacity. The choice is normally a cost, schedule and machine-tolerance decision taken with the local contractor.

## 2. Humidity is a structural detail, not a finishing choice

A spinning hall is kept at a controlled humidity so that fibres do not break. That humidity does four things to a steel building:

- it raises the corrosion category of every exposed surface, including purlins, tie rods and door frames;
- it makes the roof and walls the cold side in a warm, moist hall, so condensation forms on the steel, not in the air;
- it turns small thermal bridges — an uninsulated purlin line, a metal door frame, a drain body — into repeat drip points over machines;
- it changes maintenance: repainting a hall full of running frames is a shutdown job.

**Why does humidity matter to a steel frame?** A humid hall makes the roof and walls the cold side, so condensate forms on steel rather than in the air, and every thermal bridge becomes a drip point over machines. The fix is a vapour-tight insulated envelope and a coating system specified for the hall's corrosion category.

The practical response is an envelope designed as a vapour-tight assembly, not a roof sheet on purlins: continuous insulation, a vapour barrier on the warm side, sealed joints, few penetrations, and details that drain condensate away from product. None of this is a fixed specification — the corrosion category, coating system and insulation thickness depend on the design brief, the local climate, the humidity set point and the owner's maintenance plan, and belong in the technical specification before detailing.

## 3. Cotton dust, lint and fire change the roof, not the columns

Cotton dust and lint are combustible, and accumulated lint on ledges, purlins and duct tops is a fire load as well as a cleaning problem. The structural consequences are indirect but real:

- roof and purlin surfaces need to be reachable for cleaning, which argues for simple geometry and access walkways rather than hidden pockets;
- duct and humidification routes need supports that do not create lint traps, detailed for the loads the process vendor gives;
- smoke vents and sprinkler mains must line up with the purlin layout, because a main that lands between purlins cannot be installed as drawn;
- egress from upper spinning floors depends on stair widths set by the fire strategy, and those widths must reach the frame drawings before fabrication.

Sprinklers, detection and compartmentation are decided by the local fire authority and the insurer. The fabricator's job is to make the frame workable for that decision and flag conflicts before erection.

**Does cotton dust change the structural design?** Not directly — dust and lint are a fire and cleaning issue rather than a designed load, though long-term accumulation adds dead load. What the structure must provide is cleanable surfaces, reachable roofs, duct routes without lint traps, and purlin spacing that accepts sprinkler and vent positions.

## 4. Multi-storey spinning versus single-storey weaving

| Option | Suits | Advantages | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Multi-storey steel frame | Spinning, knitting, compact sites, phased line installation | Small footprint, short horizontal service runs, floor-by-floor handover | Floor vibration and deflection must be checked against machine tolerance; erection needs a crane with reach over a tight site |
| High single-storey shed | Weaving, finishing, large machine grids, later line changes | Simple structure, tall clear height, easy roof access and future re-layout | Large roof area exposed to wind uplift and solar gain; long drainage runs; more envelope to insulate |
| Steel frame with mezzanine | Garment lines, offices over production, storage over shipping | Lower-cost additional floor area inside the same envelope | Mezzanine loads and vibration isolation need a defined use, or the floor becomes unusable |
| Precast concrete frame | Multi-storey spinning in markets with local precast capacity | Familiar to local contractors, good vibration mass | Longer programme, less flexibility for later openings, heavier foundations |

The decision is driven by machine tolerance and by how the owner expects to change the line mix over ten years. A weaving shed that may become a warehouse should be planned with a clear height and grid that accept both uses.

## 5. The loads that are not roof plus wind

| Load source | Why it matters | Information the fabricator needs |
|---|---|---|
| Machine dead and dynamic load | Ring frames, looms and stenters impose point loads and vibration | Machine layout, weight per unit, base dimensions, vendor dynamic data |
| Ceiling-hung services | Humidification ducts, overhead cleaners, cable trays, compressed air | Route, weight per metre, hanger spacing, clearance to machines |
| Floor live load and storage | Yarn bales, cloth rolls and packed goods stack high | Storage height, racking or block stacking, forklift class |
| Wind uplift on light roofs | Large single-storey roofs in open sites see high suction | Local wind data per code, roof zone layout, edge detailing |
| Seismic action | Tall narrow halls and multi-storey frames need bracing that does not block aisles | Local seismic parameters, bracing locations, aisle widths |
| Fire and smoke control | Vents, sprinklers, compartment walls | Fire strategy, vent sizes and positions |

A quotation built on steel weight per square metre hides all of this.

## 6. Machine foundations, floors and pits are not steel

Spinning frames, looms and dyeing ranges usually sit on concrete bases or grouted plinths; the steel frame carries the building, not the machine. The split decides who coordinates what:

- the machine vendor supplies base plates, anchor layouts and dynamic data;
- the civil contractor builds and grouts the bases, and sets anchor bolts to tolerance;
- the steel fabricator supplies the frame, the floor beams, the stair and platform steel, and the embedments or anchor assemblies that the frame needs;
- the owner's engineer confirms that floor deflection under machine load is acceptable for the process.

When this split is vague, anchor bolts arrive late, base plates do not fit and the first machine cannot be aligned. In a humid hall the same detail decides corrosion: a base plate that traps water between steel and concrete rusts from the underside, so drainage and grout details belong in the drawing set.

## 7. Clear height, hanging services and the cost of changing a line

Textile machinery is long, low and laid out in rows. Three dimensions drive the frame more than architectural drawings:

- the column grid, which has to match the machine module and the aisle widths the process needs;
- the clear height to the lowest obstruction, which sets how high the overhead cleaners, ducts and monorail can sit;
- the bay spacing, which decides how many frames, purlins and bracing lines the roof needs.

Because line mixes change, the lowest-cost frame is often not the most economical in service. A shed planned with one extra bay, or with columns on a module that accepts two machine layouts, avoids a retrofit later. Where a hall may be extended, joints and cladding details belong at the start — the guide to [steel building extensions and expansion joints](/en/blog/steel-building-extension-expansion-joints/) covers that decision.

## 8. Delivery, erection and packing for a humid site

Textile projects are often built while the owner is importing machines, so the steel programme has to fit between foundation handover and machine setting:

1. Fabricate and pack by erection zone or floor, so a partial delivery can be handed over without waiting for the whole order.
2. Mark members with zone and grid references that match the erection drawing, and pack stairs and bracing with the frame they belong to.
3. Protect coated steel for a humid site: dry packing, ventilation, no ground storage on wet soil.
4. Sequence the envelope behind the frame so humidity control can start in the first completed hall.
5. Keep a punch list per zone, because a textile mill is handed over hall by hall.

Corrosion protection for a humid, dusty hall is covered in more detail in the [steel structure coating and corrosion guide](/en/blog/steel-structure-corrosion-protection-coating-guide/).

## 9. Responsibility boundary in an overseas textile project

| Stage | ZhongSai scope | Local party scope |
|---|---|---|
| Design coordination | Engineering coordination, detailing, shop drawings, interface list with machine vendors | Registered engineer, permits, local code compliance, authority submissions |
| Civil and foundations | Embedments and anchor assemblies for the frame, base plate coordination | Foundations, machine bases, pits, drainage, grouting |
| Fabrication | Fabrication, welding, surface treatment, QC and inspection records | Third-party inspection if required by the owner |
| Packing and shipping | Marking, packing, container loading plan, export coordination | Import clearance, duties, inland transport |
| Erection | Installation technical guidance, erection drawings, site queries | Local labour, cranes, physical erection, site safety |
| Commissioning | As-built drawing support | Machine setting, alignment, humidity system commissioning |

Unless the contract says otherwise, the steel supplier does not take responsibility for machine foundations, local erection labour or authority approvals. Writing that boundary into the contract before fabrication avoids the usual argument about a base that arrived out of tolerance.

## Common questions

### How much steel does a textile mill need?
There is no reliable tonnage per square metre. Frame weight depends on machine loads, clear height, span over looms, hung services, bracing for aisles, and local wind and seismic values. Two mills of the same floor area can differ substantially, so the number comes from a layout and load schedule, not a rate.

### Can a steel frame work in a humid spinning hall?
Yes, if the envelope is detailed as a vapour-tight assembly and the coating is specified for the hall's corrosion category. The frame is rarely the weak point; thermal bridges, unsealed joints and roof details that let condensate drip are. That is a detailing question, answered before fabrication.

### Is cotton dust a structural load?
No, cotton dust and lint are a fire and cleaning issue rather than a designed structural load, although accumulated lint does add dead load over years and blocks access. What the structure must provide is cleanable surfaces, reachable roofs and duct routes without lint traps, plus coordination with sprinkler and vent positions.

### Do spinning machines need concrete floors?
In most mills the machines sit on concrete bases or grouted plinths, and the steel frame carries the building and the floor beams. Whether a steel deck with concrete topping is acceptable depends on the machine's tolerance to vibration and deflection, which is a decision for the owner's engineer with the machine vendor's data.

### Do you supply the machines as well?
No. The steel scope covers the frame, floors, platforms, stairs, envelope support, marking, packing and export coordination. Machines, humidification plants and electrical systems come from their own vendors; their interface data is what makes the steel drawings correct.
