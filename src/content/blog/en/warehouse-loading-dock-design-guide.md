A loading dock is not a door cut into a wall. It is an interface between a vehicle and a building, and its dimensions are set by the trucks that will actually serve the site, not by a standard drawing. Dock height follows vehicle bed height, dock height fixes the leveler type and pit depth, and the pit, apron, canopy and wall opening then constrain the steel frame, the foundations and the floor slab. Fixing these four numbers late is the most common reason a warehouse cannot be loaded efficiently after handover.

This guide covers what a dock consists of, how height and leveler choices are actually made, and which interface data must be exchanged before a steel package is detailed and priced.

## What a loading dock actually consists of

A working dock position is a system of eight parts, and each one belongs to a different trade:

- **Dock opening and door** — the wall opening, its header, and the sectional or roller door that closes it.
- **Dock pit** — the recess in the floor slab that receives the leveler, with its own walls, floor and drainage.
- **Dock leveler** — the plate that bridges the gap and height difference between the warehouse floor and the vehicle bed.
- **Bumpers** — the rubber or polymer blocks that take the vehicle impact so the wall and pit edge do not.
- **Seal or shelter** — the flexible frame that closes the gap around the vehicle body for weather, dust, insects and temperature control.
- **Canopy** — the projecting roof that keeps rain off the interface and shades the seal.
- **Apron** — the external concrete area where the vehicle stands and manoeuvres.
- **Safety and control items** — guide lines, wheel chocks or restraints, dock lights, signal lights, and fall protection at the open door.

Two consequences follow. First, the dock is a civil, mechanical and steel interface at the same time, so it is usually the item that falls between trades. Second, every dimension on that list is fixed by the vehicle fleet, and the fleet is the buyer's information, not the supplier's assumption.

## The decision that drives everything: dock height versus vehicle bed height

Dock height is normally set so that the warehouse floor sits at, or slightly below, the bed height of the vehicles that will load at the site. Where the local fleet runs standard rigid trucks and trailers, this lands in a fairly narrow band; in markets where smaller or modified vehicles dominate, the same band is wrong.

| Input | Why it decides the dock | Who owns the answer |
|---|---|---|
| Vehicle types serving the site | Rigid truck, trailer, container chassis and light van all have different bed heights | Buyer / logistics operator |
| Loaded vs empty bed height | Suspension compresses under load, so the bed drops several centimetres when full | Buyer, from the actual fleet |
| Fleet variation | If vehicles differ by more than the leveler travel, one dock height cannot serve all of them | Buyer and leveler supplier |
| Local practice and permits | Some markets have customary dock heights and standard vehicle envelopes | Local engineer |
| Floor level inside the building | Internal drainage, slab thickness and ramp strategy all follow from floor level | Buyer and civil engineer |

The practical rule: **measure the fleet, do not copy a drawing.** A dock set 150 mm away from the real bed height still works, but the leveler runs at the end of its travel, the lip wears faster, and forklifts fight the slope all day. A dock set 400 mm away usually means the wrong leveler class and a pit that has to be rebuilt.

A useful benchmark, to be confirmed against the local fleet rather than assumed: many export-oriented warehouses set the dock floor around 1.1–1.3 m above the apron, with the apron and pit dimensions adjusted from there.

## Choosing the leveler: the four options that matter

Once dock height and vehicle range are known, leveler selection is a short list, not an open question.

| Type | Where it fits | Advantage | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pit-mounted hydraulic | Most general warehouses; the default choice where the fleet varies | Smooth travel, wide operating range, holds position | Requires a pit with drainage and a power supply; higher civil cost |
| Pit-mounted mechanical | Sites where power at the dock is unreliable or maintenance access is limited | Simple, robust, low running cost | Operator effort, less gentle on the lip, limited range |
| Edge-of-dock (top-mounted) | Retrofits, low-volume docks, thin slabs where a pit is impractical | No pit, fast installation, lower civil cost | Blocks part of the opening when stored; less suitable for heavy traffic |
| Vertical-storing | Docks that need a clear opening when idle, e.g. drive-through or mixed use | Plate stores upright, opening stays clear | Needs a deeper pit and a specific structure; higher cost |

Two selection details decide whether the dock works in practice:

1. **Lip length and reach.** The lip must reach the vehicle bed across the gap left by the bumper projection, and it must still reach when the vehicle is lightly loaded and sits higher.
2. **Service range.** Levelers are rated above and below dock level. A fleet with a wide spread of bed heights needs a leveler with enough below-level travel, otherwise the plate cannot follow a loaded trailer.

Leveler capacity and dimensions are manufacturer data and must come from the selected unit, not from a generic table. The dock pit is built to that unit's drawing, which is why the leveler should be chosen before the slab is poured.

## The pit, the apron and the truck envelope

Three external conditions determine whether trucks can actually use the dock:

- **Apron slope.** The apron must drain away from the building and still let a loaded vehicle stand without the trailer creeping. Longitudinal fall is usually kept gentle, and the crossfall is arranged so water does not run into the pit. Steep aprons cause trailers to slide or lift off the dock.
- **Approach and turning.** A vehicle has to arrive square to the dock. If the yard is tight, the driver arrives at an angle, the seal does not close, and the bumper takes an edge load instead of a flat one. Turning space depends on vehicle length and whether docks are 90-degree or drive-through.
- **Dock position spacing.** Each position needs clearance for the vehicle body plus the door and seal width, and positions are usually laid out on a regular module. Positions set too close together mean doors cannot open fully, seals interfere, and bumpers are shared where they should not be.

Where docks are arranged along one wall, the module also has to match the [steel column grid and bay spacing](/en/blog/steel-structure-column-grid-bay-spacing-guide/). That is the link most often missed between the logistics plan and the structural drawing.

## Where dock decisions hit the steel frame

This is the part that belongs to the building, and it is why dock information has to arrive before fabrication.

| Dock decision | What it changes in the steel and civil package |
|---|---|
| Dock position spacing | Column grid and bay layout along the dock wall; bracing cannot land in the middle of a dock opening |
| Opening width and height | Wall girt layout, header beam, and local stiffening around the opening |
| Canopy projection and width | Additional steel framing, connections to the wall, wind uplift on the canopy |
| Pit location and depth | Slab edge detail, pit walls, and clearance from column base plates and anchor bolts |
| Bumper impact loads | Local reinforcement at the opening edge and around the pit |
| Seal and shelter type | Clear dimensions at the opening head and jambs; head room for the door track |

The recurring conflict is between the pit and the column base. A column sits on a base plate with anchor bolts and a grout pad — the detail is covered in the [foundation design guide](/en/blog/steel-structure-foundation-design-guide/) — while a dock pit is a void with its own walls. If the dock module is fixed before the grid, the pit can end up beside a base plate with too little concrete between them, and either the dock shifts or the base detail gets a costly local redesign.

The second conflict is the opening and the bracing. A steel wall needs bracing somewhere; a dock opening removes wall area. If the bracing line and the dock line coincide, one of them has to move. Door and opening detailing is set out in the [doors and openings guide](/en/blog/steel-structure-doors-openings-guide/). This is cheap to resolve on a drawing and expensive to resolve after the columns are fabricated.

## Seals, shelters and doors: the weather interface

The seal or shelter is the part that makes a dock usable in rain, dust, heat or cold, and it is frequently underestimated.

- **Seals** attach around the opening and compress against the vehicle body. They suit a relatively consistent fleet.
- **Shelters** are a rigid frame with flexible curtains that the vehicle pushes into. They tolerate more variation in vehicle size and position, and are common on high-traffic docks.
- **Doors** must clear the shelter or seal when open, so the door track, the header and the canopy all compete for the same space above the opening.

Where the building is a cold store, a clean room or a food plant, the seal is not a comfort item but a hygiene and temperature boundary, and it changes the door type, the opening detail and sometimes the whole dock configuration. That makes the dock an early decision in those projects, not a finishing item.

## Safety, egress and the open door

An open dock door is an edge with a drop, a vehicle movement and pedestrian traffic in the same place.

- Wheel chocks or vehicle restraints keep the trailer from creeping away from the dock during loading.
- Guide rails or dock bumpers protect the door jambs and the seal from misaligned vehicles.
- Dock lights and signal lights are operational necessities on busy docks, not extras.
- Fall protection at the open door is required in many jurisdictions and must be decided before the opening detail is drawn.
- Dock doors are loading openings, not the main means of escape. Egress routes, exit doors and the fire strategy have to be designed independently, and the local authority's view on this is part of the permit package.

Safety hardware is specified to local regulation and to the site's risk assessment, so the correct answer is local. What the steel package must do is leave the geometry and the fixing points for it.

## What to fix before the steel is ordered

The following list is the interface data a fabricator actually needs. Items left blank become assumptions, and assumptions become change orders.

- [ ] Vehicle types, and bed height loaded and empty, measured on the actual fleet
- [ ] Number of dock positions, and which are receiving, despatching or mixed
- [ ] Dock height above the apron, and the internal floor level strategy
- [ ] Leveler type, capacity and the manufacturer's pit drawing
- [ ] Pit dimensions, drainage and the distance to the nearest column base
- [ ] Opening width and height, door type, and clear head room for the track
- [ ] Seal or shelter type, and its clear dimensions
- [ ] Canopy projection, width and whether it carries snow or wind uplift
- [ ] Apron slope, turning envelope and yard access
- [ ] Safety items: restraints, guide rails, lights, fall protection
- [ ] Egress and fire strategy, confirmed with the local authority
- [ ] Power supply and control location for levelers and doors

## Responsibility boundary

Dock work crosses trades, so the boundary is worth writing down before the order.

| Scope | ZhongSai | Local party |
|---|---|---|
| Steel frame, wall openings, header and canopy framing | Detailing, fabrication, marking and packing to the agreed drawings | — |
| Leveler, seals, shelters and doors | Coordination of interface dimensions only | Supply, installation and commissioning by the specialist supplier |
| Dock pit, apron, slab edge and drainage | Interface review against the foundation and slab drawings | Design and construction by the local civil contractor |
| Dock height and vehicle envelope | Engineering coordination based on buyer-supplied fleet data | Confirmation by the local engineer against local practice |
| Permits, egress and fire approval | Drawing support where required | Submission and approval by the local registered engineer |
| Erection and installation | Technical guidance | Local labour and physical installation |

## Common mistakes

- **Fixing dock height before measuring the fleet.** The building is then dimensioned around the wrong vehicle.
- **Choosing the leveler after the slab is poured.** Pit dimensions no longer match the unit, and the fix is demolition.
- **Setting dock positions without checking the column grid.** Pits end up beside base plates, or bracing ends up in an opening.
- **Ignoring the apron.** A dock that works on paper fails when loaded vehicles cannot stand square on a slope.
- **Treating the seal as an accessory.** On cold, clean or food projects it is a process boundary.
- **Forgetting the canopy load.** A long canopy is a wind-loaded structure, not a decorative shelf.

## FAQ

**What height should a warehouse loading dock be?**
It should match the bed height of the vehicles that actually serve the site, measured loaded and empty. Many export warehouses sit around 1.1–1.3 m above the apron, but the figure must be confirmed against the local fleet and local practice rather than copied from a template.

**Do I need a pit for a dock leveler?**
Not always. Pit-mounted levelers need a pit with drainage and power, while edge-of-dock levelers mount on top of the slab and need no pit but reduce the clear opening when stored. The choice depends on traffic volume, slab thickness and how clear the opening must be when idle.

**Can dock doors be placed anywhere along the wall?**
No. Openings have to fall between columns, avoid the bracing lines, and leave enough concrete between the pit and the column base plates. Dock layout and column grid therefore have to be developed together.

**How wide should each dock position be?**
Wide enough for the vehicle body, the door, the seal and the bumper, plus working clearance. Positions are usually laid out on a regular module, and the module has to suit both the vehicles and the steel bay spacing.

**Does dock design change the steel tonnage?**
It can. Extra openings mean header beams and local stiffening, a canopy adds framing and wind load, and a pit that clashes with a base plate can force a local foundation redesign. The frame itself is usually a small part of the change; the civil and coordination cost is the larger part.

**Can docks be added after the building is built?**
They can, but it is a structural alteration: the wall opening, the bracing, the slab edge, the pit and the drainage all change. Adding docks to a building whose grid and bracing were never planned for them is considerably more expensive than allowing for them at design stage.

## What to send with an enquiry

A dock design cannot be priced from a floor plan alone. Send the vehicle list with measured bed heights, the dock count and duty, the internal floor level, and any leveler or door already selected. Where the layout is not final, send the logistics concept and the constraints, and the interface can be developed with the frame instead of after it.
