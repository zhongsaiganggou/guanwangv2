A conveyor gallery is designed backwards from the belt. Belt width and speed, material weight, starting and running tension, drive location, walkway width and maintenance access fix the gallery cross-section, the trestle spacing and the support steel. The structure carries the belt, the walkway and the wind load on a long, narrow box — and it has to stay aligned while the belt runs.

## Part 1: What the gallery package actually includes

- The gallery span itself, open or enclosed, with a floor for the walkway.
- Trestles or portal supports carrying the gallery at intervals.
- A transfer tower where the belt discharges or changes direction.
- Stairs, platforms and access points to the walkway.
- Belt support frames, idler bases and pulley support steel if in scope.
- Chutes, hoppers and dribble chutes at transfer points.
- Cladding, roofing and drainage where the gallery is enclosed.

Scope lines matter more here than in a building: the belt, the idlers and the drive usually belong to the conveyor vendor, while the structural frame, walkway and cladding belong to the steel package.

## Part 2: The conveyor data you must get before detailing

| Input | Provided by | Why the steel cannot be fixed without it |
|---|---|---|
| Belt width and speed | Conveyor vendor | Sets gallery internal width and walkway clearance |
| Material type, bulk density and lump size | Process design | Sets live load, wear allowance and chute angles |
| Starting and running belt tension | Conveyor vendor | Pulls the gallery and trestle in the belt direction |
| Drive station location and weight | Conveyor vendor | Concentrates load and vibration at one support |
| Pulley and take-up positions | Conveyor vendor | Fixes openings, access and local framing |
| Walkway width and handrail standard | Client and local code | Sets gallery floor width and railing loads |
| Maintenance access and lifting points | Operations | Fixes platforms, openings and monorail provisions |
| Ambient temperature and wind | Local data | Sets expansion joints and wind bracing |
| Dust and explosion classification if any | Process and local code | Sets cladding, electrical and venting provisions |
| Interface coordinates at both ends | Site survey | Sets the actual line and level of the run |

If the tension and drive data arrive after detailing, the gallery has to be re-detailed: the belt pulls the structure along its axis, and a frame designed only for vertical load will move.

## Part 3: Gallery cross-sections and when each is used

| Section | Behaviour | Suits | Constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Truss gallery, two planes | Light, stiff, economical over long spans | Most belt widths on trestle runs | Deep section, more fabrication labour |
| Plate girder gallery | Simple, easy to clad and maintain | Medium spans, heavy belts | Heavier per metre |
| Folded plate or box section | Clean appearance, good torsional stiffness | Enclosed galleries in visible areas | Welding quality and distortion control |
| Modular pan sections | Fast site assembly | Retrofits and remote sites | More joints to align and seal |

The section follows the span and the belt, not the other way around. A wider belt with a walkway on one side needs a wider floor, and that changes the whole truss geometry.

## Part 4: Trestle spacing and support layout

- Trestle spacing is a trade-off: wider spacing means fewer foundations but a deeper gallery.
- Each intermediate support adds a foundation, a survey task and a joint to align.
- Trestles in the belt direction must resist the pull, not only the vertical load.
- Cross bracing in the trestle is what stops the whole run from walking sideways.
- Support positions should avoid the drive station, the transfer tower and any live traffic route.
- On soft ground, differential settlement between trestles shows up as belt misalignment, not as a structural crack.

## Part 5: Loads that are not gravity

| Load | Source | Effect on the steel |
|---|---|---|
| Belt tension, running and starting | Conveyor vendor | Longitudinal pull at pulleys, take-up and anchor points |
| Material live load and surge | Process design | Deck and truss loading, plus impact at transfer points |
| Drive torque and vibration | Drive station | Local framing, anchorage and fatigue details |
| Wind on a long narrow box | Local wind data | Lateral and uplift loads on trestles and gallery |
| Thermal movement on long runs | Ambient range | Expansion joints and sliding bearings |
| Dust accumulation | Operation | Extra dead load and cleaning access |
| Erection and maintenance loads | Site method | Temporary bracing and lifting points |

Wind is often the governing case for an empty gallery, because the run is long, high and lightly loaded. A frame checked only for a full belt will still fail on a windy day if the trestle bracing is thin.

## Part 6: Walkway, access and guarding

- Walkway width and handrail height follow the client standard and the local code, not a default.
- The walkway floor needs a defined drainage and cleaning method where material spills.
- Access stairs should land outside the belt line, not into it.
- Openings for pulleys, take-up and chutes need guarding that maintenance can remove and refit.
- Lifting points for idler replacement should be designed, not improvised on site.
- Emergency stops and pull cords need a route along the walkway inside the structure.

## Part 7: Open gallery versus enclosed gallery

| Option | Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Open gallery with roof only | Lower cost, easy inspection, good ventilation | Dust blows, material gets wet |
| Fully enclosed with cladding | Weather and dust control, cleaner appearance | Condensation, corrosion inside, access hatches needed |
| Enclosed with insulation | Noise and heat control | Heavier cladding, detailing at joints |
| Open with local covers at transfers | Targeted dust control at low cost | Partial protection only |

Enclosed galleries fail at the joints: if the cladding is fixed rigidly across an expansion joint, the panels crack in the first hot season.

## Part 8: Expansion joints, long runs and transfer tower interfaces

- Long runs need expansion joints at calculated intervals, with sliding or guided bearings at the trestle tops.
- The joint must allow movement in the run direction and resist lateral movement.
- Cladding, walkway floor, handrails and cable trays must all break at the same joint.
- At the transfer tower, the gallery end connects to a structure with different stiffness and different settlement.
- Clearances at chutes and hoppers must allow movement without contact.
- Nothing should be welded across a joint to "make it tight" on site.

## Part 9: Corrosion, abrasion and finish

- Abrasive material wears the deck and chute liners, not only the paint.
- Inside an enclosed gallery, condensation corrodes the underside of the roof before the outside shows anything.
- Hot-dip galvanizing suits trestles and frames that are hard to repaint; painted systems suit visible cladding.
- Coastal and fertilizer plants need a coating system chosen for the actual chemical exposure.
- Wear plates, replaceable liners and inspection hatches should be designed in, not added later.

Coating system and dry film thickness depend on the corrosion category and the chemical environment on that site; they are not a single global specification.

## Part 10: Tolerances and bolted splices

- Gallery modules are spliced on site at bolted connections; the splice must be alignable, not just strong.
- Splice positions should fall near a trestle so the joint can be supported during erection.
- Bolt holes are drilled to a tolerance that allows site fit-up; site welding of main splices is a redesign, not a fix.
- Camber and straightness affect belt tracking, so fabrication tolerances should be stated in the drawings.
- Shop trial assembly of at least one bay catches splice and cladding conflicts before shipping.

## Part 11: Shipping splits and module size

- Module length is limited by container or breakbulk dimensions and by what the site crane can lift at reach.
- Long galleries ship as several modules with marked splice pairs.
- Truss galleries are light but bulky; breakbulk or flat rack is common for wider sections.
- Cladding, walkway grating, handrails and bolts ship separately and must arrive in the same sequence as erection.
- Marking must follow the erection order along the run, not the fabrication order.

## Part 12: Erection over an operating plant

- Lifting over a live belt, a road or a stockyard needs a lift plan agreed with operations.
- Trestles are erected first, then gallery modules are landed and spliced.
- Temporary bracing stays until the splice and the second support are connected.
- Alignment is checked at each module, not only at the end of the run.
- Shutdown windows can be short; modules that arrive pre-assembled with cladding partly fitted save days.

## Part 13: Responsibility boundary

| Item | Export supplier | Local side |
|---|---|---|
| Structural design and detailing | Yes, from vendor and process data | Local engineer reviews and stamps |
| Fabrication, coating, marking, packing | Yes | — |
| Belt, idlers, drive and controls | Not included unless agreed | Conveyor vendor |
| Foundations and survey | Anchor bolt layout and loads | Local civil contractor and surveyor |
| Erection and crane | Technical guidance and lift method input | Local crew, crane and lift plan approval |
| Electrical and dust classification | Provisions only | Local electrical contractor and authority |
| Commissioning and belt tracking | Support information | Conveyor vendor and operations |

## Part 14: Checklist before design freeze

- [ ] Belt width, speed and material data received from the vendor.
- [ ] Running and starting tension values confirmed.
- [ ] Drive station location, weight and torque supplied.
- [ ] Walkway width and handrail standard agreed.
- [ ] Maintenance access and lifting points defined.
- [ ] Gallery line and level surveyed at both ends.
- [ ] Trestle spacing fixed against span and foundation cost.
- [ ] Wind and thermal cases included in the load set.
- [ ] Expansion joint positions and bearing types fixed.
- [ ] Transfer tower interface and clearances checked.
- [ ] Cladding, drainage and access hatches detailed at joints.
- [ ] Corrosion category and coating system selected.
- [ ] Splice positions aligned with trestles and transport limits.
- [ ] Marking and erection sequence agreed with the site.

## Part 15: Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Detailing before belt tension is known | Gallery walks and joints open |
| Trestle bracing treated as optional | Lateral movement under wind and starting torque |
| Cladding fixed across an expansion joint | Cracked panels in the first hot season |
| Splice placed mid-span | Cannot be aligned or supported during erection |
| Walkway access opening into the belt line | Unsafe maintenance, retrofitted guarding |
| No drain or clean-out on the walkway floor | Material build-up and overload |
| Coating chosen without chemical exposure | Early corrosion inside an enclosed gallery |
| Modules sized to transport only | Site crane cannot lift them at reach |

## Part 16: FAQ

**Question: What information does a fabricator need to design a conveyor gallery?**
Answer: Belt width and speed, material density and lump size, running and starting tension, drive location and weight, walkway width, maintenance access, the surveyed line and level, and the local wind and temperature data.

**Question: How far apart should conveyor trestles be?**
Answer: It is a trade-off between gallery depth and foundation count. Wider spacing needs a deeper, heavier gallery; closer spacing adds foundations, survey work and joints. The spacing is set with the belt loads and the local ground conditions.

**Question: Does a conveyor gallery need expansion joints?**
Answer: Long runs do, because thermal movement and support settlement accumulate. Joints are placed at calculated intervals with sliding or guided bearings, and cladding, walkway and cable trays must break at the same position.

**Question: Why does an empty gallery sometimes fail in wind?**
Answer: A long, narrow, lightly loaded box catches a lot of wind for its weight, so lateral and uplift loads can govern. Trestle bracing and anchorage must be designed for that case, not only for a fully loaded belt.

**Question: Should the gallery be open or enclosed?**
Answer: Open with a roof suits dry material and easy inspection; enclosed suits dust, rain and appearance but needs condensation control, internal corrosion protection and access hatches. Both need the joints detailed properly.

**Question: Can the gallery be shipped in container-sized modules?**
Answer: Often yes for narrow sections, but wider truss galleries usually go breakbulk or flat rack. Module length is limited by transport and by what the site crane can lift at reach, and splices should land near a trestle.


## Part 17: Fix the belt data first

Send the belt data sheet, the drive layout, the surveyed line and level, and the walkway requirement. The gallery section, trestle spacing and joints can then be fixed before any steel is cut.
