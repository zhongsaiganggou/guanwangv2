Assembly marking is the shop-applied code that tells a site team which drawing position a steel member belongs to, and which other member it was fitted to. A match mark is the part of that system which pairs members that must be assembled together: a column and its bracket, a beam end and its mating plate, or two halves of a spliced member. Assembly marks make a structure findable; match marks make a connection repeatable. Without them, a correctly fabricated frame still costs time on site, because every joint becomes a search and every pair becomes a guess.

This guide explains how assembly marks are structured, what match marks add, when trial assembly is worth the cost, how marks survive blasting, coating and packing, and what a buyer should check before the containers leave.

## Part 1: Assembly mark, erection mark, piece mark and shipping mark

Four labels are often used as if they mean the same thing. They do not.

| Mark type | Applied where | What it answers | Typical content |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assembly mark | On the member, in the shop | Which drawing position does this belong to? | Grid reference, level, member type, sequence |
| Match mark | On two or more members that fit together | Which member is this paired with, and in what orientation? | Pair code, side, rotation or arrow |
| Erection mark | On the member and on the erection drawing | Where is it lifted and fixed on site? | The same assembly code, readable at distance |
| Shipping mark | On the bundle, crate or tag | Which package goes where, and what is inside? | Package number, project code, weight, piece count |

In practice one code may serve several purposes, and that is acceptable if the logic is documented. What is not acceptable is using the same characters for two different meanings, or applying a code that exists on the shop drawing but cannot be matched to the erection drawing.

The assembly mark is decided during detailing, not after fabrication, and must agree with the grid, levels and erection sequence. Changing numbering after painting means re-marking, or a site team that trusts its own labels more than the drawings.

## Part 2: What a usable assembly mark contains

There is no single global numbering standard. Project specifications differ, and a fabricator's house logic is usually agreed with the customer's engineer before fabrication. Good systems share the same building blocks:

- **Location reference** — grid line or axis, plus level or elevation, so the mark points to a place, not just a type.
- **Member type** — a short prefix such as C for column, B for beam, R for rafter, P for purlin, BR for bracing.
- **Sequence** — a number unique within that type and location.
- **Orientation** — left/right, mirror, or a hand indicator where the member is not symmetrical.
- **Pair reference** — where relevant, the code of the member it connects to.

A mark such as `C-3/B-2-L` is readable: column at grid C-3, level B-2, left side. A bare number carries no location and creates a second numbering system on site.

Two rules matter more than the format. First, the mark must be unique: no two members may share a code, even if they look identical, unless the drawings deliberately define them as interchangeable. Second, the mark must be traceable: from the member to the shop drawing, to the packing list, to the erection drawing, without a phone call.

## Part 3: Match marks — why a pair needs its own language

Fabrication tolerances accumulate: a bracket welded 3 mm off or a plate drilled from the wrong side may still bolt up in the shop and fail on site, where there is no jig and no second chance. Match marks make the shop fit reproducible.

![Two mating members laid out with matching yellow match-mark bands and an orientation arrow at the joint (illustrative concept image)](/images/blog/steel-assembly-marking-match-marks/match-marks.jpg)

A match mark does three things:

1. **Pairs the members.** The bracket on column C-3 and the beam B-2 that was fitted to it carry the same pair code.
2. **Fixes the orientation.** An arrow or a side letter shows which end faces which, so a symmetrical-looking member is not installed upside down.
3. **Records the fit.** Where a joint was trial-fitted, the mark confirms the same pieces were used, not a replacement made later from a similar drawing.

Match marks are most valuable at connections that are geometrically constrained: tapered or skewed joints, box-section stubs, built-up sections, cranked rafters, and connections with slotted holes or welded stubs where site adjustment is limited.

They are less critical for a simple bolted end plate on a straight frame, where any identical member will fit any identical position. Marking every member as a unique pair in that case adds handling cost without adding control.

## Part 4: When trial assembly earns its cost

Trial assembly (also called pre-assembly or shop fit-up) means assembling part of the structure in the fabrication yard before dispatch, checking it, marking it, then dismantling and packing it. It is not a default step. It is justified when the risk of a site misfit is higher than the cost of the yard work.

| Condition | Trial assembly | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Complex or skewed joint geometry | Usually yes | The joint cannot be verified from separate drawings alone |
| Box-section columns and beams with welded stubs | Usually yes, at least the first of each type | Welded stubs and closed cells leave little site adjustment |
| Long span or cranked rafters | Often yes | Accumulated length and angle error shows only when assembled |
| Repeated standard portal frame | Normally no | Identical members are interchangeable and drawings control the fit |
| First article of a new profile or a new welding set-up | Yes | Confirms the shop process before mass production |
| Project specification or engineer requires it | Yes | Contractual requirement |
| Site has no crane time for adjustment | Yes | The fit has to be right on the first lift |

It also confirms that the marks themselves work: if the yard crew cannot match pieces by the marks, the site crew will not manage it either.

The cost is real: yard space, crane time, bolting and dismantling, paint repair and extra handling. It should be priced separately, so the buyer can judge whether the geometry justifies it.

## Part 5: Marking methods and how long the mark lasts

The mark has to survive blasting, painting, packing, sea transport and site handling. That is a longer life than most people expect.

| Method | Durability | Typical use | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paint stencil | Good on primed or finished surfaces | Large members, readable at distance | Can be over-coated if the shop paints after marking |
| Marker pen | Low | Temporary reference during fabrication | Fades, smears, disappears under coating |
| Metal tag | High | Bundles, small parts, members where painting is sensitive | Can be torn off in handling; needs a fixing point |
| Stamped mark | Very high | Heavy plates and members where the specification allows | Not permitted on some grades or in some locations; creates a stress point |
| Weld bead mark | High | Rare, only where explicitly permitted | Damages the surface and is usually prohibited on main members |
| Adhesive label with code | Medium | Packing lists and tagged bundles | Falls off in wet or hot conditions |

Three practical rules follow from this table.

**Mark before coating, then protect the mark.** If the member is blasted and primed, the assembly mark should be applied on the primed surface and either left uncoated or covered with a compatible clear coat. Painting over a stencil with the finish coat is acceptable only if the mark remains legible; many shops instead re-mark after the finish coat at the same position.

**Do not put the only mark where it will be hidden.** A mark on the face cast into a foundation, or under a base plate, will never be read. Mark at least two faces that stay visible after erection.

**Match marks must be on both members, at the joint.** A pair code on the beam only is useless once the beam is in a bundle and the column is in another. Put the pair code near the connection on both sides, with the same orientation reference.

## Part 6: Marking, packing and container loading must agree

Assembly marks are not only a fabrication issue. They are the thread that connects the shop to the site.

- **Packing list** — each package should list the assembly marks it contains, so a missing member can be found before the container is opened on site.
- **Loading sequence** — members for the first erection bays should be loaded last or in a dedicated package, so they are available first. Marking makes that possible; without marks, the loading plan is based on weight and guesswork.
- **Bundle labels** — the shipping mark on the bundle should reference the assembly marks inside, not a separate package numbering system that exists only in the exporter's office.
- **Site receiving** — checking marks against the packing list and erection drawing is faster than counting pieces.

A simple test: pick a random line on a package list and try to find that member on site using only the documents and the marks. If it takes more than a few minutes, the system is not finished.

## Part 7: Common mistakes and how they show up

| Mistake | What happens on site | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Marking only the member type, not the position | Crews try members in several bays | Mark includes grid and level |
| Pair code on one member only | The mating member is searched or forced | Apply the pair code to both members at the joint |
| No orientation mark on a symmetrical member | Member installed mirrored or upside down | Add a side letter or arrow at the joint |
| Mark hidden by coating or by another member | Mark is unreadable after erection starts | Mark two visible faces; re-mark after finish coat |
| Stencil painted over until illegible | Crew invents its own labels | Protect or re-apply the mark; inspect before packing |
| Marking after packing | Bundles are opened to identify members | Mark and verify before packing |
| Numbering changed after detailing | Shop drawings, marks and erection drawings disagree | Freeze the numbering with the drawing approval |
| No mark on small parts | Bracing, cleats and bolts are mixed | Tag small parts by assembly and package |

The last mistake is the most expensive in proportion to its size: a missing cleat mark costs little in the shop and a day of crane time on site.

## Part 8: What to check before shipment

- [ ] Every member carries an assembly mark that matches the approved shop drawing and the erection drawing.
- [ ] Every geometrically constrained joint has a pair code on both members.
- [ ] Orientation marks exist for mirrored, skewed or cranked members.
- [ ] Marks are legible after the final coating, not buried under it.
- [ ] At least two faces are marked where one face will be hidden.
- [ ] Small parts — cleats, bracing gussets, bolts — are tagged by assembly and package.
- [ ] The packing list uses the same assembly marks as the drawings.
- [ ] The loading sequence groups first-erection members and labels them clearly.
- [ ] Trial-assembled joints carry a record of which members were fitted together.
- [ ] A sample of marks has been checked against the drawings by someone other than the marker.

**Question: what does a steel assembly mark need to contain?**
It needs a location reference (grid and level), a member type prefix, a unique sequence number, and an orientation indicator where the member is not symmetrical. Where the member is fitted to a specific partner, it also needs the pair code used in match marking. A code that cannot be traced to both the shop drawing and the erection drawing is not a usable assembly mark.

**Question: is an assembly mark the same as an erection mark?**
Not exactly. The assembly mark identifies the member and its drawing position during fabrication and packing. The erection mark is the same information presented for site use, readable at a distance and consistent with the erection drawing. In many projects one code serves both, but the site version must be legible after transport and coating.

**Question: when should match marks be used instead of unique marks for every member?**
Use match marks where a joint is geometrically constrained or was trial-fitted: skewed connections, welded stubs on box sections, cranked rafters, or members that only fit their partner. For interchangeable standard members, a position code is enough and a pair code adds handling cost without control.

**Question: can marks be welded or stamped onto the member?**
Only where the project specification allows it. Stamping can be acceptable on some heavy plates but is often restricted on main members, high-strength steel or fatigue-sensitive details. Weld-bead marking is usually prohibited on finished members. Paint, tags and labels are the normal methods; the specification decides.

**Question: how do marks survive painting and sea transport?**
Apply the mark on a stable surface, protect it during blasting and coating, and re-apply after the finish coat where the shop system requires it. Use tags for bundles and small parts, and check a sample after packing. A mark applied with a marker pen before blasting will not survive.

**Question: should small parts be marked individually?**
Bracing gussets, cleats and bolts should be tagged by assembly and package rather than by a unique code on every piece. Individually marked small parts slow packing and lose tags in handling; the package label and the packing list carry the identification.

**Question: who pays when marks have to be redone?**
If the numbering changes after drawing approval, or the shop paints over marks, the rework is a fabrication cost that should be agreed before the order. If a member is marked correctly and the site loses the mark, re-identification is a site cost. Clear responsibility avoids the argument later.

## Part 9: Responsibility boundary

| Stage | ZhongSai scope | Local team scope |
|---|---|---|
| Detailing and numbering | Proposes the marking logic, prepares shop drawings and mark lists for approval | Reviews and approves the numbering against grid, levels and erection sequence |
| Fabrication | Applies assembly marks and match marks, trial-fits where specified | Confirms any project-specific marking requirement before fabrication |
| Packing and loading | Marks bundles, prepares packing list by assembly mark, loads in erection-friendly sequence | Confirms the receiving plan and unloading equipment |
| Site erection | Provides marked members and installation technical guidance | Reads marks, matches pairs, erects and aligns, manages local labour and crane |
| Approval | Supplies fabrication and marking records where required | Handles permits, inspections and authority submissions |

Unless the contract says otherwise, the local team owns the physical erection and the site interpretation of the marks. The supplier's responsibility is to make the marking unambiguous and to keep it consistent through packing.

## Part 10: Practical perspective from fabrication

On a typical export package, marking is one of the last operations before packing and one of the first sacrificed when the programme compresses. That is the wrong order: a missed mark cannot be fixed by a faster crane.

The workable sequence is to freeze the numbering with the approved drawings, mark at fabrication, trial-fit constrained joints, verify before coating and before packing, and build the packing list from the same mark list.
