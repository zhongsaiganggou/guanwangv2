A steel canopy for an EV charging station is not a scaled-up carport. It is a structure designed around charger layout, vehicle circulation, clear height, cable management, wind uplift, drainage and the local permit regime. The span is only one input. What usually decides the steel is how many bays sit under one canopy, whether DC cabinets stand under the roof or beside it, what vehicles must pass underneath, and how the canopy behaves in wind when it is light and open.

This guide is for developers, charge point operators, mall owners and contractors planning a charging site.

## Part 1: What the canopy actually has to carry

| Item | Usually supported by the canopy? | Design consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Canopy roof and purlins | Yes | Dead load plus wind uplift |
| Charger pedestals | Sometimes | Point loads and protection bollards |
| DC power cabinets | Sometimes | Heavier point loads, ventilation and access |
| Cable trays and conduits | Yes | Routing space under the roof |
| Lighting and CCTV | Yes | Fixing points and maintenance access |
| Signage and wayfinding | Yes | Wind area and fixing detail |
| Solar panels | Optional | Additional dead load and uplift sensitivity |
| Fire detection or suppression | Optional | Coordination with local fire authority |

Decide early which of these hang from the canopy. Retrofitting a cabinet or a PV array onto a canopy designed only for a roof is expensive.

## Part 2: Bay geometry and circulation

| Layout | Suitable for | Advantage | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Back-in bays, canopy per row | Mall and office car parks | Fits existing parking module | Cable reach and bay width are tight |
| Pull-through bays | Highway and fleet sites | No reversing, faster turnover | Needs a longer canopy and wider drive aisle |
| Single-sided cantilever canopy | Narrow sites | Column line kept outside the bay | Larger foundation moment |
| Double-sided canopy on a central column row | Large depots | Efficient steel per bay | Column row splits the two directions |
| Canopy over charger plus waiting lane | High-traffic retail | Keeps queue off the main aisle | More roof area and drainage |

The bay module should follow the vehicle dimensions and the charger cable length, not a standard parking grid copied from another project.

## Part 3: Clear height and vehicle mix

Clear height depends on what must pass under the canopy:

- **Passenger cars** — the common case, but roof equipment and signage still reduce usable height.
- **Vans and light commercial vehicles** — taller, and often the vehicles that actually use fast charging.
- **Buses and trucks** — require a much taller canopy, longer bays and a different structural form.
- **Roof-mounted equipment** — chargers, cable retractors and lighting all consume clearance.

Clear height is a design decision to confirm with the operator, not a number to copy. It is also one of the most expensive things to change after fabrication.

## Part 4: Structural forms

| Form | Suits | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Single-slope cantilever | Narrow sites, side-of-building canopies | Column outside the bay, larger base moment |
| Double-slope portal canopy | Two rows of bays | Symmetric, efficient, easy drainage to both sides |
| T-frame with two cantilevers | Central column row, four bays | Fewer columns, more steel per column |
| Free-standing canopy with four columns | Small sites, one or two bays | Simple but more columns in the parking area |
| Canopy attached to an existing building | Extensions and roadside retail | Interface with existing structure must be verified |

Attachment to an existing building is the highest-risk option, because the existing frame was never designed for the canopy's loads and uplift.

## Part 5: Loads that decide the steel

Light, open canopies are dominated by wind, not by gravity.

- **Wind uplift** — an open canopy behaves like an aerofoil; uplift can exceed downward load.
- **Wind pressure on signage and PV** — adds area and eccentric load.
- **Snow and ice** — depends on local climate and the roof geometry; drifting at edges is common.
- **Seismic** — depends on the site and local code; light canopies still need anchorage.
- **Vehicle impact** — bollards and protection are structural, not cosmetic.
- **Thermal movement** — long canopies need expansion provisions.

Every one of these must come from the project location and local code. A canopy designed for one market cannot be copied to another without verification.

## Part 6: Foundations and anchors

| Element | What decides it |
|---|---|
| Base plate size | Column load, uplift, anchor layout |
| Anchor bolts | Uplift and shear, not just compression |
| Foundation depth | Local soil, frost depth, groundwater |
| Grout and shimming | Erection tolerance and load transfer |
| Bollards | Vehicle impact protection for chargers and columns |
| Surface finish | Drainage fall and slip resistance around cabinets |

Uplift is the item most often missed. A canopy foundation sized only for downward load will not hold the roof in a storm.

## Part 7: Drainage and weather

- Gutters and downpipes must discharge away from chargers, cabinets and walking surfaces.
- Drip lines should not fall onto charging equipment or customer access areas.
- Long canopies need planned expansion joints and fall continuity.
- Coastal sites need a corrosion system suited to the environment, including cut edges and fixings.
- Roof access for maintenance should be designed, not improvised with ladders.

Water and electrical equipment in the same footprint is a maintenance problem for the life of the site.

## Part 8: Electrical coordination

The canopy is a structural package, but it constrains the electrical design:

- **Cabinet location** — under the canopy, in a separate enclosure, or remote.
- **Cable routing** — trays, conduits and pull points need space and access.
- **Earthing and bonding** — structural steel may participate; the local electrical engineer decides.
- **Ventilation and heat** — DC cabinets need airflow; a closed canopy pocket is a bad location.
- **Access for service** — doors must open without blocking a live bay.
- **Isolation and emergency stops** — positions must be agreed with the operator.

The electrical design is local scope. The canopy must be designed to accept it.

## Part 9: Fire and site safety

Battery vehicle fires behave differently from fuel fires, and local fire authorities treat charging sites accordingly. Structural implications usually include:

- Separation between charging bays and adjacent buildings or storage.
- Access and egress routes kept clear of canopy columns and bollards.
- Drainage that does not trap water where equipment sits.
- Coordination with detection, alarm and suppression if required locally.
- Signage and emergency information positions.

The exact requirements come from the local authority and the insurer, not from a generic guideline.

## Part 10: Permits and responsibility boundary

| Task | Export supplier | Local side |
|---|---|---|
| Canopy structural design and detailing | Engineering coordination and shop drawings | Local engineer of record verifies and stamps |
| Fabrication and quality control | Yes | — |
| Marking, packing, container loading | Yes | Receiving and inland transport |
| Foundation and anchor design | Interface loads and base plate details | Local civil and structural design |
| Electrical design and connection | None | Local licensed electrical contractor |
| Permits and authority submissions | Supporting documents on request | Owner and local engineer |
| Erection | Technical guidance | Local erection contractor and crane |
| Charger supply and commissioning | None unless separately agreed | Operator and charger vendor |

## Part 11: Checklist before ordering steel

- [ ] Bay layout and circulation confirmed with the operator.
- [ ] Clear height fixed for the vehicle mix, including vans.
- [ ] Charger and cabinet positions marked, with weights and access.
- [ ] Cable routing and tray paths agreed with the electrical designer.
- [ ] Canopy form selected and column positions checked against parking.
- [ ] Wind, snow and seismic parameters obtained for the site.
- [ ] Uplift checked for the open canopy condition.
- [ ] Foundation and anchor design appointed locally.
- [ ] Drainage discharge points confirmed.
- [ ] Corrosion system selected for the site environment.
- [ ] Bollards and impact protection located.
- [ ] Fire strategy and separation distances reviewed locally.
- [ ] Permit route and inspection milestones understood.
- [ ] Erection method and crane access checked on site.

## Part 12: Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Treating the canopy as a carport | Under-designed uplift and anchorage |
| Fixing clear height before the vehicle mix | Re-fabrication or restricted use |
| Adding cabinets and PV after design freeze | Overloaded canopy and changed drainage |
| Ignoring cable routes | Electrical rework on site |
| Foundation sized for downward load only | Anchors fail in wind |
| Columns placed inside the bay | Vehicles hit structure, bays become unusable |
| Drainage discharging over chargers | Continuous moisture and electrical risk |
| Ordering steel before the local engineer reviews loads | Design changes after fabrication |

## Part 13: FAQ

**Question: Can a standard carport be used for EV charging?**
Answer: Usually not without redesign. A charging canopy carries chargers, cabinets, cable trays and signage, and it must resist uplift as an open structure. A carport designed for roof load alone is typically under-designed for these conditions.

**Question: How tall should a charging canopy be?**
Answer: Clear height depends on the tallest vehicle that must pass underneath, plus roof-mounted equipment and signage. It is confirmed with the operator and the local designer, not taken from a standard parking dimension.

**Question: Do the DC cabinets have to sit under the canopy?**
Answer: Not necessarily. Cabinets can stand under the canopy, in a separate enclosure or remotely, but each option changes point loads, ventilation, access and cable routing. The decision belongs with the electrical designer and the operator.

**Question: What is the biggest structural risk on a charging canopy?**
Answer: Wind uplift on a light, open roof. Canopies have large exposed area and low dead load, so the anchorage and foundation must be designed for uplift rather than only for the weight of the steel.

**Question: Can the canopy support solar panels?**
Answer: It can if the additional dead load, uplift sensitivity, cable routing and maintenance access are designed in from the start. Adding PV to an existing canopy requires a structural check by the local engineer.

**Question: Who designs the foundation?**
Answer: The local civil or structural engineer, using the base plate loads, anchor layout and site soil conditions provided by the canopy designer. Foundation design is normally local scope, not export scope.

**Question: How long does a charging canopy take from drawings to delivery?**
Answer: It depends on design approval, detailing, material availability, fabrication slot, coating, transport and local foundation work. The programme should be built backwards from the site's energisation date.

## Part 14: Send the layout, not just the span

A charging canopy is specified by its layout, vehicle mix, equipment positions and site loads — not by a single span figure. Send the bay plan, charger and cabinet schedule, vehicle types, site location and local design parameters, and the structural options can be compared before steel is ordered.
