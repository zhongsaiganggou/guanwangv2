One of the most common questions buyers ask is: **"What do I need to send to get my steel structure fabricated?"**

The answer depends on your project stage. Some buyers have complete engineering drawings ready for fabrication. Others have only a concept, dimensions, and a building use. Both can work — but the required documents and next steps are very different.

This guide explains exactly what drawings and documents are needed at each stage, what each document contains, and how to prepare your project for smooth fabrication and quotation.

## Direct Answer: What You Need by Project Stage

| Project Stage | Required Documents | What Supplier Can Do |
|--------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| **Concept / Planning** | Building use, dimensions, location, budget | Conceptual design, budget estimate, preliminary advice |
| **Design Development** | Architectural drawings, structural design criteria | Structural design, preliminary layout, material takeoff |
| **Construction Ready** | Complete structural drawings, design calculations | Detailed design, shop drawings, fabrication quotation |
| **Fabrication Ready** | Approved shop drawings, BOQ, material specs | Direct fabrication, production scheduling, packing |

**Key takeaway**: You don't need complete fabrication drawings to start a conversation. Even basic project information allows a reputable supplier to provide guidance, preliminary pricing, and design support. The more complete your documentation, the more accurate and faster the quotation.

## Part 1: The Document Hierarchy

Steel structure projects involve several types of drawings and documents, each serving a different purpose. Understanding the hierarchy helps you know what you have and what's still needed.

```
Project Concept
    ↓
Architectural Drawings (what the building looks like)
    ↓
Structural Design Drawings (how the structure works)
    ↓
Shop / Fabrication Drawings (how each piece is made)
    ↓
Erection Drawings (how it goes together on site)
    ↓
Fabrication & Delivery
```

### Document Types Explained

| Document | Who Produces It | Purpose | Level of Detail |
|----------|----------------|---------|----------------|
| **Architectural drawings** | Architect / designer | Building layout, appearance, dimensions | Low–Medium |
| **Structural drawings** | Structural engineer | Member sizes, connections, loads | Medium–High |
| **Design calculations** | Structural engineer | Verify structural safety | Technical |
| **Shop drawings** | Fabricator / detailer | Each component's fabrication details | Very High |
| **Erection drawings** | Fabricator / detailer | Assembly sequence on site | Medium |
| **BOQ (Bill of Quantities)** | Quantity surveyor / estimator | Itemized material list and quantities | Medium |
| **Material specifications** | Engineer / buyer | Steel grades, coatings, fasteners | Reference |

## Part 2: Documents Needed for Quotation

### Minimum Information for a Meaningful Quotation

Even if you don't have formal drawings, the following information is essential for a supplier to provide a useful quotation:

#### 1. Building Dimensions and Layout
- **Overall length, width, and eave height**
- **Roof pitch** (or flat roof)
- **Number of bays** and bay spacing
- **Building layout** (single span, multi-span, lean-to, etc.)
- **Mezzanine** (if any, with area and load requirements)

#### 2. Building Use and Load Requirements
- **Primary use** (warehouse, factory, workshop, hangar, etc.)
- **Live load requirements** (roof live load, floor load if mezzanine)
- **Crane requirements** (capacity, span, lifting height, number of cranes)
- **Special loads** (equipment loads, conveyor supports, etc.)

#### 3. Project Location and Environment
- **Country and city** (determines wind/snow/seismic loads and coating requirements)
- **Wind speed** (if known, per local code)
- **Snow load** (if applicable)
- **Seismic zone** (if applicable)
- **Environmental category** (rural, urban, coastal, industrial — for coating)

#### 4. Cladding and Enclosure Requirements
- **Roof panel type** (single skin, sandwich panel, insulation type/thickness)
- **Wall panel type** (same options)
- **Doors** (size, quantity, type — sliding, overhead, personnel)
- **Windows** (size, quantity, location)
- **Ventilation** (ridge vents, wall louvers, fans)

#### 5. Design and Code Requirements
- **Design standard** (GB, AISC, EN, BS, AS/NZS, local code)
- **Steel grade preference** (Q235, Q355, A36, A572, S235, S355, etc.)
- **Connection type** (welded, bolted, mixed)
- **Coating requirements** (paint system, galvanizing, color)

#### 6. Commercial Terms
- **Delivery terms** (FOB, CIF, DDP, EXW)
- **Destination port** (if CIF/CFR)
- **Delivery site** (if DDP)
- **Required delivery date**
- **Payment terms** (if you have standard terms)

### If You Have Drawings, Send These

If you already have engineering drawings, the most useful documents for quotation are:

1. **General arrangement drawings** (plan, elevations, sections)
2. **Framing plans** (beam and column layout)
3. **Connection details** (typical and special connections)
4. **Member schedules** (beam/column sizes and lengths)
5. **BOQ / material takeoff** (if available)
6. **Design criteria sheet** (loads, codes, specifications)
7. **Roof and wall layout** (cladding, openings)

## Part 3: Documents Needed for Fabrication

Once the design is finalized and you're ready to proceed to fabrication, the following documents are required:

### 3.1 Approved Structural Drawings

These are the engineer-stamped drawings that define the complete structural system:

- **General arrangement (GA) drawings**: Plan, elevations, sections showing overall layout
- **Framing plans**: Beam and column locations, sizes, and spacing
- **Foundation plan**: Column locations, base plate sizes, anchor bolt layout (for your foundation contractor)
- **Connection details**: Typical and project-specific connection designs
- **Member schedules**: Complete list of all structural members with sizes, lengths, and grades
- **Bracing layout**: Cross bracing, portal bracing, and other lateral load resisting systems
- **Crane system drawings** (if applicable): Crane beams, brackets, runway details

### 3.2 Design Calculations

The structural engineer's calculations verifying that the design meets code requirements:
- **Load calculations**: Dead load, live load, wind load, snow load, seismic load
- **Member design**: Beam, column, and truss member sizing and verification
- **Connection design**: Bolt and weld verification for all connections
- **Foundation design**: Base plate and anchor bolt design
- **Deflection checks**: Vertical and horizontal deflection verification
- **Code references**: Applicable building code and design standard references

**Note**: Some buyers choose to have the fabricator perform detailed design based on conceptual structural drawings. In this case, the fabricator produces shop drawings and calculations for your engineer's review and approval.

### 3.3 Shop / Fabrication Drawings

Shop drawings are the detailed drawings used directly by the fabrication shop to manufacture each component. They are typically produced by the fabricator's detailing department (using software like Tekla Structures, SDS/2, or AutoCAD).

**Each shop drawing sheet contains:**
- **Piece mark / part number**: Unique identifier for each component
- **Material specification**: Steel grade, section size, length
- **Overall dimensions**: Length, width, height of the piece
- **Hole locations**: Bolt hole positions, sizes, and edge distances
- **Weld details**: Weld sizes, types, and locations
- **Cut details**: Cope, notch, and cut-out locations
- **Camber details** (if applicable): Pre-camber values for beams
- **Surface preparation and coating**: Paint system, DFT, color
- **Weight**: Individual piece weight for lifting and shipping planning
- **Assembly details**: How sub-assemblies fit together

**Typical shop drawing sets include:**
- **Anchor bolt plans**: For foundation installation
- **Column shop drawings**: Each column with base plate, splices, brackets
- **Beam shop drawings**: Each beam with end connections, holes, stiffeners
- **Truss shop drawings**: Truss member details and assembly drawings
- **Connection details**: Typical and special connection fabrication details
- **Part lists / material lists**: Complete bill of materials for each assembly
- **Erection drawings**: Site assembly sequence and connection details

### 3.4 Bill of Quantities (BOQ)

A detailed itemized list of all materials and components:

| Category | Typical Items |
|----------|--------------|
| **Primary steel** | Columns, beams, trusses, rafters, purlins, girts |
| **Secondary steel** | Bracing, tie rods, eaves struts, sag rods |
| **Connections** | Bolts, nuts, washers, base plates, cleats |
| **Cladding** | Roof panels, wall panels, flashings, trims, gutters |
| **Doors/windows** | Personnel doors, overhead doors, windows, louvers |
| **Accessories** | Downpipes, ridge vents, insulation, sealants |
| **Crane system** (if any) | Crane beams, rails, brackets, stops |

**BOQ should include:**
- Item description and specification
- Quantity (pieces, meters, square meters, tons)
- Unit weight (kg) and total weight (tons)
- Material grade and coating specification
- Reference drawing / piece mark

### 3.5 Material and Quality Documents

For fabrication to proceed, the following material and quality documents should be agreed:

- **Material specification**: Steel grades for primary and secondary members
- **Coating specification**: Surface prep, paint system, DFT, colors (see our corrosion protection guide)
- **Fastener specification**: Bolt grade (8.8, 10.9), type (hex, TC), finish (black, galvanized)
- **Welding specification**: Welding standard, weld procedures, welder qualifications
- **Inspection and test plan (ITP)**: What inspections are performed, at what stage, by whom
- **Non-destructive testing (NDT) requirements**: UT, MT, PT as applicable
- **Packing and marking specification**: How components are packed, marked, and loaded

## Part 4: If You Don't Have Complete Drawings

Many buyers — especially factory owners, investors, and first-time builders — don't have complete engineering drawings. This is completely normal. Here's what you can send and how the process works:

### What You Can Send Instead

1. **Architectural drawings** (if you have them): Floor plans, elevations, sections
2. **Sketch or hand drawing**: Even a simple dimensioned sketch helps
3. **Reference photos**: Photos of similar buildings you like
4. **Building specification**: Written description of what you need
5. **Site information**: Location, site dimensions, access constraints

### The Design Support Process

A reputable steel structure supplier can support you from concept to fabrication-ready drawings:

```
Step 1: You provide basic information
  (building use, dimensions, location, requirements)
        ↓
Step 2: Supplier provides preliminary layout and budget estimate
  (conceptual design, material estimate, budget price range)
        ↓
Step 3: You confirm the concept and proceed
  (sign contract, pay deposit)
        ↓
Step 4: Supplier performs detailed structural design
  (structural calculations, member sizing, connection design)
        ↓
Step 5: Supplier produces shop drawings for your review
  (detailed fabrication drawings for your engineer's approval)
        ↓
Step 6: You approve shop drawings
  (your engineer reviews and stamps approval)
        ↓
Step 7: Fabrication begins
  (production scheduling, material procurement, manufacturing)
```

### Important Considerations

- **Design responsibility**: Clarify whether the supplier's design is for fabrication only or for building permit purposes. Some countries require a locally licensed engineer to stamp the design.
- **Local code compliance**: Ensure the design complies with your local building code, wind/snow/seismic requirements.
- **Design fees**: Some suppliers include basic design in the project price; others charge separately for detailed design and engineering.
- **Review time**: Allow adequate time for your engineer to review shop drawings — typically 1–2 weeks for a standard project.

## Part 5: Common Document-Related Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Sending Only a 3D Rendering

A 3D rendering shows what the building looks like but contains no structural information — no member sizes, no loads, no dimensions. Suppliers cannot quote from a rendering alone.

**Solution**: Always include dimensions, building use, and location with any rendering.

### Mistake 2: Incomplete Drawing Sets

Sending only the general arrangement drawing without connection details, member schedules, or design criteria means the supplier must make assumptions — leading to inaccurate quotes.

**Solution**: Send the complete drawing set, including details, schedules, and specifications. If something is missing, say so explicitly.

### Mistake 3: Outdated or Uncontrolled Drawings

Sending an old revision of drawings that has since been updated leads to confusion and potential fabrication errors.

**Solution**: Clearly mark drawing revisions, send only the latest approved version, and maintain a drawing register.

### Mistake 4: No Design Criteria or Load Information

Without knowing the design loads (wind, snow, seismic, live load), the supplier cannot verify if the design is appropriate or provide accurate pricing.

**Solution**: Include the design criteria sheet with all drawings. If you don't have it, provide the project location and the supplier can reference local code requirements.

### Mistake 5: Sending Drawings in Non-Editable Format

Sending only PDF or image files means the supplier must redraw everything for shop detailing — adding time and cost.

**Solution**: If available, send CAD files (DWG, DXF) or BIM models (IFC, RVT, Tekla model) in addition to PDFs.

### Mistake 6: Unclear Scope of Work

Not specifying what is included (design, fabrication, delivery, installation) leads to scope gaps and disputes.

**Solution**: Clearly define the scope: who does design, who provides foundations, who installs, what is included in the price.

## Part 6: Document Preparation Checklist

### Before Sending for Quotation

- [ ] Building dimensions (length, width, height, roof pitch)
- [ ] Building use and occupancy
- [ ] Project location (country, city)
- [ ] Design loads (wind, snow, seismic — or location for code reference)
- [ ] Crane requirements (if any)
- [ ] Cladding requirements (roof/wall type, insulation)
- [ ] Doors and windows (sizes, quantities)
- [ ] Steel grade preference
- [ ] Coating requirements
- [ ] Design standard (GB, AISC, EN, etc.)
- [ ] Delivery terms and destination
- [ ] Required delivery date
- [ ] Architectural drawings (if available)
- [ ] Structural drawings (if available)
- [ ] BOQ (if available)

### Before Sending for Fabrication

- [ ] Approved structural drawings (complete set)
- [ ] Design calculations (signed by engineer)
- [ ] Approved shop drawings (signed by your engineer)
- [ ] Complete BOQ with material specifications
- [ ] Coating specification (surface prep, paint system, DFT, colors)
- [ ] Fastener specification (grade, type, finish)
- [ ] Welding specification and procedures
- [ ] Inspection and test plan (ITP)
- [ ] Packing and marking specification
- [ ] Drawing register (all revisions documented)
- [ ] Signed contract / purchase order
- [ ] Deposit payment (per contract terms)

## Part 7: File Formats and Transfer

### Recommended File Formats

| Document Type | Best Format | Also Acceptable |
|--------------|------------|----------------|
| **CAD drawings** | DWG (AutoCAD) | DXF, DGN |
| **BIM models** | IFC (neutral) | RVT, Tekla model, NWD |
| **PDF drawings** | PDF/A (archival) | Standard PDF |
| **Calculations** | PDF | Excel, Mathcad, Word |
| **Specifications** | Word / PDF | Excel |
| **BOQ** | Excel | PDF, Word |
| **Photos / references** | JPG, PNG | — |

### File Transfer Tips

- **Organize files** by discipline and revision in a clear folder structure
- **Name files clearly**: "ProjectName_Structural_GA_Rev2.pdf"
- **Include a drawing list** with all sheet titles, numbers, and revisions
- **Compress large files** (ZIP) for email transfer
- **Use cloud storage** (WeTransfer, Google Drive, etc.) for very large files
- **Confirm receipt**: Ask the supplier to confirm all files were received and are readable

## Conclusion

The documents needed for steel structure fabrication depend on your project stage, but the process is flexible:

1. **At quotation stage**: Basic project information (dimensions, use, location, requirements) is sufficient for a preliminary quote. Drawings are helpful but not mandatory.
2. **At fabrication stage**: Complete approved structural drawings, shop drawings, BOQ, and material specifications are required.
3. **If you don't have drawings**: A reputable supplier can provide design support from concept to fabrication-ready drawings.

The most important thing is to **send what you have and be clear about what's missing**. A good supplier will tell you exactly what additional information is needed and guide you through the process.

Remember: the more complete and accurate your documentation, the more accurate your quotation, the smoother the fabrication, and the fewer surprises during construction.
