A multi-storey steel frame is not a single-storey portal frame stacked twice. Once a second floor is added, wind and seismic loads are no longer carried mainly by roof bracing and frame action: they travel through floor diaphragms into a lateral system, column bases stop being simple pinned supports, and drift, floor vibration, fire protection and egress start to drive member sizes. The building also becomes a sequencing problem — you cannot erect floors before the frame below is plumb and stable.

This guide explains what changes, which structural and floor systems are used, and what a buyer must supply before a multi-storey frame can be engineered and priced.

## What actually governs the design once there is a second floor

In a single-storey shed the roof plane does most of the work: sheeting and bracing resist wind, the portal frame flexes, and some movement is acceptable because nothing above it must stay aligned. Add floors and the priorities invert.

- **Drift becomes the governing criterion.** Lateral movement at roof level of a shed may be visually irrelevant; between two floors of an office or a hospital it affects partitions, cladding joints, lift shafts and user comfort, so limits in the local code usually decide the lateral system before strength does.
- **Floors become structural diaphragms.** The slab does not merely carry people and equipment; it collects horizontal load from the frame above and passes it to the braced or moment-resisting bays. A floor system chosen only for its price per square metre can leave the building without a reliable diaphragm.
- **Columns become continuous members.** In a shed, each column is essentially a cantilever or a pinned member in a single plane. In a multi-storey frame the column carries accumulating load, and its effective length, splice positions and base fixity all matter.
- **Second-order effects and vibration enter the design.** Vertical load acting on a laterally displaced frame increases deflection, which is why taller frames need stiffer lateral systems rather than heavier beams; and floor vibration limits come from the use — a laboratory, ward or classroom will not accept a floor a warehouse would tolerate.
- **Fire and egress stop being a coating line item.** Compartmentation, protected steel, protected stairs and travel distances are decided with the local authority, and they change the tonnage of protection and sometimes the layout of the frame.

If the building is in a high-wind or high-seismic region, the lateral system decision is effectively made by the code and the site, not by preference. The mechanics behind that choice are set out in [wind load design](/en/blog/steel-structure-wind-load-design-guide/) and [seismic design](/en/blog/steel-structure-seismic-design-guide/).

## Lateral systems: what the four common options actually commit you to

The lateral system is the single decision that most changes connection design, erection sequence and price. It is not a detail to be left to the fabricator.

| System | Works well when | What it changes in the order | Watch-outs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moment-resisting frame | Architectural freedom is required on every facade; moderate height; bolted connections are preferred on site | Heavy connections, larger beams and columns, more welding or bolting, tighter erection tolerances | Drift control often forces bigger members than strength alone would; connection fabrication becomes the critical path |
| Concentrically braced frame (X, chevron or single diagonal) | Stiffness is needed at low cost; bracing can be placed in selected bays | Adds bracing members, gusset plates and a braced-bay erection sequence | Bracing blocks a bay for services or openings; brace connections attract large forces and need detailing attention |
| Eccentrically braced frame | Stiffness plus energy dissipation is required in seismic areas | Introduces defined link elements and stricter fabrication and inspection requirements | Fewer qualified fabricators; inspection and documentation load rises |
| Dual system (frame plus brace or core) | Height, code or redundancy requirements make a single system insufficient | Combines both, so both must be engineered and coordinated | More interfaces to manage; connection and bracing packages must be released together |

For most export projects the practical question is not which system is theoretically best but which one the local approving engineer will accept and which the site crew can erect — a conversation to have before the quotation.

## Floor systems: the other decision that sets the price

Steel frames are priced by weight, but in a multi-storey building the floor system often decides the tonnage and the schedule more than the frame itself.

| Floor system | Typical use | Strengths | Constraints |
|---|---|---|---|
| Profiled steel deck with concrete topping | Offices, hospitals, schools, residential, light industrial upper floors | Acts as a diaphragm, fast to lay, familiar to most site crews, good for services coordination | Wet trade on site, concrete supply and curing time, deck and studs must be detailed with the beams |
| Precast planks or hollow-core units on steel beams | Projects with local precast capacity, repetitive floors, cold regions | No site concrete for the slab, fast installation, factory-controlled quality | Heavier lifts, crane capacity and access, diaphragm action depends on how units are tied and grouted |
| Steel deck without concrete (temporary or light-duty) | Access platforms, plant decks, maintenance floors | Very light, quick, removable | Not a building floor diaphragm; vibration and fire performance must be checked separately |
| Concrete slab on permanent formwork between beams | Small floor plates where local concrete labour is cheap | Simple, locally sourced | Slower, heavier, diaphragm behaviour needs design confirmation |

Two practical points decide more projects than the table suggests: whether concrete is reliably available at site, and how the floor ties to the frame — the diaphragm only works if deck, studs, edge details and beam connections are designed as a system. Detail is in the [steel deck and composite floor guide](/en/blog/steel-deck-composite-floor-guide/).

## What changes when you move from one storey to several

| Item | Single-storey shed | Multi-storey frame |
|---|---|---|
| Lateral load path | Roof bracing and portal frame action | Floor diaphragms into braced or moment bays, then to foundations |
| Column base | Often nominally pinned, simple base plate | Fixity, anchor bolt group, base plate stiffness and grouting become design decisions |
| Floor | Not applicable | System choice, diaphragm, vibration, fire rating, wet trades |
| Fire protection | Usually a coating specification on the frame | Compartmentation, protected steel, protected stairs, and authority approval |
| Egress | Simple, short travel distances | Protected stairs, travel distance limits, sometimes pressurisation |
| Tolerances | Fit-up tolerances for cladding | Plumbness and floor level tolerances that accumulate storey by storey |
| Erection sequence | Erect frames, brace the first bay, sheet the roof | Braced bay first, then floors, then the next level; each level must be stable before loading it |
| Services | Roof-level coordination | Vertical shafts, risers, ceiling voids and floor penetrations coordinated with beams |

The column base is the item most often under-specified at quotation stage. Its design depends on the foundation the local party builds, the anchor bolt group, the base plate and how the base is grouted — see [column base and anchor bolt design](/en/blog/steel-column-base-anchor-bolt-design-guide/).

## The inputs a buyer must provide before a multi-storey frame can be priced

A fabricator can price a shed from a footprint and an eave height. A multi-storey frame cannot be priced responsibly from those two numbers alone.

1. **Use and occupancy per floor** — office, ward, classroom, retail, plant or storage; this sets live load and vibration criteria.
2. **Floor-to-floor and total height** — including basement or mezzanine, and the clear height each floor needs.
3. **Grid and bay dimensions** — and which bays must stay free of bracing for openings or services.
4. **Design code and approving engineer** — which code the authority checks and who signs the design.
5. **Site conditions** — wind, seismic category, snow, corrosion environment, and the foundation type built locally.
6. **Fire strategy** — required rating per element and the protection method the authority accepts.
7. **Facade and cladding system** — weight, fixing method, and whether it needs its own sub-frame.
8. **Egress and lift requirements** — stair cores, lift shafts, and whether lifts sit inside the steel scope.
9. **Services** — risers, ceiling voids, floor penetrations and rooftop plant.
10. **Site access and lifting plan** — crane available, maximum piece weight, and how members are received and stored.

Items 4, 5 and 6 most often stall a quotation: they belong to the local side and are often left "to be confirmed", so the frame ends up priced on assumptions.

## Fire, egress and the authority interface

Fire is where a multi-storey steel building diverges most from a shed, and where the responsibility boundary matters most.

Fire resistance is expressed as a period of time for a specific element — columns, beams, floors, stairs — and the required period comes from occupancy, height and compartmentation under the local code. How it is achieved is a separate decision: intumescent thickness depends on the member's massivity and the tested system, while boards and encasement are bulkier but more forgiving on site. None can be selected from a catalogue before the required period is fixed. The coating logic is covered in [fire protection for steel structures](/en/blog/steel-structure-fire-protection-guide/).

Egress follows the same pattern: protected stairs, travel distances and sometimes smoke control are checked by the local authority, and the stair core is often built locally even when the steel frame surrounds it. If the core is in the steel scope, say so in the enquiry — it changes tonnage, bracing and erection sequence.

## Erection: the braced bay comes first

Multi-storey erection has one rule that overrides most others: nothing is loaded until the level below is plumb, bolted and stable.

A typical sequence is: erect the columns and beams of the first braced bay, plumb and secure it, extend along the grid, place the floor of that level, then repeat. Temporary bracing or a designed temporary condition is needed until the permanent lateral system for that level is complete. Surveying is a step in the sequence, not a final check, because errors accumulate upward and a frame that is out at level one does not correct itself at level four.

Two consequences for the buyer: the erection plan must be agreed before shipping, because it determines marking and packing; and the site must lift the heaviest piece, often a composite beam or precast unit rather than a column.

## Where the cost actually moves

Multi-storey frames cost more per square metre of floor than single-storey sheds, and the reason is structural: drift control forces heavier lateral members, every floor level multiplies beams, plates and bolts, protection and deck are added, and tighter tolerances mean more survey time. None of this can be judged from a price per square metre, which is why a multi-storey enquiry should be priced from a drawing set and a floor schedule. The cost drivers themselves are covered in [what actually drives steel building cost](/en/blog/steel-warehouse-cost-guide/).

## Buyer checklist

- Use, live load and vibration criteria stated per floor
- Floor-to-floor heights, total height, and clear heights confirmed
- Column grid and bay spacing issued, with bracing-permitted bays marked
- Design code and approving engineer named
- Wind, seismic, snow and corrosion parameters from the site, not from memory
- Foundation type and anchor bolt interface agreed with the local engineer
- Fire rating per element fixed, and the acceptable protection method confirmed
- Stair and lift scope decided — inside or outside the steel package
- Facade weight and fixing method confirmed
- Crane capacity, maximum piece weight and site access agreed before shipping

## FAQ

**Can a single-storey portal frame design simply be extended upward?**
No. The portal frame relies on roof-plane bracing and frame action within one plane. Adding floors introduces diaphragms, continuous columns, drift limits and second-order effects, so the lateral system and column bases have to be designed for the taller configuration rather than scaled up.

**Is a braced frame always cheaper than a moment frame?**
Usually lighter and stiffer, but not always cheaper once consequences are counted: bracing occupies a bay that may be needed for openings or services, and brace connections attract concentrated forces that need detailing and inspection.

**Which floor system should an export buyer specify?**
It depends on local concrete reliability, lifting capacity and the diaphragm and fire requirements. Composite deck with topping is the most widely understood and gives a reliable diaphragm; precast units suit projects with dependable local precast and heavy lifting. Choose the floor with the frame, not after the steel is ordered.

**Does the steel supplier design the building?**
In most export projects the structural design and its approval sit with a registered engineer in the destination country. The fabricator coordinates engineering, produces shop drawings and fabricates to the approved design. A supplier that offers to "design everything" without a local engineer is a warning sign, not a convenience.

**How does height affect steel weight per square metre?**
It rises with height, mainly because drift control requires stiffer lateral members and heavier connections, and because floors add beams, deck, studs and protection. The rate of increase depends on the lateral system, the code's drift limit, the floor system and the site's wind or seismic load, so it cannot be given as a fixed percentage.

**What most often delays a multi-storey quotation?**
Missing local design inputs — approving engineer, fire strategy, foundation interface, wind and seismic parameters. Without them the fabricator can only price assumptions.

**Standalone answers for citation**

A multi-storey steel frame differs from a portal frame in four ways: lateral load travels through floor diaphragms into a designed lateral system, columns are continuous with designed base fixity, floors must provide diaphragm action, fire performance and acceptable vibration, and erection follows a level-by-level stability sequence.

Floor systems are normally either a profiled steel deck with concrete topping, which gives a reliable diaphragm and is widely understood on site, or precast planks on steel beams, which avoid site concrete but need lifting capacity and careful tying to the frame.

Before a multi-storey steel frame can be priced, the buyer must provide per-floor use and loads, floor heights, the column grid and bracing-permitted bays, the design code and approving engineer, site wind/seismic/corrosion parameters, the fire strategy, stair and lift scope, facade weight and site lifting capacity.
